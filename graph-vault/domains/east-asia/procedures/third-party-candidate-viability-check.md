# Third-Party Candidate Viability Check

## When to Use

Apply this procedure when a forecast question asks whether a third-party or independent candidate can win a national election (presidential or parliamentary) in a first-past-the-post (single-round plurality) system, OR when the question names a candidate not affiliated with one of the country's two dominant parties.

## Pre-Forecast Audit

### Step 1: Confirm the Electoral System
Is this a single-round plurality (FPTP) election or a majority-runoff/two-round system?

- **FPTP**: The third-party ceiling applies structurally. Proceed to Step 2.
- **Two-round runoff**: The third-party candidate CAN reach the second round and has a non-trivial win probability there. This procedure does NOT apply — use standard election forecasting instead.

### Step 2: Classify the Candidate

What type of "third-party" candidate is this?

| Type | Definition | P(Win) |
|------|-----------|--------|
| **Genuine third-party** | Candidate of a minor/alternative party with <15% historical national vote share, no major-party heritage | <1% |
| **Independent (no party)** | Candidate running without any party label, no organizational base | <1% |
| **Splinter candidate** | Former major-party member running as independent/third-party after losing primary or leaving party | 1-5% (depends on how much of the original party they take) |
| **Spoiler-entrenched third party** | Third party that has existed for 2+ cycles and consistently polls 15-30% but has never won | <1% structurally, but exercises kingmaker power |
| **De facto major candidate** | Candidate of a formerly dominant party that has reorganized under a new label (e.g., MAS in Bolivia, Podemos in Spain's transformed system) | Apply standard forecasting — not a true third-party candidate |

For Types 1-4, the third-party ceiling concept applies. For Type 5, this is not a third-party situation.

### Step 3: Check the Historical Ceiling

Has any candidate of this type won a national FPTP election in this country's history?

- If NO: The ceiling is structural, not contingent. P(win) < 1%.
- If YES (rare): Assess the conditions that made it possible — did a major party collapse? Was it a de facto two-party race?

For Taiwan specifically: No third-party candidate has ever won the presidency. James Soong (ind., 36.84% in 2000) and Ko Wen-je (TPP, 26.46% in 2024) both lost. The ceiling is structural.

### Step 4: Assess the Candidate's Organizational Depth

Key indicators of a potential ceiling breach:

| Indicator | Weak (ceiling holds) | Strong (reassess) |
|-----------|---------------------|-------------------|
| Party legislative seats | <10% of seats | >20% of seats |
| Local/regional offices | None or token | Controls significant local governments |
| Donor base | Individual donations only | Sustained donor pipeline comparable to major parties |
| Candidate pipeline | One presidential candidate only | Multiple candidates across levels of government |
| Party age | <10 years | >15 years with consistent performance |

If ALL indicators are in the "strong" column, the candidate might be evolving into a de facto major party. Otherwise, the ceiling holds.

### Step 5: Identify the Spoiler Direction

Even though the third-party candidate cannot win, they determine which major-party candidate does. Identify:

1. Which major-party bloc does the third-party candidate draw more from? (Poll cross-tabs showing "second choice" preferences)
2. If the third-party candidate were removed, which major candidate would gain more votes?
3. What is the pre-third-party baseline? Would the opposition have won without the spoiler?

**Example (Taiwan 2024)**: Ko Wen-je drew disproportionately from the KMT base (younger anti-DPP but not pro-China voters). Combined KMT+TPP vote (59.95%) exceeded Lai's DPP total (40.05%). Without Ko, Hou (KMT) would likely have won in a two-way race.

### Step 6: Assess the Prisoner's Dilemma

Does the third-party candidate have an incentive to stay in the race even though they cannot win?

- **Brand building**: Staying in preserves the third party's identity for future cycles (Ko's choice).
- **Spoiler leverage**: The threat of staying in may extract concessions from one major party.
- **Personal ambition**: The candidate may value the campaign platform over winning.

Document which incentive dominates. This determines whether the candidate is likely to withdraw (rare) or stay in (likely).

### Step 7: Calibrate the Forecast

| Scenario | P(Third-Party Win) |
|----------|-------------------|
| Genuine third-party, 3+ major candidates, FPTP | <1% |
| Third-party polling 20-30%, all others lower | <1% (ceiling effect) |
| Third-party with major-party collapse in their favor | 5-20% (reassess — may be de facto major candidate) |
| Third-party with 2+ strong indicators from Step 4 | 10-20% (monitored for evolution into major party) |
| Third-party in runoff system (not FPTP) | 5-30% depending on second-round match-up |

### Step 8: Document the Structural Rationale

Example:

> Taiwan uses single-round plurality (FPTP) for presidential elections. Ko Wen-je is a third-party candidate (TPP) with no major-party heritage, no legislative majority, and a party founded only in 2019. No third-party candidate has ever won the Taiwan presidency. The historical ceiling in FPTP systems is 30-37% for third-party candidates, and even at the top of this range they cannot win. Ko's 26.46% in 2024 confirmed this ceiling. His role is as a spoiler — determining which major candidate wins — not as a contender. P(Ko wins) < 1%.

## Post-Forecast Reflection

After outcome known, verify:
- Did the ceiling hold? Did the third-party candidate's vote share fall within the historical range?
- Was the spoiler direction correctly identified?
- Did any unexpected withdrawal or endorsement occur?
