# Third-Party Candidate Ceiling in FPTP Presidential Systems

## Definition

A structural pattern in which third-party or independent candidates in first-past-the-post (single-round plurality) presidential elections cannot win the presidency, regardless of their personal popularity, because the electoral system imposes a ceiling on third-party vote share. The candidate serves as a **spoiler** — determining *which* major-party candidate wins — rather than a **contender** capable of winning themselves.

Key characteristics:
- Third-party candidate receives 10-37% of the vote (the high end of the ceiling range)
- Combined major-party vote still exceeds the third-party vote
- Third-party candidate lacks a national party organization, ground infrastructure, or donor network comparable to major parties
- Voters who prefer the third-party candidate over both major options face the "wasted vote" dilemma
- The third-party candidate draws votes disproportionately from one major-party bloc (asymmetric defection), determining which major candidate wins
- The third-party candidate cannot win under any plausible vote distribution unless a major party collapses entirely

## Structural Drivers

### 1. Organizational deficit
Major parties in FPTP systems maintain permanent national infrastructure: precinct-level organizers, donor lists, media relationships, candidate pipelines, and policy apparatus. Third-party campaigns must build this from scratch within a single election cycle. Ko Wen-je's TPP, founded in 2019, was organizationally thin compared to the DPP (founded 1986) and KMT (founded 1919) — it lacked legislative candidates in most districts and had no previous electoral track record.

### 2. Donor scarcity
Major-party donors fund candidates who can win. Third-party candidates are perceived as non-viable, creating a self-fulfilling funding gap. Even self-funded billionaires (Ross Perot, Terry Gou) hit a ceiling because money cannot buy the party infrastructure or voter loyalty that major parties have built over decades.

### 3. Strategic voting against third-party candidates
Voters who prefer the third-party candidate learn through polling that their candidate cannot win. They face a choice: "waste" their vote on their preferred candidate or vote for the least-bad viable option. This creates a **late-campaign collapse effect** — third-party candidates poll higher months before the election than on election day.

The collapse follows a predictable trajectory:
- **6+ months out**: Third-party candidate peaks as a "protest" or "new voice" option. Polling is inflated by name recognition, media curiosity, and voters expressing general dissatisfaction.
- **2-4 months out**: As the election approaches and media focus shifts to the top-two competitors, the third-party candidate's support begins to erode. Strategic voters start considering alternatives.
- **0-4 weeks out**: Accelerated collapse. Polling converges toward the structural ceiling as late-deciding voters strategically consolidate behind viable candidates.
- **Election day**: The third-party candidate's vote share is at the BOTTOM of their polling range.

**Canonical data points** (all showed this trajectory):
- Ko Wen-je (Taiwan 2024): Peaked at ~30% in Oct 2023, settled at 26.46% on election day — a ~4-point collapse.
- James Soong (Taiwan 2000): Polled at 42% in some February 2000 polls, settled at 36.84% — a ~5-point collapse.
- Ross Perot (US 1992): Led both Bush and Clinton at 39% in June 1992, ended at 18.9% — a massive collapse driven by his July withdrawal and re-entry.

**Forecasting implication**: When a third-party candidate polls at 25-30% three months before election day, assume a 3-8 point collapse. The ceiling at election day is lower than the peak polling suggests. Do not extrapolate polling trajectory linearly — collapse is non-linear and concentrated in the final 4 weeks.

### 4. Media coverage disparity
Major-party candidates receive disproportionate media coverage due to their party's institutional weight, polling viability, and established campaign apparatus. Third-party candidates struggle to be covered as contenders rather than curiosities.

### 5. Ballot access barriers
Many FPTP systems impose signature requirements, filing fees, or other barriers that third-party candidates must overcome just to appear on the ballot.

## The Ceiling Range

Historical data from presidential FPTP systems shows a consistent ceiling:

| Candidate | Election | Vote Share | System | Outcome |
|-----------|----------|-----------|--------|---------|
| James Soong (ind.) | Taiwan 2000 | 36.84% | Single-round plurality | Spoiler (split KMT, DPP won) |
| Ko Wen-je (TPP) | Taiwan 2024 | 26.46% | Single-round plurality | Spoiler (split anti-DPP, DPP won) |
| Ross Perot (Reform) | US 1992 | 18.91% | Electoral College (FPTP states) | Spoiler (arguably helped Clinton) |
| George Wallace (AIP) | US 1968 | 13.53% | Electoral College (FPTP states) | Spoiler (drew from both but mainly Dems) |
| Ross Perot (Reform) | US 1996 | 8.40% | Electoral College (FPTP states) | Minor spoiler |
| John Anderson (ind.) | US 1980 | 6.61% | Electoral College (FPTP states) | Minor spoiler |
| Robert F. Kennedy Jr. (ind.) | US 2024 | 5+% (dropout Nov) | Electoral College (FPTP states) | Minor spoiler before withdrawal |

**Interpretation**: The ceiling for a genuine third-party/independent candidate (not a former major-party nominee running as an independent) in a presidential FPTP system is approximately **30-37%** — and even at the top of this range, the candidate cannot win. The only scenario in which a third-party candidate breaches 40% is when they are effectively a major-party candidate running outside the party label (e.g., a former president or party nominee), which is a distinct phenomenon.

## When the Ceiling CAN Be Breached (Rare Conditions)

A third-party candidate can win only under specific conditions that are rarely all met:

1. **Major-party collapse**: One of the two dominant parties disintegrates or fails to field a viable candidate, reducing the race to de facto 2 candidates (one major, one third-party). Example: Rafael Correa in Ecuador 2006 (a leftist outsider who won after the traditional party system collapsed, though Ecuador uses a two-round system so this is a different case).

2. **De facto two-party race**: What appears as a third-party run is actually the candidate of a formerly dominant party that has reorganized. Example: Evo Morales in Bolivia 2005 (MAS started as a movement party but became the dominant left force — not a "third-party" in the structural sense).

3. **Electoral system change to runoff**: If the system changes to two-round majority, the third-party candidate can reach the second round and potentially win there. But in pure FPTP, the ceiling is structural, not contingent.

## Forecasting Application

When a forecast question asks whether a third-party or independent candidate will win a national FPTP presidential election:

1. **Check candidate status**: Is this candidate running as a third-party/independent (not a major-party nominee)? If yes, apply the ceiling.
2. **Assess polling trajectory**: Is the candidate at 15-30% in polls? Expected — this is the pre-election ceiling range.
3. **Check organizational depth**: Does the candidate have a party organization covering most districts? If not (e.g., a new party or independent), the organizational deficit applies.
4. **Identify the major-party alignment**: Which major-party bloc does the third-party candidate draw from? This determines SPOILER direction.
5. **Calibrate forecast**:
   - Third-party/independent candidate: P(win) < 1% in a genuine 3-way FPTP race
   - Third-party candidate polling 20-30%: P(win) ~ 0% structurally — treat as spoiler, not contender
   - Third-party candidate benefiting from major-party collapse: reassess — may be a de facto major candidate
6. **Document the structural rationale**: "Candidate X is a third-party candidate in an FPTP system. No third-party candidate has won a presidential FPTP election at the national level. The historical ceiling is 30-37% vote share. Even if Candidate X polls at 25%, they cannot win — the question is which major candidate they hurt."
