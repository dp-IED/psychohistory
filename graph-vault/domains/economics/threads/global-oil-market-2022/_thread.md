# Global Crude Oil Market 2022

## Overview

The 2022 global crude oil market was defined by the most severe geopolitical supply shock in decades — Russia's invasion of Ukraine — layered on top of a already-tight market recovering from the COVID-era demand collapse. The year saw Brent crude peak at ~$128 (closing), European natural gas hit all-time highs, and a wave of unprecedented government intervention (strategic petroleum reserve releases, G7 price caps, EU embargoes). The year divides into three phases: pre-war tightness (Jan-Feb), invasion shock and policy response (Mar-May), and the recession-driven demand destruction equilibrium (Jun-Dec).

## Key Dynamics

1. **Pre-war baseline (Jan-Feb 2022)**: Brent in $80-95 range, already elevated from pre-COVID $60 levels due to OPEC+ production discipline and recovering global demand. European gas storage was below the 5-year average. The war risk was present but not fully priced — markets assigned low probability to a full-scale invasion.

2. **Invasion shock (Feb 24 – Mar 8, 2022)**: Brent crossed $100 on invasion day and peaked at ~$128 close (intraday ~$139) on March 8. The spike was amplified by: (a) Biden's Russian oil import ban announcement, (b) fear of European sanctions on Russian energy, (c) precautionary buying, (d) low global inventories — the post-COVID demand recovery had drawn inventories down significantly.

3. **SPR intervention (Mar-Apr 2022)**: The IEA coordinated a 60M barrel release on March 1, followed by the US 180M barrel SPR announcement on March 31 (largest in history), and an additional IEA 120M barrel release in April. Total ~365M barrels committed — unprecedented in scale. These releases suppressed prices by $5-15/barrel in the near term.

4. **Calibration and partial reversion (Mar-Apr 2022)**: By March 15, Brent had fallen back to ~$99 as the market absorbed three key facts: (a) Russian oil continued flowing (India and China absorbed discounted barrels), (b) Europe did not immediately ban Russian oil, (c) SPR releases were coming. Brent stabilized in $100-110 range through April.

5. **EU embargo and price cap (May-Dec 2022)**: The EU agreed on a phased Russian oil embargo (May 31): seaborne crude banned from December 5, 2022, with product bans from February 5, 2023. The G7 price cap mechanism (agreed September, effective December 5) was designed to maintain Russian production while capping Russia's revenue. This was the most complex sanctions regime ever imposed on an energy superpower — its effects are tracked in [[concepts/energy-weaponization-economic-warfare]].

6. **Recession-driven rebalancing (Jun-Dec 2022)**: By June 2022, recession fears began dominating oil market narrative. Fed tightening, European energy crisis, China's zero-COVID lockdowns (Shanghai April-May, sporadic lockdowns throughout), and demand destruction from high prices drove Brent from ~$120 in June to ~$85 by September. OPEC+ cut production by 2M bpd on October 5 to support prices. By year-end, Brent traded at ~$82.

## Quarterly Summary

### 2022-Q1: Invasion shock
Brent opens at ~$80, peaks at ~$128 (Mar 8), closes quarter at ~$108. See [[2022-Q1]].

### 2022-Q2: SPR intervention and embargo negotiation
Brent oscillates in $100-120 range. EU embargo negotiation dominates Q2 narrative. US SPR releases 1M bpd. Price cap concept is floated by US Treasury. Shanghai lockdown (April) starts demand-side worry.

### 2022-Q3: Recession fear and price decline
Brent declines from ~$120 (June) to ~$85 (September) as recession fears, European gas crisis demand destruction, and aggressive Fed tightening dominate. OPEC+ signals willingness to cut production.

### 2022-Q4: OPEC+ cuts and price floor
OPEC+ cuts 2M bpd (October 5), Brent stabilizes at ~$80-90. EU oil embargo takes effect December 5. G7 price cap at $60/barrel implemented same day. Russian crude trades at $10-20 discount to Brent (the "Urals discount").

## Forecasting Significance

The 2022 oil market is the canonical modern example of:
1. How geopolitical shocks transmit to commodity prices
2. The spike-reversion pattern
3. Government intervention as a price suppression mechanism
4. The interaction between monetary policy (Fed rate hikes), demand (recession), and supply (OPEC+)

Any future forecast about oil prices during a geopolitical crisis should use 2022 as the primary reference case. See [[geopolitical-commodity-spike-reversion]] for the analytical framework.
