# Geopolitical Commodity Spike and Reversion

## Definition

A recurring pattern where a sudden geopolitical shock (invasion, sanctions, blockade threat, terrorist attack on infrastructure) causes a sharp, fear-driven price spike in a globally traded commodity, followed by a partial reversion within 2-8 weeks as the market calibrates actual physical supply disruption against the worst-case scenario priced during the spike. The reversion is rarely complete — prices typically settle 10-30% above the pre-shock baseline, reflecting a permanently elevated geopolitical risk premium and any residual disruption to supply chains.

This pattern is distinct from a structural bull market (driven by sustained demand growth, chronic under-investment, or secular supply decline) or a speculative bubble (driven by leverage, momentum trading, and herding). The distinguishing feature is a clear **catalytic trigger event** followed by a **calibration phase** in which the market re-prices risk as new information arrives.

## Canonical Example: Russian Invasion of Ukraine and Brent Crude (February-March 2022)

The canonical case for this concept is the Brent crude oil spike of February-March 2022:

**Phase 1 — Pre-shock baseline (January 2022):**
Brent crude traded in the $80-90/barrel range throughout January 2022. This was already elevated from pre-COVID levels (~$60) due to recovering demand, OPEC+ production discipline, and a pre-existing European gas crisis. The invasion risk was being monitored but not priced in at full scale; the US intelligence declassification campaign (January-February) gradually raised awareness but markets expected diplomacy or limited conflict.

**Phase 2 — Shock and fear spike (February 24 – March 8, 2022):**
- February 24 (invasion day): Brent crosses $100/barrel for the first time since 2014 — a ~15% one-day move.
- March 7-8: Biden announces US ban on Russian oil imports. Brent peaks at ~$128 close (intraday ~$139). European TTF gas hits EUR 345/MWh.
- Total spike from baseline: ~50-80% above pre-invasion levels.
- **Drivers of the spike:** (a) Fear of global supply disruption — Russia produces ~10% of global oil and exports ~5 million bpd. (b) Precautionary buying and hedgers covering shorts. (c) Sanctions anxiety — would Europe also ban Russian oil? Would Russian oil buyers voluntarily shun Russian crude? (d) Financial amplification via commodity index rebalancing, margin calls, and algorithmic trading.

**Phase 3 — Reality calibration (March 8 – March 15, 2022):**
- March 15 (question date): Brent closes at ~$99 — a ~23% decline from the peak in 7 days.
- **Calibration factors that drove reversion:**
  1. **Physical supply not disrupted:** Russian oil continued flowing through March. The US ban applied only to US imports (~90k bpd, ~1% of Russian exports). Europe did not impose an oil embargo in March (it came in stages from May through December).
  2. **Alternative buyers emerged:** Indian and Chinese refiners increased purchases of discounted Russian crude. Global oil is fungible — Russian oil displaced rather than lost.
  3. **SPR release telegraphed:** IEA coordinated 60M barrel release (March 1) and US 180M barrel SPR announcement (March 31, but leak/telegraph in mid-March). Markets price anticipated policy responses.
  4. **Demand destruction signal:** $130 oil was self-limiting — it would destroy demand through recession and switching. The market priced in this auto-stabilizer.
  5. **Ceasefire talks:** Russia-Ukraine talks in Istanbul (March 29) followed the March 15 date but signaled possible de-escalation, reducing the worst-case premium.

**Phase 4 — New equilibrium (late March – April 2022):**
- Brent stabilized in the $100-110 range — approximately 20-30% above the pre-invasion baseline.
- The permanent risk premium reflected: (a) the ongoing war and risk of escalation, (b) European dependence on Russian gas and the REPowerEU transition timeline, (c) OPEC+ discipline maintaining supply constraints, and (d) depleted global inventories from the pre-war drawdown.
- Full reversion to pre-invasion levels ($80-90) did not occur until Q3-Q4 2022, when recession fears and Chinese COVID lockdowns dented demand.

## Pattern Archetype

The pattern follows a predictable sequence:

### Phase 1: Trigger Event (Day 0)
A sudden, unexpected geopolitical event that threatens supply from a major producer or transit route. Key trigger types:
- **Invasion / interstate conflict** (Russia-Ukraine 2022, Iran-Israel 2024)
- **Sanctions / embargo** (US/Russia 2022, US/Venezuela, EU/Russia)
- **Blockade / chokepoint threat** (Houthis in Red Sea 2024, Hormuz threat)
- **Infrastructure attack** (Nord Stream sabotage, pipeline attacks, Abqaiq-Khurais 2019)
- **Producer disruption** (Libya civil wars, Venezuela collapse, Kazakhstan unrest)

### Phase 2: Fear Spike (Days 1-14)
The shock propagates through multiple channels:
- **Physical:** Actual or threatened supply loss from the producer
- **Precautionary:** Importers and refiners increase inventories as insurance
- **Financial:** Short covering, commodity index rebalancing, speculative buying, margin cascade
- **Policy:** Unclear response creates uncertainty premium
- **Amplification:** The spike is magnified when (a) the affected producer accounts for >5% of global supply, (b) spare capacity is limited, (c) inventories are low, (d) the shock coincides with other market tightness (e.g., post-COVID demand surge + invasion)

Peak spike magnitude relative to baseline is typically:
- **Moderate shock** (3-5% supply affected): 15-25% spike
- **Severe shock** (5-10% supply affected): 30-60% spike  
- **Extreme shock** (>10% supply affected): 60-100%+ spike (rare, e.g., 1973, 1990 Gulf War)

### Phase 3: Reality Calibration (Days 3-30)
The most critical phase for forecasting. Key calibration questions:
1. **Is actual physical supply disrupted?** Pipeline flows, tanker loadings, and port operations in real time. Markets often overestimate physical impact during the fear spike.
2. **Can alternative supply fill the gap?** Spare OPEC+ capacity, US shale response time (6-12 months), SPR releases, Iranian/Venezuelan crude if sanctions are relaxed.
3. **Are alternative buyers absorbing displaced supply?** If Russian oil flows to India/China instead of Europe, the barrel is still consumed — the disruption is in routing, not volume.
4. **Is there a policy response?** SPR releases, IEA coordinated draws, price caps, demand-side measures. Markets price telegraphed responses before they're executed.
5. **Is demand destruction already active?** Very high prices reduce consumption — this auto-stabilizer begins operating within weeks.
6. **Is there a de-escalation signal?** Ceasefire talks, diplomatic progress, or signs the triggering event is contained rather than escalating.

Reversion magnitude is typically 50-80% of the spike — meaning if the spike was +50%, the market settles ~10-25% above the pre-shock baseline.

### Phase 4: New Equilibrium (Weeks to Months)
The market establishes a new baseline incorporating:
- The ongoing risk premium from the unresolved geopolitical situation
- The structural impact of sanctions (if permanent) on supply routing
- The long-term demand response to sustained high prices
- The adaptive capacity of producers and consumers
- The duration of any physical supply disruption that did materialize

The new equilibrium is always higher than the pre-shock baseline unless the event is fully resolved (e.g., a failed coup attempt with no lasting disruption). A fully resolved event sees prices revert to baseline within 2-3 months.

## Forecasting Application

### For questions of the form "Will [commodity] reach [price level] by [date]?"

1. **MUST check for the spike-reversion pattern**: If the question's timeframe overlaps with an ongoing geopolitical shock, assess whether the market is in Phase 2 (fear spike), Phase 3 (reality calibration), or Phase 4 (new equilibrium). The answer to "will price hit X" depends critically on which phase the question's target date falls in.

2. **Key distinction: Peak vs settled price**: Questions about "price at close on [date]" test the settled price after calibration, not the intraday peak. The peak is almost always during Phase 2. By Phase 3, prices have typically reverted significantly.

3. **Calibration rate estimate**: For a severe shock, the reversion from peak is typically:
   - 1 week post-peak: 15-30% of spike withdrawn
   - 2 weeks post-peak: 30-50% of spike withdrawn
   - 4 weeks post-peak: 50-70% of spike withdrawn
   - These rates vary with the persistence of the physical disruption

4. **Brent vs WTI distinction**: For "crude oil" questions without specification, default to Brent (global benchmark). But check whether the question has a WTI-specific framing (e.g., explicit "West Texas Intermediate" or settlement venue mention).

5. **Policy response anticipation**: Markets price SPR releases and IEA coordinated actions BEFORE they are formally announced. The telegraphing of SPR releases (Biden's late March 2022 announcement was telegraphed for 2+ weeks) means the suppression effect begins during the anticipation phase, not the execution phase.

6. **False pattern recognition warning**: Not all geopolitical shocks follow the spike-reversion pattern uniformly. Key exceptions:
   - **Persistent supply loss**: If actual physical production is destroyed (Libyan civil war, Venezuela's secular decline), reversion is minimal.
   - **Permanent sanctions regime**: If sanctions permanently exclude a major producer from global markets (Iranian oil 2018-2021), the new equilibrium is structurally elevated.
   - **Chokepoint blockade**: If a critical transit chokepoint is blocked (Strait of Hormuz), the impact is sustained until the blockade is resolved.
   - **Broad-based commodity shock vs single-commodity**: When the shock affects multiple commodities simultaneously (war + energy + food in 2022), the macro feedback loop can sustain prices longer.

### For format: "Did/Will [commodity] price reach [level] on [date]?"

Check:
1. Was there a geopolitical shock in the 4-8 weeks before the target date?
2. If yes: what was the peak price from the fear spike?
3. How many days before the target date was the peak? (Calibration rate matters)
4. Was there a policy response (SPR, sanctions, price caps) that was telegraphed or executed?
5. Was actual physical supply disrupted, or was it routing displacement?

### Default probability framework:

When the target price is 15-30% above baseline and a geopolitical shock occurred 2-4 weeks before the target date, the default forecast should be "NO" — the market is still in or just exiting the reality calibration phase and is unlikely to have re-spiked above the fear peak. This is a high-conviction default pattern.
