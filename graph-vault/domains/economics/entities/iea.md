# International Energy Agency (IEA)

## Summary

The International Energy Agency (IEA) is an autonomous intergovernmental organization founded in 1974 in the wake of the 1973-74 oil crisis, operating within the OECD framework. Its founding mission is to ensure reliable, affordable, and clean energy for its 31 member countries (plus accession and association countries). Its most important operational function for forecasting is the **coordinated emergency oil stock release** — member countries hold strategic petroleum reserves equivalent to at least 90 days of net oil imports and can coordinate releases to stabilize global oil markets during supply disruptions. The IEA has triggered coordinated releases only a handful of times in its history (1991 Gulf War, 2005 Hurricane Katrina, 2011 Libya civil war, and 2022 Russia-Ukraine war).

## Significance for Forecasting

- **Coordinated SPR release trigger**: The IEA's emergency release mechanism is triggered by actual or imminent supply disruptions. Its activation signals that member governments believe the disruption is severe enough to warrant intervention — a leading indicator of sustained government attention on oil prices.
- **Magnitude signal**: The size of the coordinated release communicates the perceived severity of the disruption. The March 2022 release of 60 million barrels (including the US 180M barrel release) was the largest in IEA history — signaling extreme concern.
- **Price suppression effectiveness**: IEA coordinated releases typically suppress prices by $5-15/barrel in the near term (1-4 weeks). The effectiveness depends on whether the release addresses the root cause of the disruption (physical supply vs. financial speculation vs. fear premium).
- **Frequency changes over time**: The IEA has activated coordinated releases more frequently in crisis periods. A second coordinated release (April 2022, additional 120M barrels, mostly from US SPR) and discussions of further releases indicated sustained tightness.
- **Institutional relationships**: The IEA works alongside OPEC+ (supply-side management) and the US SPR (largest single-country reserve) as the three institutional pillars of global oil market intervention.
