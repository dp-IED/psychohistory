# Brent Crude Oil

## Summary

Brent Crude is the global benchmark crude oil grade, representing light, sweet crude oil extracted from the North Sea (Brent, Forties, Oseberg, and Ekofisk fields). It is the primary pricing reference for approximately two-thirds of the world's internationally traded crude oil, including production from the North Sea, Africa, the Middle East, Russia, and Asia. Unlike WTI (which reflects US market conditions), Brent is the benchmark for global maritime crude flows and is priced at the Sullom Voe terminal in the Shetland Islands. Brent futures trade on ICE Futures Europe.

## Significance for Forecasting

- **Global benchmark, not US-specific**: Questions about "a barrel of crude oil" without specification typically reference Brent (global) rather than WTI (US). The two closely track but diverge on US infrastructure factors (pipeline constraints, export capacity).
- **Geopolitical risk transmission mechanism**: Brent prices respond instantly to Middle East conflict, Russia-Ukraine/Russia-energy disruptions, OPEC+ production decisions, and Strait of Hormuz blockade threats. The premium between Brent and WTI widens during global supply scares.
- **Key price levels**: $100 is a psychological and political threshold. When Brent crosses $100, it triggers: (a) demand destruction signals from consuming countries, (b) IEA coordinated emergency reserve releases, (c) political pressure on OPEC+ for supply increases, and (d) macro-economic recession warnings.
- **Spike reversion pattern**: Brent price spikes from geopolitical shocks typically partially revert within 2-4 weeks as markets calibrate actual supply disruption vs worst-case scenarios. This pattern is formalized in [[domains/economics/concepts/geopolitical-commodity-spike-reversion/_concept]].
- **Brent-WTI spread**: A widening Brent-WTI premium (>$5/barrel) signals global supply tightness relative to US, often driven by sanctions on Russian/Iranian crude or OPEC+ cuts.
