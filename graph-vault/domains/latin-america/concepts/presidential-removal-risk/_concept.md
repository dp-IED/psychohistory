# Presidential Removal Risk Assessment

## Definition

A structured framework for assessing the probability that an incumbent president ceases to hold office before their constitutionally scheduled term end. Applies to any question of the form "Will [Leader] still be in power on [Date]?" or "Will [Leader] leave office before [Date]?"

## The Four Removal Pathways

A president can leave office early through four mechanisms, each with distinct triggers and thresholds:

### 1. Impeachment / Congressional Removal

- **Mechanism**: Formal legislative process resulting in conviction and removal
- **Common trigger**: Corruption scandal, abuse of power, criminal indictment
- **Threshold**: Varies by constitution (typically supermajority). In Argentina: 2/3 of both Chamber of Deputies (initiation) and Senate (conviction). In US: majority of House for articles, 2/3 of Senate for conviction.
- **Success factors**: Opposition legislative supermajority OR majority party defections; credible bipartisan case; sustained media pressure
- **Failure modes**: President's party holds floor; opposition lacks supermajority; defections insufficient (partisan loyalty holds)
- **Historical frequency (Latin America)**: Several attempts, few successes. Ecuador (Abdala Bucaram 1997, "mental incapacity"), Peru (Fujimori 2000, resigned before vote), Brazil (Dilma Rousseff 2016, successful), Paraguay (Lugo 2012, successful), Argentina (none successful since 1983).

### 2. Resignation

- **Mechanism**: President voluntarily steps down
- **Common triggers**: Overwhelming protest, loss of military/police support, deal to avoid prosecution, health crisis
- **Success factors**: Sustained mass protests with elite defections; credible threat of impeachment/conviction; personal scandal that delegitimizes continued tenure
- **Failure modes**: President rides out scandal; protests dissipate; elite allies hold
- **Historical pattern**: Usually occurs when president faces near-certain impeachment OR regime collapse is imminent (e.g., Nixon 1974, Fujimori 2000 via fax from Japan)

### 3. Death / Permanent Incapacity

- **Mechanism**: Natural death, assassination, or formal declaration of incapacity
- **Common triggers**: Age/health condition, violent political environment, assassination attempt
- **Success factors**: Advanced age of leader; known serious health condition; high-violence political environment; history of political assassinations in country
- **Failure modes**: Young/healthy leader; low-violence environment; strong security apparatus
- **Note**: Very low base rate for modern presidents in stable democracies. Hard to predict; usually not the primary pathway unless specific health information is available.

### 4. Coup / Unconstitutional Removal

- **Mechanism**: Military force, civilian uprising, or irregular legal process (e.g., legislative declaration of vacancy)
- **Common triggers**: Deep institutional crisis, military disloyalty, mass protests with security-force defections
- **Preconditions**: Military willing to act against civilian authority; breakdown of constitutional order; weak democratic institutions
- **Failure modes**: Strong democratic institutions/civilian control of military; international pressure; military loyalty to constitutional order
- **Historical frequency**: Declining in Latin America. Zero successful coups in Argentina since 1976; last in region: Honduras 2009, Bolivia 2019 (disputed).

## Key Forecasting Variables

For each removal pathway, assess:

| Variable | Weight | Argentina calibration (Milei 2025) |
|----------|--------|-----------------------------------|
| Legislative supermajority threshold | High | 2/3 of both chambers. Milei's LLA held 10/257 seats (2023-2025), then 64/257 (post-Oct 2025). Combined with allied blocs, no opposition supermajority possible in either chamber. |
| Economic trajectory (inflation, growth, wages) | High | Strong improvement: inflation 254%→1.5% monthly, GDP recovering, poverty 41.7%→31.6%, wages rising. Voters attribute improvement to Milei. |
| Approval rating level | Medium | Volatile but above collapse threshold. Hit 32% low in Sep 2025 but recovered to 40%+ range by Oct election. Above the ~20% floor that triggers resignation historically. |
| Protest intensity | Medium | Significant anti-government protests in 2024 (university funding, subsidies) but below regime-threatening levels. Protests diminished as economic conditions improved in 2025. |
| Military loyalty | Medium | Argentina's military has been thoroughly civilianized since 1983 democratic transition. No coup threat since the 1990 Carapintadas. Zero coup probability for any modern Argentine president. |
| International support (IMF, US) | Medium-High | Strong: IMF $20B loan, US Treasury support, Milei aligned with Trump administration. International community would oppose any irregular removal. |
| Unity of opposition | Medium | Peronist opposition fragmented (Fuerza Patria, Primero País, FIT-U). No single opposition leader or party had national majority. |
| Personal scandal severity | Variable | $LIBRA rug pull (Feb 2025) and Karina ANDIS kickback scandal (Aug 2025) were damaging but not individually sufficient for removal. Impeachment calls lacked legislative support. |

## Reference Class: First-Term Radical Reformers

For "will a populist/radical reformer survive their first term" questions:

| Leader | Country | Term | Survived? | Key factor |
|--------|---------|------|-----------|------------|
| Milei | Argentina | 2023-2027 (scheduled) | Yes (so far) | Inflation reduction, improved economy, fragmented opposition |
| Bolsonaro | Brazil | 2019-2022 | Yes | Strong agribusiness base, fragmented opposition, COVID gave emergency powers |
| Trump | US | 2017-2021 | Yes | Survived two impeachment attempts, strong partisan loyalty, low protest impact |
| Fujimori | Peru | 1990-2000 | Yes (10 years) | Implemented shock therapy, defeated terrorism, then became authoritarian |
| Castillo | Peru | 2021-2022 | **No** (impeached/removed Dec 2022) | Weak legislative position (no party), constant scandal, economy deteriorated, two impeachment attempts → third succeeded |
| Boric | Chile | 2022-2026 (scheduled) | Yes (so far) | Won on left platform, moderated in office, survived constitutional defeat |
| Petro | Colombia | 2022-2026 (scheduled) | Yes (so far) | Survived scandal, legislative investigation, but still in office |
| Correa | Ecuador | 2007-2017 | Yes (full term) | Strong majority, oil boom funded spending, changed constitution |

**Key insight**: The base rate for first-term radical reformer survival is HIGH (approx 7/8 in the modern period). Removal is the exception, not the rule. The Castillo case (impeached Dec 2022) is instructive as the failure case: weak/no legislative party, economy deteriorating not improving, multiple scandals, and opposition eventually found the votes.

## Argentina-Specific Institutional Resilience

Argentina has not had a president removed before term end since the return to democracy in 1983. Key institutional facts:

- **No successful impeachment** since 1983. Multiple attempts (Alfonsín 1989 hyperinflation crisis, De la Rúa 2001 resigned before impeachment, Fernández 2002-2003, Kirchner 2003-2007, Fernández de Kirchner 2007-2015, Macri 2015-2019) all failed or never reached floor.
- **2001 crisis**: 5 presidents in 2 weeks, but all changed through constitutional succession mechanisms (resignation → legislative appointment). No institutional breakdown, no military intervention.
- **Military**: Thoroughly civilianized. No coup attempts since 1990 Carapintadas mutiny. Military budget is <1% of GDP. The military is neither capable nor willing to intervene in politics.
- **Constitution**: Presidential removal requires impeachment by the Chamber of Deputies (2/3 majority) AND conviction by the Senate (2/3 majority). This is a very high bar. No single party has held 2/3 of either chamber since 1983.
- **Judicial**: Independent judiciary but slow; no precedent for courts removing a president.

## Scoring Rubric

For presidential-removal questions, produce a structured assessment:

1. **Identify plausible removal pathway(s)** from the four categories above
2. **For each pathway**: assess whether the threshold/trigger conditions are met
3. **Check base rate**: how often do presidents in this country/region leave office early?
4. **Check reference class**: how do similar leaders (ideology, economic program) fare?
5. **Synthesize**: if no pathway has its threshold met AND base rate is low AND reference class supports survival, predict NO (continued tenure)
