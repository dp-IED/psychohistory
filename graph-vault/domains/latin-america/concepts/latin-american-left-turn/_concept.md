# Latin American Left Turn (New Pink Tide)

## Definition
The Latin American left turn (also called the "Pink Tide" or "New Pink Tide") refers to the electoral wave of left-wing and center-left governments across Latin America, beginning with Hugo Chávez in Venezuela (1998), followed by Lula in Brazil (2002), Kirchner in Argentina (2003), Evo Morales in Bolivia (2006), Correa in Ecuador (2007), and culminating in the "New Pink Tide" around 2018-2022 with AMLO in Mexico (2018), Boric in Chile (2022), Petro in Colombia (2022), and Lula's return in Brazil (2023).

## Three Generations

### First Pink Tide (1998-2010)
- **Drivers**: Commodity supercycle (oil, gas, copper, soy), inequality backlash after Washington Consensus austerity, weak party systems
- **Mechanism**: Resource nationalism funded social spending expansions; commodity revenues insulated left governments from economic accountability
- **Decline**: Commodity price collapse (2014-2015), corruption scandals (Lava Jato), conservative backlash (Macri in Argentina, Temer/Bolsonaro in Brazil)
- **Key flaw**: Economic model was resource-dependent; when commodity revenues fell, social spending became unsustainable, eroding approval

### New Pink Tide (2018-2023)
- **Drivers**: Anti-incumbent backlash after conservative governments failed to deliver growth (Macri, Temer, Piñera, Duque), inequality persistence, COVID-19 pandemic exposed state capacity gaps
- **Mechanism**: Unlike the first tide, the New Pink Tide has been characterized by **social program entrenchment** rather than commodity-funded expansion. Programs like Brazil's Bolsa Família (re-expanded by Lula), Mexico's pension and youth scholarship expansion (AMLO), and Chile's social reforms (Boric) created durable approval floors decoupled from commodity prices.
- **Distinction**: The New Pink Tide is structurally more resilient than the first because social program recipients are a durable voter bloc irrespective of economic growth. Mexico's AMLO maintained 60%+ approval with mediocre GDP growth — a feat impossible in the commodity-dependent first tide.
- **Current status**: Ongoing but showing limits. Electoral momentum slowed 2024-2025: MORENA dominance in Mexico appears durable, but Lula's approval has declined in Brazil, Petro is struggling in Colombia, and Boric shifted to the center after the 2023 constitutional referendum defeat.

## Core Mechanisms

### 1. Social Program Entrenchment (New Tide only)
Sustained welfare expansion creates a direct beneficiary class that becomes a durable voting bloc. Unlike commodity-funded populism (first tide), social program recipients continue to support the incumbent even when economic growth is weak, because the program *is the economic relationship* — the transfer is more salient than GDP growth. See [[domains/latin-america/concepts/social-program-approval-sustainability/_concept]] for full framework.

### 2. Anti-Establishment Brand While Governing
New Pink Tide leaders (AMLO, Petro, Boric in initial phase) campaigned as anti-establishment outsiders but governed as the establishment. This created a paradox: their base continued to view them as "fighting the elite" even as they controlled all state institutions. The opposition could not effectively attack them as incumbents without being characterized as "defending the old regime."

### 3. State Capacity Weaponization
New Pink Tide governments have systematically reformed electoral systems, judiciaries, and security forces to entrench their institutional advantages. Mexico's 2024 judicial reform (elected judiciary), Colombia's Petro's attacks on the Attorney General's office, and Bolivia's Morales-era constitutional court packing all illustrate this pattern.

## Forecasting Application
When a question involves a Latin American left-wing government or candidate:

1. **Determine which generation**: First Pink Tide (commodity-dependent, pre-2015) or New Pink Tide (social-program dependent, post-2018)? The resilience mechanism differs fundamentally.
2. **Assess social program entrenchment**: How many voters directly benefit from government transfers? What % of GDP is social spending? Durable approval floors form above 15-20% of GDP in social spending.
3. **Check commodity dependence correlation**: Is approval correlated with commodity prices (oil, copper, lithium)? If yes, first-tide vulnerability. If no, New Tide resilience.
4. **Assess institutional capture**: Has the government reformed electoral institutions, the judiciary, or security forces? Entrenched incumbents are harder to dislodge electorally.
5. **Identify successor potential**: If the current left-wing president is term-limited, assess whether the successor-dominance dynamic (see [[domains/latin-america/concepts/incumbent-successor-dominant-party/_concept]]) applies.
