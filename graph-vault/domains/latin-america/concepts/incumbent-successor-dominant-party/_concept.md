# Incumbent-Party Successor Dominance

## Definition
In dominant-party systems where the incumbent leader is **term-limited and cannot run again**, the party's presidential candidate inherits a structural electoral advantage from the outgoing leader — even when the outgoing leader is not on the ballot. This advantage operates through three mechanisms:

1. **Approval transfer**: Voters who approve of the outgoing leader's performance transfer that approval to the successor candidate from the same party. In Mexico 2024, AMLO's ~60% approval produced Sheinbaum's ~60% vote share.
2. **Incumbent campaigning**: The outgoing leader actively campaigns for the successor, deploying their personal approval and party machinery.
3. **Welfare continuity signal**: Voters understand that the successor will continue social programs, preventing the opposition from promising policy change as a differentiating platform.

## Canonical Examples

### Mexico 2024 (Sheinbaum ← AMLO)
- AMLO term-limited (Mexico's single-term presidency)
- AMLO approval: 60-65% throughout 2023-2024
- Sheinbaum's campaign message: continuity with AMLO's "Fourth Transformation" (4T)
- Result: Sheinbaum won with ~60% — nearly identical to AMLO's approval rating
- Opposition fully fragmented, but even unified opposition would have faced 60%+ approval wall

### Chile 2022 (Boric ← Piñera — negative transfer, negative result)
- Piñera's approval was in the low 20s by end of term
- The incumbent-party candidate (Sebastián Sichel, Chile Vamos) ran on continuity with an unpopular government
- Result: Opposition candidate Boric won — the "successor penalty" in reverse
- **Extensions to concept**: When the outgoing incumbent has LOW approval (below 40%), the successor candidate is structurally disadvantaged, NOT advantaged. The mechanism is symmetric — approval transfer works in both directions.

### Brazil 2022 (Lula ← Bolsonaro — negative transfer)
- Bolsonaro was eligible for re-election and ran (no successor dynamic)
- But Bolsonaro's endorsement of allied governors/congressional candidates in 2022 functioned as a parallel mechanism: unpopular incumbent depresses downballot allied candidates

### Venezuela 2012-2013 (Maduro ← Chávez)
- Chávez was not term-limited; he died in office
- Maduro ran as Chávez's chosen successor, won by 1.5% against Capriles
- Chávez's approval was ~55% at death; Maduro won 50.6% — approval transfer operated but was less efficient without Chávez actively campaigning
- Distinguish: post-death succession (no campaigning support) vs term-limited succession (active campaigning)

## Distinguishing from Opposition-Fragmentation Wins
The incumbent-party successor dynamic is distinct from the [[domains/east-asia/concepts/divided-opposition-plurality-win/_concept]] dynamic:

| Feature | Successor Dominance | Fragmentation Win |
|---------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Driver | Positive approval for incumbent | Opposition splitting vote |
| Winner's vote share | Typically 50-65% | Typically 35-48% |
| Opposition status | Weak regardless of coordination | Would win if unified |
| Key metric | Incumbent approval rating | Number of viable candidates |

Mexico 2024 was a **successor dominance** case, not primarily a fragmentation win (though fragmentation was present). Sheinbaum's 60% suggests she would have won even a unified two-candidate race.

## Successor Selection Mechanism: Dedazo vs. Competitive Primary

A critical sub-dimension of successor dominance is **how the successor is chosen**. Two distinct mechanisms exist, with different implications for successor viability:

### Dedazo (Direct Anointing)
- **Mechanism**: The outgoing leader directly designates the successor, and the party machinery ensures the designation is ratified. The term "dedazo" (from *dedo*, finger — "the pointing of the finger") is a Mexican term from the PRI's 71-year rule (1929-2000), where each outgoing president personally chose his successor.
- **How it works**: The incumbent uses party control, government resources, and personal popularity to eliminate alternatives — either by sidelining potential rivals during the primary campaign (making them unelectable) or by structuring the "primary" so the preferred candidate faces weak opposition.
- **Advantages**: The chosen successor inherits the full weight of the outgoing leader's endorsement, party machinery, and government resources. Successor selection is clear and unambiguous — there is no primary fight to heal.
- **Risks**: If the outgoing leader's popularity declines between selection and the election (due to scandal, crisis, or health), the successor is directly associated with a weakened brand. The successor also has weaker independent political identity, making post-election "mask or mandate" governance harder.
- **Mexican tradition**: Every PRI presidential succession from 1929-2000 operated through dedazo (Ávila Camacho → Alemán → Ruiz Cortines → López Mateos → Díaz Ordaz → Echeverría → López Portillo → de la Madrid → Salinas → Zedillo). AMLO explicitly revived this tradition in 2023 by anointing Sheinbaum.

### Competitive Primary
- **Mechanism**: The ruling party holds an open or semi-open competitive primary. Multiple candidates from the party run; the winner emerges with demonstrated electoral strength.
- **How it works**: Candidates compete on policy differentiation, personal organization, and campaign skill. The primary win signals independent political strength.
- **Advantages**: The successor has their own mandate within the party, demonstrated campaigning ability, and independent political identity. The "mask or mandate" problem is reduced.
- **Risks**: Primaries can become factional wars that weaken the party's general-election position. A weaker candidate may win due to party insider maneuvering rather than general-election appeal.

### Forecasting Implications
| Feature | Dedazo | Competitive Primary |
|---------|--------|-------------------|
| Approval transfer efficiency | 90-100% (full inheritance) | 70-85% (partial inheritance) |
| Successor independent mandate | Weak — seen as "continuity" candidate | Stronger — has own campaign coalition |
| Post-election governance | Faces "AMLO version 2" vs "own path" tension | Can claim own mandate more credibly |
| Vulnerability to scandal | High — association with incumbent direct | Moderate — can distance if needed |
| Historical examples | Mexico 2024 (Sheinbaum ← AMLO), Venezuela 2013 (Maduro ← Chávez), Brazil 2010 (Rousseff ← Lula) | Chile 2022 (Sichel ← Piñera via primary, but lost), Brazil 2026 future (likely competitive Lula successor) |

Mexico 2024 was a **pure dedazo** case — AMLO structured the selection process to eliminate Marcelo Ebrard and other alternatives, ensuring Sheinbaum's nomination was effectively an anointment.

## Social Program Approval Floor as Structural Multiplier

In dominant-party systems with large-scale social programs (see [[domains/latin-america/concepts/social-program-approval-sustainability/_concept]]), the successor inherits not just the outgoing incumbent's personal approval but an **institutionalized approval floor** embedded in the beneficiary population:

- **Mechanism**: Social program recipients (typically 30-60% of households) support the dominant party because the party is associated with program continuity. This support transfers to the successor even if the successor has low personal recognition.
- **Magnitude**: The social program approval floor provides an additional 5-15% vote share cushion beyond what the incumbent's personal approval directly transfers. In Mexico 2024, Sheinbaum's ~60% vote share was higher than AMLO's *personal* approval would predict from the raw approval-transfer model, because social program recipients voted for MORENA (the party), not just for AMLO's endorsed candidate.
- **Interaction with dedazo**: Dedazo + social program floor = the most robust succession configuration. The successor inherits both the full personal endorsement of the outgoing leader AND a durable voter coalition anchored by program identity.

## Economic Perception Decoupling (The Peso Paradox)

A counterintuitive factor that strengthens successor dominance in Latin American dominant-party systems: **the decoupling of economic perception from objective macroeconomic performance**.

In Mexico 2024:
- **GDP growth**: Averaged ~1% annually during AMLO's term (2020-2023) — mediocre by historical standards
- **Peso exchange rate**: The peso strengthened dramatically (the "superpeso"), reaching levels not seen since 2015, driven by high interest rates, nearshoring inflows, and remittances
- **Inflation**: Moderate (peaked ~8% in 2022, declined to ~4.5% by early 2024)
- **Voter perception**: Surveys showed a majority of Mexicans rated AMLO's economic performance positively, despite objectively weak GDP growth

**Mechanism**: Voters evaluate economic performance through multiple heuristics, not just GDP growth:
1. Exchange rate strength (a visible, commonly discussed metric)
2. Personal financial situation (program recipients feel better due to transfers)
3. Social program access (beneficiaries evaluate the economy through program adequacy)
4. Inflation relative to wage/program growth (if transfers keep pace with inflation, voters feel stable)

**Forecasting implication**: In dominant-party systems with social program entrenchment, do NOT assume that weak GDP growth will reduce successor vote share. The economic perception variables that matter are: (a) real value of social program transfers, (b) exchange rate stability/direction, (c) inflation relative to benefit growth. Traditional macroeconomic forecasting models (growth → vote share) systematically underestimate incumbent successor performance in these contexts.

## Pattern Archetype
The successor-dominance cycle typically follows:

1. **Term-limited popular incumbent** (approval >55%) cannot run again
2. **Successor selection**: Dedazo (incumbent anoints preferred successor) or competitive primary (multiple candidates compete). Dedazo produces cleaner approval transfer.
3. **Approval transfer**: Successor's poll numbers converge to incumbent's approval rating, amplified by social program beneficiary floor
4. **Opposition reaction**: Opposition tries to run on "change" but cannot attack the popular incumbent directly; opposition coalitions form but are ideologically incoherent
5. **Incumbent campaigning**: Outgoing president actively campaigns for successor, maintaining high visibility
6. **Economic perception cushion**: Weak GDP growth is offset by social program continuity, exchange rate stability, and beneficiary satisfaction
7. **Result**: Successor wins with vote share approximating incumbent's approval rating (±5% depending on social program entrenchment)
8. **Post-election**: Successor faces "mask or mandate" problem — own mandate vs continuation of predecessor's agenda

## Forecasting Application
When a question asks about an election in a dominant-party system where the incumbent is term-limited:

1. **Check term limits**: Is the incumbent able to run? If no, the successor dynamic is active.
2. **Measure incumbent approval**: If >55%, apply successor dominance framework. If <40%, apply successor penalty framework (opposition likely wins). If 40-55%, competitive election.
3. **Assess successor selection**: Was the successor chosen via a competitive primary (less coherent platform) or anointing (more coherent)?
4. **Compare to opposition**: Can the opposition articulate a message that bypasses the incumbent (e.g., run against "more of the same") without attacking the popular incumbent directly?
5. **Forecast the vote share**: Expected successor vote share ≈ incumbent approval rating, ±5%. This is more reliable than polling for the specific match-up.
