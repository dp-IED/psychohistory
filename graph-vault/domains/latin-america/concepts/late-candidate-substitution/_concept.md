# Late Candidate Substitution

## Definition

A pattern in authoritarian or hybrid-regime elections where the regime disqualifies (bars, arrests, or otherwise removes) the most popular opposition candidate, and the opposition selects a late replacement candidate who must rapidly absorb the barred figure's voter support. The critical question for forecasting: under what conditions does the replacement candidate successfully inherit the barred candidate's voter base, versus the substitution failing and the opposition's vote collapsing?

This is a subset of the broader [[domains/latin-america/concepts/authoritarian-electoral-facade/_concept]] pattern — a specific tactic the regime uses to try to hollow out the opposition's electoral challenge. But the substitution dynamic has its own logic that cuts across regime types and requires separate analysis.

## Canonical Cases

### Venezuela 2024 (Successful Substitution)

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| Barred candidate | [[maria-corina-machado|María Corina Machado]] (barred June 2023; won Oct 2023 primary with 92%) |
| Replacement | [[edmundo-gonzalez|Edmundo González]] (career diplomat, low name recognition, selected April 2024) |
| Vote result | González ~67% per parallel count (ConVzla from 81% of polling centers) |
| Mechanism | Machado actively campaigned for González; her grassroots organization transferred wholesale; "one vote" campaign unified opposition behind the replacement |
| Key condition | The barred figure **remained politically active** and **endorsed credibly** — she didn't go silent or go into exile until after the election |

**Success factors:**
1. The barred figure remained free (not arrested) — able to campaign for the replacement.
2. The replacement was seen as a **proxy/placeholder** not a rival — González was a clean slate with no negative baggage.
3. The opposition had already unified organizationally (PUD coalition) — the substitution did not fragment the coalition.
4. Time between substitution and election (~4 months) was sufficient for name recognition to build.
5. The regime's barring mechanism (administrative disqualification via comptroller) allowed Machado to stay active, unlike an arrest.

### Belarus 2020 (Complex Substitution)

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| Barred candidate | Sergei Babaryka (arrested); Viktor Babaryka (detained); other opposition figures arrested or exiled |
| Replacement | Sviatlana Tsikhanouskaya (wife of barred blogger Sergei Tsikhanousky; no political experience) |
| Vote result | Tsikhanouskaya ~60-70% per independent estimates; Lukashenko claimed 80% |
| Mechanism | Wife-as-replacement substitution — the regime did not anticipate a family member stepping in. Tsikhanouskaya's campaign was organized by other opposition figures' teams merging. |
| Key condition | The substitution worked because multiple opposition campaigns (Babaryka, Tsikhanouskaya, Statkevich, etc.) unified behind one replacement — mirroring the PUD's strategy in Venezuela. |

### Russia 2024 (Failed Substitution)

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| Barred candidate | Alexei Navalny (barred, imprisoned, then died February 2024) |
| Replacement | No viable replacement emerged; Boris Nadezhdin briefly collected signatures but was barred; anti-war candidate Yekaterina Duntsova barred |
| Vote result | Putin claimed 87% of the vote |
| Mechanism | Regime systematically barred or arrested any potential replacement before they could build a campaign. The opposition could not organize any parallel vote tabulation. |
| Failure cause | The regime's preemptive repression was comprehensive enough to prevent any organization from forming around a replacement. No credible proxy remained. |

## Causal Chain Analysis

The late-candidate-substitution pattern involves three phases:

### Phase 1: Regime Disqualification Trigger

When does the regime bar a candidate? The regime weighs:
- **Threat level**: How popular is the candidate? (Machado had 60%+ polling; Navalny had ~20-30%)
- **Visibility**: Is the barring internationally noticed? (Venezuela's comptroller ban was less visible than an arrest)
- **Cost of allowing**: Will the candidate threaten regime control? (Machado was existential threat because she united the opposition AND had a reformist agenda that threatened PSUV patronage)
- **Cost of barring**: Sanctions, international reputational damage, opposition mobilization

**Forecasting heuristic**: The regime will bar a candidate when the threat to regime survival exceeds the international cost of barring. The international cost is lower when: (a) an electoral facade exists (official results can be fabricated), (b) the US/EU are distracted, (c) the regime has sufficient sanctions immunity through alternative partners (China, Russia, Iran).

### Phase 2: Replacement Selection

The opposition selects a replacement. Three archetypes:

1. **Proxies with endorsement** (Venezuela 2024): A clean-slate figure endorsed by the barred candidate. Success depends on the barred candidate's credibility and ability to campaign.
2. **Family substitutes** (Belarus 2020): Spouse or relative steps in. Success depends on the family member's political acumen and the opposition's organizational infrastructure.
3. **Coalition candidate** (generic): A party insider or consensus figure from within the opposition coalition. Least likely to inherit the barred figure's personal support unless the barred figure endorses.

**Forecasting heuristic**: Substitution succeeds when:
- The barred figure can **actively campaign** for the replacement (not in prison, not in exile)
- The opposition has **organizational infrastructure** (PVT, ground game) independent of the barred figure's personal brand
- The replacement is seen as a **credible proxy** — the barred figure's supporters do not defect or stay home

### Phase 3: Vote Transfer

The actual voter support transfer depends on:
- **Barred figure's endorsement credibility**: Does the base trust the endorsement?
- **Replacement's profile**: Is the replacement seen as a competent proxy or an empty placeholder?
- **Opposition infrastructure**: Does the ground organization (poll watchers, campaign workers, social media) transfer wholesale?
- **Regime counter-moves**: Does the regime try to discredit the replacement or create a spoiler candidate?

**Forecasting heuristic**: Vote transfer is near-complete (80%+ of barred figure's support transfers) when all four conditions hold. Partial transfer (40-60%) when some conditions fail. Near-zero transfer when the barred figure is imprisoned and no credible organization exists.

## Forecasting Application

When asked about an opposition candidate's chances in an authoritarian election:

1. **Check for disqualification**: Has the regime barred the most popular opposition figure? If no, this concept is not applicable — use standard election forecasting. If yes:
   - Who is the replacement?
   - When was the replacement selected relative to election day?
   - Can the barred figure campaign for the replacement?

2. **Assess substitution viability**:
   - Is the replacement a credible proxy (endorsed by the barred figure)?
   - Does the opposition have organizational infrastructure independent of the barred figure?
   - Has the opposition coalition unified behind the replacement?
   - Can the replacement build name recognition before election day?

3. **Calibrate probability**:
   - **Successful substitution** (replacement wins vote): Requires (a) credible endorsement, (b) unified coalition, (c) PVT infrastructure, (d) sufficient campaign time. Probability ~60-80% if all conditions met.
   - **Partial substitution** (replacement competitive but loses): Missing some conditions. Probability ~30-50%.
   - **Failed substitution** (replacement non-viable): Replacement is an unknown party figure without endorsement, or organization is insufficient. Probability ~10-30%.

4. **Resolution criteria distinction**: Even with successful substitution, distinguish "winning the vote" from "assuming office." The late-candidate-substitution concept predicts vote outcome only — power transition requires separate analysis (see [[domains/latin-america/concepts/authoritarian-electoral-facade/_concept]]).
