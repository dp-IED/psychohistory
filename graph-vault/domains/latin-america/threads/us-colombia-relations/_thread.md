# US-Colombia Relations

## Overview

The US-Colombia bilateral relationship is one of the most durable strategic partnerships in Latin America, anchored in counternarcotics cooperation, trade (US-Colombia FTA since 2012), and shared security interests. However, the relationship has experienced friction under the Petro administration (2022-2026) due to diverging approaches to drug policy, Venezuela, and US military presence. The 2026 Colombian presidential election (May 31 first round) will determine the next phase of the bilateral relationship.

## Key Dynamics

### 1. Counternarcotics Cooperation (Plan Colombia Legacy)

Colombia is the world's largest cocaine producer (~1,700 metric tons/year as of 2024). The US has invested ~$12B in Colombia through Plan Colombia and successor programs since 2000. Under the Petro administration, Colombia shifted from forced eradication to voluntary crop substitution, creating tension with US drug policy priorities.

Trump 2.0's approach to drug trafficking emphasizes:
- Designation of cartels as Foreign Terrorist Organizations (FTOs)
- Potential use of military assets for counternarcotics
- Pressure on source countries to reduce production

The FTO designation framework for Colombian cartels (Clan del Golfo, ELN) could create legal pathways for expanded US operations, though "strikes" remain a significant escalation from current cooperation levels.

### 2. Venezuela Border Dynamics

The 2,200-km Colombia-Venezuela border is a zone of:
- Armed group presence (ELN, FARC dissidents, Venezuelan colectivos)
- Narcotrafficking routes
- Venezuelan migrant flows (~2.5M Venezuelans in Colombia)
- Periodic cross-border incidents

US policy toward Venezuela remains hawkish under Trump 2.0. Any escalation of US-Venezuela tensions could spill into Colombian territory, particularly if:
- Iranian/Hezbollah elements are identified operating from Venezuelan territory near the Colombian border
- Maduro's regime directly threatens Colombian sovereignty
- A mass migration event triggers humanitarian/security crisis

### 3. Military Presence and Cooperation

- US maintains a small military advisory presence under bilateral defense agreements
- Colombia is a Major Non-NATO Ally (designated 2022)
- Joint exercises (e.g., UNITAS, PANAMAX) occur regularly
- US Southern Command (SOUTHCOM) coordinates regional security cooperation
- **No history of unilateral US military strikes on Colombian soil** in the modern era

### 4. Trade Relationship

The US-Colombia Free Trade Agreement (FTA) supports ~$40B in annual bilateral trade. Colombia is the US's third-largest trading partner in Latin America (after Mexico and Brazil). US companies have significant investments in Colombian energy, mining, and manufacturing sectors. Any military escalation would threaten this economic relationship.

### 5. Political Alignment

- **Petro administration (2022-2026)**: Left-wing, cool toward US, pursued "Total Peace" with armed groups, shifted drug policy away from forced eradication. The Trump administration has been publicly critical of Petro's policies.
- **Post-election (June 2026)**: The incoming government (likely Cepeda or de la Espriella) will set the tone for US relations. Cepeda (left/Pacto Histórico) would likely continue Petro's skepticism of US military operations. De la Espriella (populist right) could be more receptive to US security cooperation but would still face domestic constraints on allowing US strikes.

### Structural Barriers

1. **No precedent**: Zero US strikes in Latin America in 35+ years
2. **Ally, not adversary**: Colombia is a democratic ally, not a hostile state
3. **Diplomatic blowback**: Would unite Latin America against the US, empowering China/Russia
4. **Economic cost**: Threatens $40B trade relationship
5. **Military bandwidth**: SOUTHCOM is the lowest-priority combatant command
6. **Congressional notification**: War Powers Resolution requirements create friction, even for Trump
