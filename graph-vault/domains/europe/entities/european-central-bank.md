# European Central Bank

## Summary

The European Central Bank (ECB) is the central bank for the eurozone, responsible for monetary policy across the 19 (later 20) member states that have adopted the euro. Established in 1998, the ECB's primary mandate under Article 127 TFEU is price stability — maintaining eurozone inflation below but close to 2% over the medium term. Its secondary mandate is to support the EU's general economic policies.

The ECB Governing Council, composed of the six-member Executive Board plus the 19 (later 20) national central bank governors, sets interest rates and implements monetary policy through a suite of instruments: the main refinancing operations rate (MRO), the deposit facility rate (DFR), and the marginal lending facility rate (MLF), plus non-standard tools including asset purchase programmes (APP, PEPP), targeted longer-term refinancing operations (TLTROs), and forward guidance on the rate path.

## Significance

The ECB is the most consequential monetary authority for global financial markets after the Federal Reserve. Its decisions directly affect the euro — the world's second-most-traded currency — and financing conditions across the eurozone's ~$15 trillion economy. Under President Christine Lagarde (2019–), the ECB has navigated three distinct regimes: a prolonged low-inflation/low-rate environment (2019–2021), a delayed response to surging post-COVID inflation (2021–2022), and one of the most aggressive rate-hiking cycles in its history (July 2022–September 2023, totalling 450bp).

The ECB's reaction function differs structurally from the Fed's in key ways:

- **Single mandate vs dual mandate**: The ECB's primary objective is price stability; growth and employment are secondary. This makes the ECB structurally more hawkish than the Fed in theory, though in practice it has been slower to hike in this cycle due to the weaker eurozone growth picture and fragmentation risks.
- **No fiscal counterparty**: The ECB operates without a eurozone-wide fiscal authority. Sovereign debt crises (2010–2012, 2022 spread widening) force the ECB into quasi-fiscal roles through tools like the Transmission Protection Instrument (TPI).
- **Governing Council fragmentation**: The 20 national central bank governors bring divergent national inflation experiences, fiscal positions, and political pressures to rate decisions, producing a persistent hawk-dove split that shapes forward guidance and vote outcomes.
- **Exchange rate sensitivity**: As a small-open-economy bloc, the eurozone is more sensitive to currency fluctuations than the US. A weak euro amplifies imported inflation (especially energy priced in USD), making EUR/USD an indirect input into ECB policy decisions.

## HICP Inflation Anchor

The ECB targets eurozone HICP (Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices, [[concepts/hicp-eurostat-inflation-measurement]]) at "below but close to 2% over the medium term." Following the 2021 strategy review, the target was symmetrically revised to 2% — allowing for symmetric overshoots and undershoots. HICP differs from US CPI in several structural respects, particularly the higher weight of energy and food in the eurozone basket and the exclusion of owner-occupied housing costs.

## Cutting Cycle — Causal Chain (For Forecasting)

The ECB's 2024-2025 easing cycle was predictable in sequence because:

1. **Easing cycle momentum**: Once the ECB had confirmed a cutting cycle (June 2024 first cut), the default expectation for subsequent meetings was continued cuts — absent a data shock or guidance shift. This pattern applies to all advanced-economy central banks and is formalized in [[domains/economics/concepts/easing-cycle-sequential-momentum]].

2. **Inflation below target**: Eurozone HICP fell from 10.6% (Oct 2022 peak) to ~1.7% by Sep 2024 — below the 2% target. The disinflation was faster than the ECB's own projections, removing the primary constraint on rate cuts.

3. **Growth deterioration**: The eurozone economy weakened through 2024 — Germany entered a technical recession, manufacturing PMIs remained below 50, and the French political crisis (post-snap election hung parliament) created additional uncertainty for the bloc's second-largest economy.

4. **Global synchronization**: The Fed's September 50bp cut provided global easing cover. Central banks generally prefer to move in sync with the global cycle to avoid disruptive exchange rate movements.

5. **Hawk-dove balance shifted dovish**: The Governing Council's internal balance shifted as inflation undershot the target. Key hawks (Nagel, Holzmann) either voted with the majority or only expressed "concerns" without formal dissent.

## Relationship to Fed Cycle — Key Differences

The ECB cutting cycle diverged from the Fed's in important ways:
- ECB began cutting earlier (June 2024 vs. September 2024) because eurozone inflation peaked earlier and growth was weaker
- ECB used 25bp increments throughout (no 50bp first cut), while the Fed used 50bp for its first cut
- ECB maintained a more consistent cutting cadence (every other meeting through mid-2024, then consecutive from Sep 2024) while the Fed faced a tariff-driven mid-cycle pause in H1 2025
