# Switzerland

## Summary

The Swiss Confederation is a federal republic in Central Europe with a unique political system characterized by direct democracy, consociational governance (multi-party consensus), and permanent neutrality. Swiss citizens vote on popular initiatives and referendums approximately four times per year. The Federal Council (executive) is composed of seven members from the four largest parties, operating under a collegial rather than majoritarian model.

Switzerland's political system makes it structurally resistant to sudden policy shifts. Popular initiatives — constitutional amendments proposed by citizens who collect 100,000 signatures — pass at a historically low rate (~10-15% since 1891). The referendum system acts as a brake on legislative change, while the initiative system provides an outlet for popular mobilization against elite consensus.

## Political Structure

- **Federal Council**: 7-member executive council elected by the Federal Assembly (parliament). Presidency rotates annually among councilors
- **Federal Assembly**: Bicameral parliament (National Council + Council of States)
- **Direct Democracy**: Citizens vote on 3-4 federal ballot measures per year. Initiatives (constitutional amendments) require majority of both popular vote AND cantonal votes to pass
- **Neutrality**: Permanent armed neutrality codified in international law (1815 Congress of Vienna, 1907 Hague Convention)

## Forecasting Significance

Switzerland's initiative system produces predictable patterns: highly restrictive or extreme measures (population caps, blanket bans) tend to fail because they require both popular majority and cantonal majority. The "No to ten million" initiative (June 14, 2026 vote) — which aims to cap Switzerland's population — follows this pattern. Immigration-limiting initiatives have a mixed record: the 2014 "Against Mass Immigration" initiative passed narrowly (50.3%) but subsequent attempts to tighten further have failed as the economic costs became visible.
