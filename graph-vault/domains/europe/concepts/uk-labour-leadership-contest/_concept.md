# UK Labour Party Leadership Contests

## Overview

Labour Party leadership succession — from vacancy creation to new leader appointment — is a multi-stage process with distinct structural constraints relevant to "next PM" forecasting.

## Vacancy Creation

A Labour leadership contest is triggered when:
1. **Incumbent resigns** — the most common path; typically follows a general election defeat or major internal revolt
2. **Incumbent dies or becomes incapacitated** — rare but existential (e.g., John Smith 1994)
3. **Leadership challenge succeeds** — requires 20% of Labour MPs to nominate a challenger (currently ~81 of 404 Labour MPs); triggers a confidence vote; if lost, the incumbent resigns
4. **Party conference vote** — mechanisms exist for delegates to force a contest, but never used in modern history

## Contest Rules (Post-2015 Reforms)

Since Ed Miliband's 2015 reforms:
1. **Nomination**: Candidates need 10% of Labour MPs to nominate (currently ~40 of 404 MPs)
2. **Shortlisting**: If more than 2 candidates, MPs vote in a series of elimination ballots until 2 remain — each round drops the lowest-vote candidate
3. **Final ballot**: One-Member-One-Vote (OMOV) ballot of all full Labour Party members (~350-400K as of 2026), plus affiliated supporters (union members who opt in)
4. **Winning threshold**: Simple majority in final round (not ranked-choice; single vote between final 2)
5. **Timeline**: Typically 8-12 weeks from vacancy to new leader announcement. Emergency procedures can compress but never below ~4 weeks

## Key Structural Patterns

### MP Filter
Only Labour MPs can stand. A non-MP (like Andy Burnham) CAN run — there is no legal or party rule barring non-MPs. However:
- No non-MP has ever won a Labour leadership contest
- Campaigning without a parliamentary platform is exceptionally difficult
- Practical reality: every Labour leader since 1906 was an MP at the time of election

### Electoral College Dynamics (Pre-2015 — Historical Context)
Before 2015, the leadership used a three-part electoral college: MPs/MEPs (⅓), party members (⅓), affiliated unions/societies (⅓). This gave unions a blocking minority. Reforms abolished the college in favor of OMOV.

### Membership Composition
- ~350-400K members (2026 estimate, down from ~500K peak in 2016-2019)
- Skews older, more left-wing, more public-sector than the general electorate
- Soft-left/centrist candidates generally poll best among members (Starmer 2020, Brown 2007, Blair 1994)
- Hard-left candidates (Corbyn 2015) can win when membership surges with new pro-Corbyn joiners

## Historical Precedents

| Year | Outgoing Leader | Reason | Winner | Duration |
|------|----------------|--------|--------|----------|
| 1994 | John Smith | Death | Tony Blair | ~12 weeks |
| 2007 | Tony Blair | Resignation | Gordon Brown | No contest (unopposed) |
| 2010 | Gordon Brown | Resignation (election defeat) | Ed Miliband | ~12 weeks |
| 2015 | Ed Miliband | Resignation (election defeat) | Jeremy Corbyn | ~10 weeks |
| 2020 | Jeremy Corbyn | Resignation (election defeat) | Keir Starmer | ~12 weeks |

Governing party transitions (when Labour is in power): Brown 2007 (unopposed) — no modern precedent for a contested Labour leadership with the party IN government.
