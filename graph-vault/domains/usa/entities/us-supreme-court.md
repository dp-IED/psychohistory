## Summary

The Supreme Court of the United States (SCOTUS) is the highest federal court, with nine justices. The Court's composition during the 2023-2024 term was 6-3 conservative (Chief Justice John Roberts, Clarence Thomas, Samuel Alito, Neil Gorsuch, Brett Kavanaugh, Amy Coney Barrett; liberal: Elena Kagan, Sonia Sotomayor, Ketanji Brown Jackson). The Court's decision in Trump v. United States (July 1, 2024) on presidential immunity was the most consequential ruling for Trump's criminal cases.

The Court agreed to hear Trump's immunity appeal in February 2024 — a decision that effectively delayed the DC election interference trial past the November election. Oral arguments were held April 25, 2024. The ruling, issued July 1, 2024, held that presidents have broad immunity for official acts but not for private conduct, and remanded to the District Court to determine which acts in the indictment were "official" vs. "private."

The timeline from cert grant (February) to ruling (July 1) — approximately 5 months — is standard for a major constitutional case and demonstrates why appeals inherently create delay. Even after the ruling, the remand proceedings consumed additional months. Had the Court declined cert or ruled faster, the trial could have proceeded before the election; but neither outcome was procedurally plausible given the novelty and importance of the question.

## Significance for Forecasting

The Supreme Court's docket and decision timing are critical variables for any forecast involving federal criminal cases against political figures during an election year. The Court's decision to take the case (grant cert) was itself a signal that no trial would occur before the election, given typical briefing-to-decision timelines of 4-6 months for major cases. When the Court grants cert on a novel constitutional question with direct implications for a pending trial, the de facto result is a stay of that trial for the duration of the Court's consideration — typically 5-8 months from cert grant to mandate issuance.

### Procedural Signals for Forecasting

The Court's procedural choices are often the strongest available signal for forecasting substantive outcomes in high-profile cases with statutory deadlines:

1. **Cert before judgment**: The Court grants review before the circuit court has ruled. Extremely rare (~5 cases per term). Signals that the question is urgent and likely clear-cut from the Court's perspective. When the Court takes a case this way, it intends to resolve on the merits before the deadline — not to delay.

2. **Compressed schedule**: Oral arguments within 3-6 weeks of cert grant (vs. 8-12 weeks standard). Signals the Court believes the legal question is relatively clear and wants to rule before a statutory or constitutional deadline.

3. **No stay granted while expediting**: The single strongest signal that the law will be upheld. The Court is saying, in effect, "We'll decide this quickly, but in the meantime the law stands." When the Court denies a stay and expedites the merits review, the probability of the challenge succeeding drops below 10%.

4. **Fast post-argument ruling**: Decision within 7-14 days of oral argument (vs. 60+ days for deeply divided cases). Signals the votes were locked before arguments. Typically correlates with unanimous or near-unanimous decisions.

5. **Unanimous procedural vote**: When all nine justices agree to take a case or deny a stay, the internal division on the merits is minimal — predicting a lopsided ruling.

These signals are formalized in the [[concepts/scotus-procedural-signals]] concept, which provides calibrated probabilities for each procedural choice.
