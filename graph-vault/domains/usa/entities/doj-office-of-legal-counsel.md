# DOJ Office of Legal Counsel (OLC)

## Summary

The Office of Legal Counsel is a division of the US Department of Justice that provides authoritative legal advice to the President and executive branch agencies. The OLC's opinions are binding on the executive branch and operate as controlling internal policy. For forecasting purposes, OLC's opinions on presidential immunity from criminal prosecution are the most consequential — they are the actual mechanism that prevents federal prosecution of a sitting president, independent of any constitutional ruling by the courts.

## Key OLC Opinions on Presidential Immunity

### 1973 Opinion (Watergate, Nixon)

During the Watergate investigation, the OLC issued an opinion concluding that a sitting president cannot be indicted or criminally prosecuted. The opinion reasoned that:

1. Criminal proceedings against a sitting president would impair the president's ability to perform constitutional duties
2. The indictment power was not intended to apply to the president while in office
3. The proper remedy for presidential misconduct is impeachment, not criminal prosecution

This opinion was issued during the Nixon administration but was not specific to Nixon — it articulated a structural constitutional principle that the OLC has maintained ever since.

### 2000 Opinion (Clinton)

The OLC reaffirmed and updated the 1973 opinion during the Clinton administration, providing a more detailed constitutional analysis. This opinion:

1. Confirmed that the immunity applies to any federal criminal prosecution of a sitting president — indictment, trial, or sentencing
2. Distinguished between federal and state prosecutions (the OLC has no authority over state prosecutors)
3. Made clear that the immunity extends for the full duration of the presidency but does not survive leaving office
4. Confirmed that impeachment is the congressionally authorized remedy for presidential misconduct, not criminal prosecution while in office

### Relationship to SCOTUS's Trump v. United States Ruling

The OLC doctrine and the SCOTUS immunity ruling in Trump v. United States (July 1, 2024) address different questions:

| Dimension | OLC Doctrine (1973/2000) | SCOTUS Trump v. US (2024) |
|-----------|------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Legal force** | Internal DOJ policy (revocable by AG) | Constitutional law (binding on all courts) |
| **Who is immune** | Sitting president only | Former presidents for official acts |
| **Scope** | Bars any federal prosecution while in office | Bars prosecution for official acts; private conduct still prosecutable |
| **Effect after office** | Expires upon leaving office | Permanent bar for official acts |
| **Key mechanism** | DOJ will not prosecute | Courts cannot try official-act charges |

For the Trump federal cases:
- **Pre-election**: The SCOTUS ruling was the controlling factor — it delayed the trial by requiring remand proceedings on what was "official" vs. "private" conduct.
- **Post-election**: The OLC doctrine was the controlling factor — once Trump won, DOJ policy prevented any further prosecution regardless of what the courts had ruled.

## Forecasting Significance

For any question about federal prosecution of a political figure who could become president:

1. **If the figure wins the presidency, federal prosecution ends** — regardless of the pre-election legal posture or SCOTUS rulings. The OLC doctrine is absolute while in office.

2. **State prosecutors are not bound by OLC doctrine** — state-level cases (like the NY hush-money case) can proceed against a sitting president. The OLC opinions explicitly note they do not bind state authorities.

3. **A future Attorney General could revoke the OLC doctrine** — but this would require a presidential administration that accepts prosecution of its own sitting president, an unprecedented and politically unlikely scenario.

4. **The OLC doctrine creates a structural two-track system**: federal cases must be completed before the defendant wins an election, while state cases can proceed regardless. This creates the "electoral mooting" dynamic where the defendant's incentive is to delay federal cases past the election while fighting state cases on their merits.

## Key Personnel (Forecasting-Relevant)

- **Assistant Attorney General for OLC**: The Senate-confirmed official who heads the office. Current officeholder controls whether existing OLC opinions are reaffirmed, modified, or revoked.
- **Principal Deputy Assistant Attorney General**: The career official who often serves as acting head during transitions.
