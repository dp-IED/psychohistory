## Summary

Alvin Leonard Bragg Jr. is the Manhattan District Attorney who brought the criminal case against Donald Trump — 34 felony counts of falsifying business records in connection with hush-money payments to Stormy Daniels. He is the first prosecutor in US history to convict a former president of a felony.

Elected in November 2021 as the first Black Manhattan DA, Bragg took office in January 2022. He previously served as a New York Chief Deputy Attorney General, an assistant US Attorney in the Southern District of New York, and a state prosecutor. His career has focused on white-collar crime, public integrity, and civil rights.

## The Trump Prosecution

Bragg inherited the Trump investigation from his predecessor Cyrus Vance Jr., who had secured subpoenas for Trump's financial records and tax returns. Bragg initially hesitated — he declined to present the case to a grand jury in early 2022, leading two prosecutors on the case (Mark Pomerantz and Carey Dunne) to resign and publicly criticize Bragg's decision. This created early narratives that Bragg was too cautious.

### Chronology
- **February 2022**: Pomerantz and Dunne resign after Bragg declines to pursue charges (at that time) — the resignations are leaked to the press, creating public pressure
- **January 2023**: Bragg convenes a grand jury — signaling a reversal of his earlier caution
- **March 30, 2023**: Grand jury indicts Trump on 34 felony counts — Bragg becomes the first prosecutor to charge a former US president with a crime
- **April 15 - May 30, 2024**: Trial — Trump convicted on all 34 counts
- **December 2024** (post-election): Bragg's office files a sentencing memorandum conceding that incarceration is "no longer a practicable recommendation" given Trump's status as president-elect — the decisive factor in Judge [[entities/juan-merchan]]'s sentencing decision
- **January 10, 2025**: Trump sentenced to unconditional discharge — the conviction preserved without tangible punishment

### Bragg's Legal Strategy
Bragg's prosecution relied on elevating the 34 falsifying-business-records counts (Class E felonies) from misdemeanors to felonies by alleging they were committed with the intent to conceal another crime — in this case, violations of New York State Election Law § 17-152 (conspiracy to promote a candidacy by unlawful means) and federal campaign finance violations. This "elevation" strategy was novel but upheld by Judge Merchan. Critics called it an untested legal theory; supporters called it creative use of New York law.

### The Prosecution Posture Concession
After Trump's November 2024 election victory, Bragg's office faced a choice: continue seeking incarceration (which was now practically impossible), or concede the point and preserve the conviction. Bragg chose the latter — his office formally stated that incarceration was "no longer a practicable recommendation" but urged the court to impose a sentence that preserved the conviction.

This concession is a canonical example of Stage 3 (Post-Election Status Shift) in the [[concepts/presidential-sentencing-dynamics]] framework: the prosecution's sentencing posture changes fundamentally once the defendant becomes president-elect, because the judge cannot impose what the prosecutor cannot defend.

## Forecasting Significance

Bragg's prosecution and the subsequent sentencing concession provide a template for forecasting prosecutorial posture in high-profile political cases:

| Variable | Bragg's Case | Forecasting Value |
|----------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Prosecutor's political context** | Elected Democrat in Manhattan (overwhelmingly Democratic); no electoral risk from prosecution | Prosecutor's electoral incentives matter — Bragg was insulated from backlash |
| **Legal theory novelty** | Novel elevation of misdemeanor to felony — untested but plausibly defensible | Novel theories survive pre-trial review but limit sentence range; defense can attack the theory on appeal |
| **Post-victory posture** | Conceded incarceration was impractical; prioritized preserving conviction | Prosecutor's priority shifts from "maximum sentence" to "conviction survives" once the defendant's status elevates |
| **Prosecutor's career trajectory** | Not seeking higher office; new Manhattan DA (first term) | No political ambition to use the case for advancement; pure institutional prosecutor |
| **Timing of charge decision** | Three-year investigation, public prosecutor resignations, then reversal | Bragg's caution before charging indicates a prosecutor who prioritizes legal defensibility over political impact — consistent with his post-election concession |

**Key insight**: Bragg's initial hesitation (declining to charge, resignations by Pomerantz/Dunne) was actually a leading indicator of his eventual sentencing posture — he was a cautious, defensibility-oriented prosecutor who would not pursue an extreme sentence. A more aggressive prosecutor might have pushed for incarceration even post-election, but Bragg's track record indicated he would prioritize the conviction's survival over the punishment's severity.
