## Summary

Jack Smith served as Special Counsel for the US Department of Justice from November 2022 through January 2025, overseeing two federal criminal investigations of Donald Trump: (1) the investigation into efforts to overturn the 2020 presidential election (culminating in the January 6 DC election interference case, US v. Trump, before Judge Tanya Chutkan), and (2) the investigation into retention of classified documents at Mar-a-Lago (US v. Trump, before Judge Aileen Cannon in the Southern District of Florida). Smith was appointed by Attorney General Merrick Garland following Trump's announcement of his 2024 presidential campaign, a move designed to insulate the investigation from political pressure.

Smith's prosecutions faced an aggressive defense strategy centered on delay: Trump's legal team filed multiple motions to dismiss based on presidential immunity, challenged Smith's appointment itself, and used procedural appeals to push timelines past the November 2024 election. The Supreme Court's July 1, 2024 ruling in Trump v. United States (broad presidential immunity for official acts) effectively ensured neither federal case would go to trial before the election. After Trump won the election in November 2024, DOJ policy prohibiting prosecution of a sitting president rendered both cases moribund. Smith resigned as Special Counsel on January 10, 2025, releasing a final report that detailed his findings.

Smith's career prior to the special counsel role included serving as chief prosecutor at the Kosovo Specialist Chambers (The Hague), acting US Attorney for the Middle District of Tennessee, and Chief of the DOJ's Public Integrity Section. His appointment and prosecution strategy were directly influenced by the timeline of the 2024 election — he sought to move cases forward aggressively before the electoral window closed.

## Significance for Forecasting

Smith's experience illustrates a critical dynamic in high-profile political prosecutions: the tension between the prosecution's desire for timely justice and the defendant's incentive to use all available procedural avenues to delay past a politically consequential deadline. For any question about whether a political figure will face trial before an election, the key variables are (a) the complexity of the legal questions the defendant can raise on appeal, (b) the defendant's demonstrated willingness to use delay tactics, (c) the relevant court's docket speed and judge's scheduling practice, and (d) the presence of any extraordinary writ or interlocutory appeal that can stop proceedings.

Smith's tenure also demonstrates the structural vulnerability of politically sensitive prosecutions that overlap with election cycles: the DOJ's policy against election-year investigative steps (the "60-day rule") and against prosecuting a sitting president create hard deadlines that defense delay strategies can exploit.
