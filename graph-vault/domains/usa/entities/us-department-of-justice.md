# United States Department of Justice (DOJ)

## Summary

The US Department of Justice is the federal executive department responsible for law enforcement and legal affairs. For forecasting purposes, the DOJ's most relevant features are its internal policies that constrain federal prosecutions in politically sensitive contexts — especially the Special Counsel regulations, the "60-day rule" against election-influencing investigative steps, and the Office of Legal Counsel opinions on presidential immunity from prosecution.

## Key Internal Policies for Forecasting

### Special Counsel Regulations (28 CFR Part 600)

The Special Counsel framework, created after the independent counsel statute lapsed in 1999, grants a specially appointed prosecutor autonomy over investigation and prosecution decisions while remaining technically under the Attorney General's supervision. The regulations give the Attorney General the power to remove the Special Counsel for "good cause" but otherwise require the Special Counsel to proceed independently.

Key provisions for forecasting:
- The Special Counsel must follow DOJ policies and procedures (28 CFR 600.7)
- The Special Counsel reports to the Attorney General but makes prosecution decisions independently
- The Attorney General must notify Congress of any decision to terminate a Special Counsel
- The Special Counsel submits a confidential report at the conclusion of the investigation

**Forecasting significance**: The Special Counsel framework insulates politically sensitive investigations from direct political pressure but does NOT protect them from DOJ internal policies (including the OLC opinions on presidential immunity). A Special Counsel can indict but cannot prosecute a sitting president.

### The "60-Day Rule"

The DOJ has a longstanding policy against conducting overt investigative steps that could influence a federal election within 60 days of that election. The policy originates from internal guidance (Justice Manual Section 9-85.000) and is based on the principle that the DOJ should not take actions that could affect the outcome of an election.

**Forecasting significance**: For any federal prosecution of a political candidate within 60 days of an election, the DOJ will not take public investigative steps (search warrants, subpoenas, indictments, or trial proceedings). This creates a hard deadline defense teams can exploit: if a trial date falls within the 60-day window, the DOJ will seek to delay it. The 60-day rule was a factor in the DC election interference case timeline, but was secondary to the immunity appeal stay — the case was already stayed by the appeal and never reached the 60-day window.

### The Office of Legal Counsel (OLC) Opinions on Presidential Immunity

The OLC has issued two controlling opinions that a sitting president cannot be indicted or prosecuted:

- **1973 OLC Opinion (Watergate era)**: During the Nixon administration, the OLC concluded that a sitting president is immune from criminal prosecution. The opinion reasoned that criminal proceedings against the president would unduly burden the executive branch and impair the president's ability to perform constitutional duties.

- **2000 OLC Opinion (Clinton era)**: During the Clinton administration, the OLC reaffirmed and updated the 1973 opinion, providing a more detailed constitutional analysis. This opinion confirmed that the immunity applies to any federal criminal prosecution of a sitting president — indictment, trial, or sentencing.

These OLC opinions are separate from and independent of the constitutional immunity the Supreme Court addressed in Trump v. United States (July 1, 2024). The OLC opinions are DOJ internal policy, not constitutional law. A future Attorney General could revoke them. But while in effect, they operate as an absolute bar to federal prosecution of a sitting president.

**Forecasting significance**: The OLC opinions are the actual mechanism that mooted the two federal cases against Trump after his November 2024 election victory. The SCOTUS immunity ruling in Trump v. United States addressed a different question (whether former presidents have immunity for official acts) and was relevant only to the pre-election delay strategy. Post-election, it was the OLC policy — not the SCOTUS ruling — that prevented Smith from proceeding. For any forecast involving federal prosecution of a presidential candidate who could win the election, the OLC doctrine must be considered: if the defendant wins, federal prosecution ends regardless of the pre-election legal posture.

### Election-Year Prosecution Guidelines

The DOJ's internal guidelines (Justice Manual 9-85.000) prohibit federal prosecutors from taking any investigative step or making any prosecution decision for the purpose of affecting an election. While this is framed as a prohibition on partisan intent, its practical effect is to create institutional risk aversion toward any prosecution that could be characterized as election-influencing.

**Forecasting significance**: Federal prosecutors are structurally conservative about election-year cases. The combination of (a) the 60-day rule, (b) OLC immunity opinions, and (c) election-year guidelines creates a strong institutional bias toward delay in any federal case against a presidential candidate. This bias exists independently of the specific legal arguments the defendant raises — it is embedded in DOJ institutional culture.

## Significance for Forecasting

When forecasting outcomes of federal prosecutions of political figures, DOJ internal policies are often more consequential than substantive criminal law. The three key questions to check:

1. **Is the OLC doctrine applicable?** Is the defendant a sitting president, president-elect, or likely to become one before the case resolves? If yes, federal prosecution will terminate upon that event.

2. **Is the 60-day rule implicated?** Is a federal election within 60 days of a scheduled trial or investigative step? If yes, expect the DOJ to delay rather than proceed.

3. **Is the Special Counsel framework active?** Has a Special Counsel been appointed? If yes, the investigation enjoys structural independence but is still bound by OLC doctrine and DOJ guidelines.

For state-level prosecutions (like the New York hush-money case), none of these DOJ policies apply — state prosecutors operate under state law and state ethical rules, which generally lack equivalent immunity doctrines or election-related limitations.

## Timeline (Forecasting-Relevant)

- **1973**: OLC issues first opinion that sitting president cannot be indicted
- **2000**: OLC reaffirms sitting-president immunity doctrine
- **1999**: Independent Counsel statute lapses; replaced by Special Counsel regulations (28 CFR Part 600)
- **November 18, 2022**: AG Merrick Garland appoints Jack Smith as Special Counsel for Trump investigations
- **2023**: Smith obtains two federal indictments against Trump (DC election interference, Florida documents)
- **November 5, 2024**: Trump wins election; OLC doctrine takes effect; both federal cases become effectively dead
- **January 10, 2025**: Smith resigns, releases final report; OLC doctrine prevents any further federal proceedings
