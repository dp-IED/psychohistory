# Trump Criminal Cases

## Overview

Donald Trump faced four concurrent criminal cases during the 2024 presidential campaign — two federal (DC election interference, Florida classified documents) and two state (New York hush money, Georgia election interference). This thread tracks all four cases as a unified legal-political narrative, because the cases interacted in complex ways: the federal cases were overseen by Special Counsel Jack Smith, the New York case produced the first felony conviction of a US president, and the Georgia case was delayed by a district attorney disqualification scandal. The thread's primary forecasting value is understanding how legal timelines interact with electoral timelines in high-profile political prosecutions.

## Key Events

### Pre-Indictment Phase (2022-2023)

- **November 18, 2022**: Attorney General Merrick Garland appoints [[entities/jack-smith]] as Special Counsel to oversee the two federal investigations into Trump (January 6/election interference and Mar-a-Lago documents).
- **March 30, 2023**: Manhattan DA Alvin Bragg obtains indictment of Trump on 34 felony counts of falsifying business records (the "hush money" case). Trump becomes first former US president indicted.
- **June 8, 2023**: Federal indictment in Southern District of Florida — 37 counts related to classified documents (Case No. 23-cr-80101, before Judge [[entities/aileen-cannon]]).
- **August 1, 2023**: Federal indictment in DC — 4 counts related to election interference (Case No. 23-cr-00257-TSC, before Judge [[entities/tanya-chutkan]]).
- **August 14, 2023**: Georgia state indictment — 13 counts related to election interference (Fulton County RICO case, before Judge [[entities/scott-mcafee]], DA [[entities/fani-willis]]).

### Q4 2023: Delay Motions Begin

- **December 2023**: Judge Chutkan denies Trump's motion to dismiss DC case on presidential immunity grounds. Trump immediately files interlocutory appeal — this is the beginning of the delay strategy that would define the DC case's timeline.
- **December 2023**: Supreme Court grants certiorari on the immunity question — effectively staying the DC case for the duration of the appeal.

### Q1 2024: Legal and Political Calendars Diverge

- **January 2024**: Judge Chutkan vacates the March 4 trial date for the DC case, citing the pending immunity appeal. No new date set.
- **January 2024**: New York hush-money trial initially scheduled for March 25.
- **February 2024**: Supreme Court hears oral argument scheduling; sets April 25 for immunity arguments.
- **March 4, 2024**: Missed DC trial date (originally scheduled by Chutkan).
- **March 25, 2024**: New York hush-money trial delayed to April 15 due to late document production.

### Q2 2024: Conviction and Immunity Ruling

- **April 15 - May 30, 2024**: New York hush-money trial. Trump attends daily. [[entities/donald-trump]]
- **May 30, 2024**: Trump convicted on all 34 counts — first US president convicted of a felony. Sentence scheduled for July 11, 2024.
- **June 27, 2024**: First presidential debate (Biden-Trump). Debate disaster triggers Biden withdrawal cascade.
- **July 1, 2024**: [[entities/us-supreme-court]] issues Trump v. United States — 6-3 holding broad presidential immunity for official acts. Case remanded to Judge Chutkan to determine which acts are official vs. private.

### Q3 2024: Immunity Litigation on Remand

- **July 2, 2024**: Judge Chutkan sets August 16 deadline for status conference on next steps.
- **July 11, 2024**: Manhattan Judge Juan Merchan delays Trump sentencing in NY case to September 18, then later to November 26 (post-election).
- **July 13, 2024**: First assassination attempt against Trump at Butler, PA rally.
- **July 15-18, 2024**: Republican National Convention; Trump formally nominated.
- **July 21, 2024**: Biden withdraws from race.
- **August 27, 2024**: Special Counsel Jack Smith files superseding indictment in DC case, adjusting the charges to comply with the Supreme Court's immunity framework (removing certain allegations about Trump's interactions with DOJ, a clearly official function).
- **September 15, 2024**: Second assassination attempt at Trump's golf course.
- **September-October 2024**: Immunity litigation continues in DC District Court. Both sides brief the application of the immunity framework. Judge Chutkan does not set a trial date.

### Q4 2024: Election Moots Federal Cases

- **November 5, 2024**: Trump wins presidential election.
- **November 6, 2024 onwards**: DOJ policy against prosecuting a sitting president takes effect. Both federal cases (DC and Florida) become effectively dead.
- **November 26, 2024**: Judge Merchan orders Trump's NY conviction sentencing stayed indefinitely.
- **December 2024**: Georgia case: Court of Appeals disqualifies DA [[entities/fani-willis]] over misconduct allegations; entire prosecution structure thrown into doubt.
- **January 10, 2025**: Jack Smith resigns as Special Counsel, releases final report detailing findings in both federal cases.

### Post-Election Resolution and Sentencing

- **January 10, 2025**: Manhattan Judge [[entities/juan-merchan]] sentences Trump to unconditional discharge in the NY hush-money case — no jail, no probation, no fine. In his ruling, Merchan notes that incarceration is "a sentence authorized by the conviction but one the People concede they no longer view as a practicable recommendation." The US Supreme Court denies Trump's last-minute emergency stay request in a 5-4 vote (Roberts and Barrett joining the three liberal justices; Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, and Kavanaugh would have granted). The stay denial — applying the [[concepts/adequate-independent-state-grounds]] barrier and the [[concepts/scotus-procedural-signals]] emergency stay framework — was structurally predictable: the federal question was weak (the immunity ruling's relevance to a state business-records case was unclear) and SCOTUS rarely intervenes in state criminal proceedings. Trump becomes the first US president sentenced for a crime. (Source: AP, January 10, 2025)
- **January 2025**: Judge [[entities/aileen-cannon]] dismisses Florida documents case (ruling that Smith's appointment as Special Counsel was unconstitutional — a ruling later criticized by the DOJ but effective given Smith had already resigned).
- **2025**: DC case remains technically open but dormant per DOJ policy against prosecuting a sitting president.

## Key Dynamics

### Delay as a Coordinated Legal-Political Strategy

Trump's legal strategy across all four cases was consistent: use every available procedural mechanism (immunity appeals, motions to dismiss, recusal motions, discovery disputes, interlocutory appeals) to push each trial past the November 2024 election. The strategy succeeded in 3 of 4 cases:
- **DC case**: Immunity appeal ate March-November window → mooted by election → effectively dismissed
- **Florida case**: Judge [[entities/aileen-cannon]] slow-walked proceedings → dismissed post-election
- **Georgia case**: District attorney disqualification scandal → thrown into prolonged appeals
- **New York case**: Only case to go to trial (pre-election conviction) but sentencing was delayed past election

The DC case was the paradigmatic example: defendant appealed an immunity ruling, the appeal was taken by SCOTUS (which had no obligation to take it), the Court's decision-making consumed ~5 months, and the remand proceedings consumed the remaining time before the election. Each step was individually defensible (novel constitutional question, proper appellate procedure) but their cumulative effect was determinative.

### The Supreme Court as De Facto Gatekeeper

The Supreme Court's decision to grant cert in Trump v. United States was not legally required — the Court could have let the DC Circuit decide the immunity question first (the DC Circuit had already rejected Trump's immunity claim). By taking the case directly, the Court ensured that the trial would be delayed past the election. This created a structural criticism: the Court could have expedited its decision (issuing it by March rather than July) to allow the trial to proceed, but chose not to. Whether this outcome was intentional, institutional (the Court processes cases at its own pace), or pragmatic (complex constitutional questions cannot be rushed), the effect was the same.

### The "Election-Year Prosecution" Vulnerability

The cases revealed a structural weakness in the American legal system: a determined defendant facing federal charges in an election year can exploit procedural rights to push trial dates past the election, at which point victory moots the prosecution entirely. This creates a perverse incentive: the more serious the charges (such that conviction would motivate maximum legal resistance), the more likely the delay strategy succeeds, because the defendant's stakes are existential. This is distinct from the normal "delay as defense tactic" pattern because the election creates a hard deadline: if the trial crosses that deadline and the defendant wins, the case is moot. This dynamic only applies when the defendant is a presidential candidate — no other office provides prosecutorial immunity upon victory.

### Four Cases, Four Different Timelines

The four cases diverged dramatically in timeline and outcome due to different judges, different legal questions, and different procedural postures:

| Case | Judge | Pre-Election Trial? | Outcome |
|------|-------|---------------------|---------|
| NY Hush Money | Juan Merchan | **Conviction (May 30)** | Unconditional discharge (Jan 10, 2025) — no jail, probation, or fine |
| DC Election Interference | Tanya Chutkan | No | Mooted by election victory |
| FL Classified Documents | [[aileen-cannon]] | No | Dismissed post-election (Smith appointment unconstitutional) |
| GA Election Interference | Scott McAfee | No | DA disqualification appeals; frozen |

The NY case was the outlier — it went to trial before the election because the charges were state-level (no immunity application), the facts were relatively simple, and Manhattan DA Alvin Bragg was not subject to federal timelines. This demonstrates that state-level prosecutions are structurally less vulnerable to appeal-driven delay than federal ones, because state courts have independent procedures and state-level crimes do not implicate federal immunity doctrines.

## Forecasting Significance

The Trump criminal cases thread provides a framework for forecasting questions about:

1. **"Will X political figure face trial before Y date?"** — Map the available appeals, identify the judge's scheduling practice, determine the appellate timeline, and identify whether the defendant has an incentive to delay. Key indicators: pending interlocutory appeal, novel constitutional question, automatic stay provisions.

2. **"Will a federal prosecution of a political candidate succeed before an election?"** — Assess whether the charges are state or federal. Federal cases face immunity doctrines, longer appeal timelines, and DOJ policy constraints. State cases (NY hush money) are more likely to proceed to trial.

3. **"Will the Supreme Court decision affect a pending trial timeline?"** — When SCOTUS grants cert in a case that directly affects a pending prosecution, the de facto result is an automatic stay for 4-8 months while the Court decides and the lower court applies the ruling.

4. **"Will a criminal conviction affect election outcomes?"** — The NY conviction did not reduce Trump's support; it solidified his base. But this may be specific to Trump's polarization dynamics — not generalizable to all candidates.
