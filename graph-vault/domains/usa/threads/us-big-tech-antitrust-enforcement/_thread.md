# US Big Tech Antitrust Enforcement (2020-2026)

## Summary

A sustained campaign by US federal antitrust enforcers — the FTC and DOJ Antitrust Division — to challenge the market power of the largest technology platforms (Google, Meta, Amazon, Apple). Unlike the national security tech ban framework (TikTok), antitrust enforcement operates through litigation under the Sherman Act and Clayton Act, with timelines measured in years rather than months, and remedies that face extraordinary judicial and political barriers.

## Key Cases

### 1. DOJ v. Google (2020-present)

- **Filed**: October 20, 2020 (DOJ under Trump)
- **Court**: DC District Court (Judge Amit Mehta)
- **Claims**: Google monopolized search and search advertising markets through exclusive distribution agreements (paying Apple $15B+/year for default Safari placement)
- **Status**: Trial concluded Nov 2023; Judge Mehta ruled Google maintained an illegal monopoly in search (August 5, 2024). Remedy phase ongoing in 2025-2026.
- **Significance**: First major US antitrust win against a tech platform since Microsoft (2001). Established that Big Tech platforms CAN lose antitrust cases.

### 2. FTC v. Meta (2020-present)

- **Originally filed**: December 9, 2020 (FTC under Trump administration)
- **Refiled**: August 19, 2021 (FTC under Biden, after original complaint dismissed)
- **Court**: DC District Court (Judge James Boasberg)
- **Claims**: Meta maintained monopoly in personal social networking (PSN) market through anticompetitive acquisitions of Instagram (2012) and WhatsApp (2014), and through anticompetitive conduct against competitors (e.g., restricting third-party app access)
- **Key procedural history**:
  - June 28, 2021: Judge Boasberg dismissed the original complaint for insufficient factual pleading (failure to define the market adequately)
  - August 19, 2021: FTC refiled amended complaint with more detailed market definition
  - January 11, 2022: Judge Boasberg denied Meta's motion to dismiss the amended complaint
  - 2023-2024: Discovery phase (fact discovery + expert discovery)
  - 2025: Summary judgment motions pending
- **Status**: Ongoing in 2026. Trial not yet scheduled as of early 2026. Possible trial date in 2026 or 2027.
- **Significance**: Tests whether antitrust law can unwind consummated mergers (Instagram acquired 2012, WhatsApp acquired 2014 — years before the complaint).

### 3. FTC v. Amazon (2023-present)

- **Filed**: September 26, 2023 (FTC under Lina Khan)
- **Claims**: Amazon maintains monopoly through anticompetitive conduct in online marketplace and fulfillment
- **Status**: Discovery phase in 2025-2026

### 4. DOJ v. Apple (2024-present)

- **Filed**: March 21, 2024
- **Claims**: Apple monopolizes smartphone market through anticompetitive conduct (e.g., iMessage lock-in, cloud gaming restrictions)
- **Status**: Early litigation stage

## Political Landscape

### Biden Administration (2021-2025): Enforcement-First Era

- **Lina Khan** (FTC Chair, 2021-2024) — Transformative figure who rejected the consumer welfare standard as the sole antitrust framework. Advocated for structural remedies (breakups) and aggressive enforcement against tech platforms.
- **Jonathan Kanter** (AAG Antitrust Division, 2021-2024) — Similarly aggressive, revived long-dormant monopolization theories.
- **Executive Order on Competition** (July 9, 2021) — Biden's EO encouraged aggressive antitrust enforcement across all sectors.
- **Result**: Three major monopolization cases filed (Google, Meta, Amazon, Apple), multiple merger challenges, and renewed focus on labor market monopsony.

### Second Trump Administration (2025-): Uncertainty and Moderation

- **Andrew Ferguson** (FTC Chair, 2025-) — More moderate approach. While Trump's first term FTC brought the original Meta case, Trump appointees are generally skeptical of aggressive antitrust enforcement against US tech companies.
- **Leadership shift at DOJ Antitrust Division** — Trump appointees in 2025 brought a more permissive approach.
- **Key dynamic**: The FTC v. Meta case was FILED under Trump (Dec 2020). Trump's DOJ also filed DOJ v. Google (Oct 2020). The cases have institutional momentum but new leadership at both agencies could influence remedy positions and settlement postures.
- **Trump's tech policy**: The Trump administration broadly favored US tech companies, viewing aggressive antitrust enforcement as undermining US competitiveness against China. This created a permissive environment for Big Tech.

### Bipartisan Momentum (2020-2024)

The 2020-2024 period saw unusual bipartisan consensus on Big Tech antitrust:
- Both parties criticized Big Tech (Democrats: market power, privacy, disinformation; Republicans: censorship, conservative viewpoint discrimination)
- The House Judiciary antitrust subcommittee (led by Rep. David Cicilline) issued a landmark report in 2020
- Bipartisan bills like the American Innovation and Choice Online Act (AICOA) and the Open App Markets Act advanced but failed to pass
- Congressional gridlock meant enforcement fell to agencies — but the political will for legislation evaporated after the 2024 election

## Forecasting Implications

1. **Divestiture timeframe**: No US tech platform has been forced to divest a major asset via antitrust litigation since the AT&T breakup (1984). Even in the most aggressive scenario (DOJ v. Google), the first Google search remedy ruling took ~4 years (filing 2020, liability ruling 2024). Remedy likely takes additional 1-3 years. Meta: filed 2020, earliest remedy possible ~2027-2029.

2. **Political headwinds**: The 2025 administration shift from enforcement-first (Khan) to permissive (Ferguson) reduces the probability of aggressive remedies. Trump's FTC may settle on weaker terms or narrow the scope of relief.

3. **Legal barriers to unwinding mergers**: FTC v. Meta seeks to break up consummated acquisitions (Instagram 2012, WhatsApp 2014). Courts are highly skeptical of unwinding mergers that were previously reviewed and cleared. The standard for post-consummation relief is extraordinarily high — the government must show that the merger itself caused anticompetitive harm AND that divestiture is feasible and would restore competition.

4. **Comparative perspective**: EU enforcement (DMA, competition cases against Google/Apple) has proceeded faster and with stronger remedies because EU law does not require showing anticompetitive effects in the same way. US antitrust remedies require extensive economic evidence at each stage.
