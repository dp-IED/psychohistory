# State-Level AI Regulation

## Overview

A thread tracking major state-level AI regulation bills in the United States, with primary focus on California as the bellwether jurisdiction. When federal AI regulation is gridlocked, states — especially California — act as de facto national regulators. This thread tracks the legislative trajectory, veto dynamics, and industry response to state-level AI bills.

The canonical case is SB 1047 (California, 2024), which would have established safety testing requirements for large AI models. It passed the California Senate but was vetoed by Governor Gavin Newsom in September 2024.

## Key Events

### 2023
- **2023-Q1**: California State Senator Scott Wiener announces intent to introduce AI safety legislation, influenced by growing concerns about AI existential risk and the rapid deployment of large language models (ChatGPT launched Nov 2022).
- **2023-Q4**: Wiener introduces SB 1047, the "Safe and Secure Innovation for Frontier Artificial Intelligence Models Act." The bill requires safety testing for AI models above a compute threshold and creates developer liability for "critical harms."

### 2024
- **2024-Q1**: SB 1047 passes the California Senate (32-1). Moves to the Assembly for consideration.
- **2024-Q2**: Heavy lobbying campaign by major tech companies (OpenAI, Google, Meta, Anthropic partially critical). Assembly committee hearings feature split testimony — AI safety researchers (Yoshua Bengio, Geoffrey Hinton) testify in favor; tech industry representatives oppose as too burdensome.
- **2024-Q3, May-Aug**: Assembly floor debate and amendment process. Former Speaker Nancy Pelosi (CA-11) publicly opposes SB 1047, creating a significant Democratic split. The bill is amended multiple times to address industry concerns but maintains its core liability framework.
- **2024-Q3, Aug 31**: California legislative deadline for bill passage. SB 1047 fails to pass the Assembly before the deadline — the bill effectively dies when it does not reach the Governor's desk.
- **2024-Q3, Sep 29**: Newsom announces a separate executive order on AI safety, convening experts and establishing a framework for state-level AI risk assessment — signaling his preferred approach (lighter-touch, expert-driven) vs. the legislative approach of SB 1047.
- **2024-Q3, Sep 30**: Newsom vetoes SB 1047 (the bill did not reach him, but his public posture and alternative executive order effectively function as a pre-veto signal).
- **2024-Q4**: Newsom signs executive order on AI safety and convenes a working group. AI regulation debate shifts to potential 2025 legislative session.

### 2025
- **2025-Q1**: Revised AI safety bills introduced in California legislature, learning from SB 1047's failure. Key changes: narrower scope, more industry consultation, phased implementation.

### 2026
- **2026-Q1**: California legislature considers new AI safety bills in 2026 session. Federal gridlock (deregulatory Trump administration, midterm election bandwidth) maintains California as the de facto AI regulator.
- **2026-Q2**: With the 2026 California gubernatorial primary in full swing (June top-two primary), the governor's race injects AI regulation into campaign dynamics. Multiple candidates stake positions on AI, with Tom Steyer's climate/tech platform and Alex Padilla's Washington experience creating divergent approaches. The open-seat race means the September 30 gubernatorial signature deadline may see different political calculations depending on who emerges as the front-runner in the general election.

## Key Dynamics

1. **California as federal placeholder**: Congress has not passed comprehensive AI regulation. California fills the vacuum, creating de facto national standards that affect companies operating nationwide. This creates tension between state regulation and federal preemption arguments.

2. **Democratic party split on AI**: AI regulation divides the Democratic coalition between:
   - **Safety advocates**: Primarily coastal progressives and existential risk researchers who support precautionary regulation (SB 1047, model licensing).
   - **Innovation Democrats**: Centrists and members with strong tech industry ties who oppose heavy-handed regulation that could cede AI leadership to China.
   - This split is structural, not temporary — it will affect every future AI regulation question.

3. **Governor as veto point**: California's governor (Newsom) positions himself as a national figure on technology policy. A governor with national ambitions faces competing incentives: tough regulation builds progressive credentials, but alienates the tech industry (a major state economic driver) and risks being labeled anti-innovation. The governor's posture is the single most important variable for any bill that reaches the desk.

4. **Tech industry lobbying power**: Major AI companies are headquartered in California (OpenAI in SF, Google in Mountain View, Meta in Menlo Park, Anthropic in SF). Their lobbying spending, campaign contributions, and threat to relocate operations give them disproportionate influence over the legislative process.

5. **Legislative deadline compression**: California's part-time legislature operates on a tight calendar. Bills must pass by August 31 of the second year of the session. This creates a binary cliff — if a bill misses the deadline, it dies regardless of support level. This deadline compresses negotiations, amendments, and coalition-building into a few months.

## Forecasting Significance

This thread enables systematic analysis of future state-level AI regulation questions:
- SB 1047 established a baseline pattern of safety legislation failing due to governor veto; future bills may succeed if they address the specific concerns that led to the veto.
- The Newsom veto created a template for state-level AI regulation: executive order first (light-touch, expert-driven), then potentially legislation in a subsequent session.
- The California legislative calendar (Aug 31 deadline) and gubernatorial signature window (Sep 30) are predictable temporal constraints.
- Future questions about AI regulation in other states (New York, Colorado, Washington) can use California as a reference case for industry response and legislative dynamics.
