# US House Elections

## Overview

The US House of Representatives, with all 435 seats elected every 2 years, is the most directly democratic federal institution — and also the most structurally affected by gerrymandering, geographic sorting, and the nationalization of politics. Since 1994, only one party has held the House for a majority of the time, but the margin has narrowed dramatically since 2018. The 2024 election produced the narrowest House majority of the 21st century: Republicans 220-215, a 4-seat margin — narrower than the 2018-2020 Democratic majority (235-199) and the 2022-2024 Republican majority (222-213). This thread tracks House election outcomes, seat dynamics, and the systemic forces that determine which party controls the chamber.

## Status

Active. The 2026 midterm elections will be a critical stress test of whether the Republican gerrymandering advantage can survive a challenging national environment, or whether Democrats can flip the House back to a majority.

## Key Dynamics

### 1. Generic Ballot to Seat Conversion: The Gerrymandering Gap

The single most important structural feature of modern House elections is the **gap between the national popular vote and seat share**. Since 2012, Republicans have consistently won a seat share that exceeds their vote share — a product of three converging factors:

- **Controlled gerrymandering in key states**: Republicans controlled redistricting after the 2010 census in states like Ohio, North Carolina, Florida, Texas, Georgia, and Wisconsin. The 2020 census redistricting cycle preserved these advantages despite court challenges.
- **Democratic vote concentration**: Democratic voters are disproportionately concentrated in urban districts, producing large vote margins in a smaller number of districts (e.g., NY-13: D+60, CA-12: D+70). Republican voters are more efficiently distributed across suburban/rural districts.
- **Bias persistence**: The efficiency gap (difference in wasted votes between parties) has favored Republicans by 1-3 percentage points in every election since 2012. In 2024, Democrats won the House popular vote by ~0.5% but lost the seat count 220-215 — a seat-vote gap of approximately 5-8 seats.

**Historical seat-vote relationship**: In a neutral national environment (popular vote within 1 point), the seat split is typically 215-220 for one party. The 2024 outcome (R+5 seat margin with D+0.5 vote margin) fits this pattern precisely. A national popular vote margin of ~3 points is typically required to flip control (pick up ~25 seats), because the gerrymandering advantage creates a structural floor for the advantaged party.

### 2. The Narrow Majority Era (2018-present)

Since the 2018 midterms, every House majority has been narrow by historical standards:

| Congress | Years | Majority Party | Seats | Margin | Popular Vote |
|----------|-------|---------------|-------|--------|-------------|
| 116th | 2019-2021 | Democratic | 235-199 | D+36 | D+8.6% |
| 117th | 2021-2023 | Democratic | 222-213 | D+9 | D+3.1% |
| 118th | 2023-2025 | Republican | 222-213 | R+9 | R+2.9% |
| 119th | 2025-2027 | Republican | 220-215 | R+5 | D+0.5% |

The narrowing trend has structural causes:
- **Nationalization of politics**: Local candidate quality and district-level brand differentiation matter less than the national political environment
- **Declining ticket-splitting**: The share of districts that split their ticket between presidential and House candidates fell from ~20% in the 1990s to ~5% in 2024
- **Uniform swing**: Congressional elections increasingly move in lockstep with the national popular vote

### 3. Presidential Coattails in the Modern Era

The 2024 election demonstrated that **not all presidential wins produce House coattails**. Trump won the presidency (312-226 electoral, R+1.5 popular vote) while Republicans **lost** a net ~2 House seats relative to the 2022 midterm baseline. The coattail effect was effectively zero.

Key factors that suppress presidential coattails:
- **The candidate is a known quantity**: An incumbent or former president running again generates less downballot lift than a fresh candidate. Voters have already formed their opinion — the presidential race doesn't generate new information about the party.
- **Low ticket-splitting environment**: In a nationalized electorate, the presidential vote and House vote are tightly correlated. A candidate who barely wins the presidential vote cannot generate a large House wave because the underlying partisanship is already maximally expressed.
- **The midterm penalty narrative**: When polls show a close presidential race, the expectation that the opposing party will retake the House in the midterms may reduce marginal voters' willingness to give the winning party unified control.

Strong presidential coattails (10+ House seats gained with a presidential win) typically require: (a) a fresh candidate or outsider, (b) the presidential winner outperforming the party's baseline by 3+ points, and (c) the opposition party being in structural crisis. The 2008 Obama win (D+8 seats), the 2004 Bush re-election (R+3 seats), and the 1984 Reagan landslide (R+14 seats) all met these conditions. The 2024 Trump win met none of them — Trump was a known quantity, his national margin was +1.5 (roughly baseline), and the Democratic Party, though in post-withdrawal transition, was not in structural crisis.

See [[domains/usa/concepts/presidential-coattail-variability/_concept]] for the full framework.

### 4. The Narrow Majority and Governing

A House majority of 215-220 seats (4-10 seat margin) has specific governing consequences distinct from larger majorities:

- **Speaker survival is fragile**: A 4-5 seat margin means the Speaker can lose only 2-3 votes on any party-line bill. This gives the House Freedom Caucus and other hardline factions effective veto power.
- **Bipartisan governance becomes necessary**: With a narrow majority, the Speaker must increasingly rely on Democratic votes to pass appropriations bills — creating tension with the faction that demands conservative policy riders.
- **Motion to vacate threat is amplified**: Any single member (under current rules) can force a Speaker removal vote. With a narrow majority, even a small faction's defection can paralyze the chamber.
- **Midterm flip risk increases**: A 4-5 seat majority in a presidential year is highly vulnerable to the traditional midterm penalty. Historically, the president's party loses an average of ~25 House seats in midterm elections.

### 5. The 2024 House Election: Outcome and Dynamics

**Results**: Republicans 220, Democrats 215 (435 races). Republicans retained control by a 4-seat margin — the smallest House majority since the 191st Congress (1931-1933).

**Geographic distribution of GOP gains**: 
- Republicans held most of the 19 Democratic-held districts where Trump outperformed expectations
- Democrats flipped several seats in California and New York that had been held by Republicans facing scandal or redistricting challenges
- The overall result was near-identical seat distribution to 2022, reflecting a deeply polarized and stable district map

**Key districts that determined the outcome**:
- CA-45 (Michelle Steel, R): Held by Democrat Derek Tran — one of 6 GOP seats in CA that flipped
- NY-19, NY-22: Democrats flipped both — two of the most competitive districts nationally
- PA-07, PA-08: Republicans defended both narrowly — the Philadelphia suburbs remain a key battleground
- MI-07, MI-08, MI-10: All 3 competitive Michigan districts flipped or were held narrowly — the state's House delegation is now evenly split 7-6 R

**Key lesson for range questions — 215-219 (correct NO)**: The question asked whether Republicans would have 215-219 seats. The actual result was 220 — exactly 1 seat above the upper bound. The reasoning that led to answering NO was correct structurally (Republicans' seat share was near the upper edge of projections), but the range was simply too tight. A forecaster who understood the structural gerrymandering advantage (~5 seats above popular vote share) would have noted that with a tied national popular vote, the Republican seat range would center around 219-220 — making the question's range of 215-219 an unlikely capture of the lower distribution tail (p_yes ~30%). The correct NO prediction reflected good distributional reasoning.

**Key lesson for range questions — 220-224 (wrong NO, actual YES)**: The question asked whether Republicans would have 220-224 seats. With a tied popular vote and GOP structural advantage of ~5-8 seats, the central tendency was ~219. This meant the range 220-224 captured the right-skewed upper tail including the mode area. The correct probability estimate was ~35% — making NO the rational prediction (p_yes < 50%), but with significant uncertainty (35% events happen ~1 in 3 times). The actual 220 outcome fell at the very bottom of the range, confirming the distribution was correct. The lesson: for range questions where the lower bound is AT the central tendency, always treat the probability as moderate (25-35%) even when the range seems high relative to the point estimate. Do NOT treat "the point estimate is below the range" as equivalent to "the range is very unlikely." Use the [[domains/usa/procedures/house-seat-range-forecast]] procedure for structured distributional reasoning.

## Timeline

### Pre-2024: The Narrow Majority Precedent
- **2022 midterms**: Republicans win 222 seats — a historically narrow majority despite expectations of a "red wave." The first sign that the partisan gerrymandering advantage would persist into the post-2020 cycle.
- **2023-2024**: Speaker chaos — McCarthy elected after 15 rounds (Jan 2023), ousted via motion to vacate (Oct 2023), Johnson elected after 3 weeks. The narrow majority made factional defections existentially threatening to GOP governance.

### 2024: The Election
- **2024-11-05**: Republicans win 220 seats, Democrats 215. The US House results confirm the gerrymandering advantage persists. Trump's presidential win produces negligible coattails — Republicans lose seats in New York and California despite Trump's improved performance in those states.
- **December 2024**: The lame-duck shutdown demonstrates the narrow majority's governing consequences. Speaker Johnson cannot pass a CR without Democratic votes, but HFC pressure blocks bipartisanship. The final American Relief Act passes 366-34 — a genuinely bipartisan vote that avoids HFC obstruction.

### 2025: The 119th Congress
- **January 2025**: Mike Johnson re-elected Speaker — requiring multiple rounds and significant concessions to HFC members. The 220-215 margin is the smallest Speaker vote in modern history.
- **Trump's first 100 days**: The narrow House majority constrains Trump's legislative agenda. Major policy priorities (tax cuts, border security, regulatory reform) must be structured to avoid losing more than 2-3 GOP votes.

## Forecasting Significance

1. **House seat projection**: The gerrymandering advantage means the generic ballot-to-seat conversion favors Republicans by ~5 seats. A tied national popular vote produces R+5 seat outcome. To win control, Democrats need a ~2-3 point popular vote advantage.

2. **Midterm vulnerability**: The 220-215 Republican majority is the thinnest possible — the traditional midterm penalty of ~25 seats means Democrats need only a ~3-4 point popular vote advantage to flip the House in 2026. This is a much lower threshold than the structural advantage might suggest because the GOP seat floor is thin.

3. **Speaker survival monitoring**: Any question about House governance in the 119th Congress must account for the 220-215 margin. Speaker Johnson's survival probability is structurally lower than a typical Speaker because of the narrow margin and HFC leverage.

4. **Government shutdown probability**: The narrow Republican majority makes shutdowns more likely because the Speaker cannot pass a clean CR on party-line votes. The baseline shutdown probability for FY2026 is elevated (see [[domains/usa/threads/us-budget-shutdown-dynamics]]).

|5. **Range question methodology**: When a forecasting question asks about a specific numerical range of House seats (e.g., "220-224"), the correct methodology is distribution-based, not point-estimate-based. Use the [[domains/usa/procedures/house-seat-range-forecast]] procedure for structured reasoning. The key insight: a range's lower bound at the distribution mode (~35% probability) is very different from a range's lower bound above the distribution ceiling (~5% probability). Without a procedure, the natural tendency is to compare the range midpoint against the point estimate, which systematically underestimates probability for ranges that capture the right-skewed tail.

|6. **Exact-count methodology — distinct from range questions**: Some forecasting questions ask about an exact count (e.g., "exactly 223 seats") rather than a range. These require fundamentally different methodology. In a 435-seat chamber, no single seat count exceeds ~12% probability at the mode, and counts above the structural central tendency have ~3-7% individual probability. The bin-level distribution table (220-224 = 35%) cannot be used directly for exact-count questions — the within-bin distribution must be estimated. For the 2024 election (μ≈219, σ≈3.5), P(223) ≈ 5%. Always classify the question type before applying the distribution model — see [[domains/usa/concepts/exact-count-vs-range-forecast/_concept]] and the [[domains/usa/procedures/exact-seat-count-forecast]] procedure.
