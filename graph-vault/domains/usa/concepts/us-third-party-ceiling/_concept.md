# US Third-Party Ceiling in Presidential Elections

## Overview

A US-specific application of the cross-domain **third-party ceiling in FPTP** concept (see [[domains/east-asia/concepts/third-party-ceiling-fptp/_concept]]). The general concept — that third-party/independent candidates in first-past-the-post presidential systems cannot win regardless of popularity — applies to US presidential elections with additional structural barriers unique to the American system.

## Core Claim

**No third-party or independent candidate has won a single US state's popular vote in a presidential election since 1968.** Despite numerous high-profile independent campaigns (Ross Perot 1992/1996, John Anderson 1980, Ralph Nader 2000, Jill Stein 2012/2016, Gary Johnson 2012/2016, Robert F. Kennedy Jr. 2024), none has reached the 15-28% state-level threshold needed to overcome the Democratic or Republican floor in even a single state.

The last third-party candidate to win any state was **George Wallace** (American Independent Party, 1968), who won 5 southern states (AL, AR, GA, LA, MS, plus one faithless elector from NC) as a protest candidate on a segregationist platform — a unique historical case that does not serve as a replicable model.

## US-Specific Structural Barriers

The general third-party ceiling (organizational deficit, donor scarcity, strategic voting, media coverage disparity, ballot access) applies in the US with these additional barriers:

### 1. Electoral College Amplification of FPTP

The Electoral College is 51 separate FPTP elections (50 states + DC). Even if a third-party candidate won a plurality of the national popular vote (which no third-party candidate has since 1860), they would still need to carry individual states. The Electoral College raises the state-level threshold: a third-party candidate needs not just a plurality of voters in a state but enough of a coalition to overcome the state's partisan floor.

**Example**: In 1992, Ross Perot won 18.9% of the national popular vote — the best third-party showing since 1912 — but won **zero states** and **zero electoral votes**. His support was distributed too evenly across states to reach a majority or even a plurality in any single state. This is the "national ceiling without state breakthrough" pattern.

### 2. Ballot Access Variation by State

Unlike Taiwan (single national ballot), the US has 51 different ballot access regimes. Third-party candidates must petition separately in each state, with varying signature requirements, filing deadlines, and fees:

| State | Requirement to appear on ballot |
|-------|-------------------------------|
| Texas | ~113,000 signatures (highest) |
| California | ~219,000 signatures for independent |
| New Mexico | ~24,000 signatures for minor-party candidate |
| Oklahoma | ~64,000 signatures |
| Many states | 5,000-30,000 signatures |

**Forecasting implication**: A third-party candidate's ballot access footprint is a concrete, verifiable indicator of organizational capacity. A candidate on all 50 state ballots (like Ross Perot 1992, Gary Johnson 2016, Jill Stein 2016) has greater spoiler potential than one on 30. A candidate on only 10-20 ballots cannot mathematically win enough electoral votes.

### 3. Debate Commission Threshold

The Commission on Presidential Debates (CPD) requires candidates to reach 15% in 5 nationally recognized polling averages to qualify for the general election debates — a threshold no third-party candidate has reached since the commission was formed in 1987. Ross Perot came closest (14.5% in August 1992). Exclusion from debates suppresses third-party candidates' visibility and perceived viability, creating a self-reinforcing ceiling.

### 4. Campaign Finance Asymmetry

Major-party candidates receive public funding and DNC/RNC coordinated spending; third-party candidates face FEC limits on coordinated spending with their nominal party. The Supreme Court's *Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee v. FEC* (1996) and *McConnell v. FEC* (2003) created a legal landscape where major-party PACs and super PACs vastly outspend third-party equivalents.

### 5. Media Horse-Race Framing

US media coverage of presidential elections focuses almost exclusively on the two major-party candidates. Third-party candidates receive coverage only when they are polling at 10%+ (creating a self-reinforcing ceiling — they need coverage to reach 10%, but won't get coverage until they do) or when they are perceived as a spoiler threatening one major-party candidate.

## Historical US Third-Party/Independent Candidates and State Wins

| Candidate | Election | Natl. Vote % | States Won | Best State % | Outcome |
|-----------|----------|-------------|------------|-------------|---------|
| John C. Breckinridge (Southern Dem) | 1860 | 18.1% | 11 | ~75% (SC, AL, MS) | Lost, but won electoral votes in a 4-way race |
| Robert M. La Follette (Progressive) | 1924 | 16.6% | 1 (WI) | 53.2% | Won only home state |
| George Wallace (AIP) | 1968 | 13.5% | 5 (AL, AR, GA, LA, MS) | 65.6% (AL) | Last to win states; segregationist protest |
| John Anderson (Ind.) | 1980 | 6.6% | 0 | 15.2% (MA) | No states |
| Ross Perot (Ind./Reform) | 1992 | 18.9% | 0 | 30.0% (ME) | No states despite 18.9% nationally |
| Ross Perot (Reform) | 1996 | 8.4% | 0 | 13.8% (ME) | No states |
| Ralph Nader (Green) | 2000 | 2.7% | 0 | 10.1% (AK) | Spoiler role in FL 2000 debate |
| Gary Johnson (Libertarian) | 2016 | 3.3% | 0 | 9.3% (NM) | No states |
| Jill Stein (Green) | 2016 | 1.1% | 0 | 2.6% (HI) | Negligible |
| Robert F. Kennedy Jr. (Ind.) | 2024 | 5+% (withdraws Aug) | 0 | ~10-12% in some early polls | Withdrew, endorsed Trump |

**Key pattern**: Only candidates with a **sectional base** (Wallace: southern segregationists; La Follette: midwestern progressives) have won states. Nationally distributed third-party candidates (Perot, Anderson) win zero states regardless of national vote share. A third-party candidate could win 20% of the national vote and still win zero states if their support is evenly distributed.

## New Mexico-Specific Third-Party History

New Mexico has never been won by a third-party candidate in its history as a state (1912-present). The best third-party performances in NM:

| Candidate | Election | NM Vote % | National Vote % |
|-----------|----------|-----------|-----------------|
| Ross Perot | 1992 | ~16% | 18.9% |
| Ross Perot | 1996 | ~8% | 8.4% |
| Robert F. Kennedy Jr. | 2024 | Withdrew before filing completed | ~5% (pre-withdrawal polling) |
| Gary Johnson (former NM Gov.) | 2016 | 9.3% (best state) | 3.3% |

**Note on Gary Johnson 2016**: Johnson was a former two-term governor of New Mexico — the absolute best-case scenario for a third-party candidate winning the state. He had name recognition, executive experience, and was on the ballot in all 50 states. Despite this, he won only 9.3% in New Mexico (his best state nationally). The result confirms that even a locally popular third-party candidate faces a ceiling well below electoral viability.

## Forecasting Application

When a question asks "Will a candidate from another party win [State X] in the US presidential election?"

### Step 1: Classify the state's reliability
Use [[domains/usa/concepts/state-electoral-reliability/_concept]] to determine whether the state is competitive in a two-party context.

### Step 2: Identify the third-party/independent candidate
Is there a credible non-major-party candidate? Options in US elections:
- **Major independent**: Ex-major-party figure running as independent (RFK Jr. 2024, Perot 1992)
- **Third-party nominee**: Candidate of a minor party (Jill Stein/Green, Chase Oliver/Libertarian)
- **Spoiler-entrenched**: Nader 2000, Stein 2016

### Step 3: Apply the ceiling framework
- No third-party candidate has won ANY state since 1968 (Wallace).
- Only candidates with deep sectional/regional bases (Wallace: South) have won states.
- Even a former governor (Johnson in NM) could only reach 9.3%.
- The candidate requires not just statewide popularity but enough to overcome the stronger major party's floor in that state.

### Step 4: Check for withdrawal risk
Third-party candidates often withdraw late in the cycle (RFK Jr. August 2024). A candidate who withdraws cannot win. Track:
- Polling trajectory (is it collapsing or stable?)
- Endorsement signals (major-party overtures?)
- Ballot access status (have they completed filing or are they cutting deals?)

### Step 5: Calibrate probability

| Scenario | P(Third-party wins state) |
|----------|--------------------------|
| Third-party candidate, any state, no Wallace-level sectional base | <1% |
| State has history of third-party strength (e.g., AK, VT, ME) | <2% (they draw support but still below winning threshold) |
| Former governor of the state runs as independent | 1-3% (best-case: still below winning threshold) |
| Major independent polling 15-25% nationally | 0% (national polling ≠ state wins; Perot 1992 proof) |
| Sectional candidate with deep regional support (Wallace 1968 pattern) | Depends on state electoral strength, typically 5-20% for states in the region, 0% outside |

For New Mexico specifically: **P(third-party candidate wins) ≈ 0%**. Even Gary Johnson (former governor, in-state favorite son) only reached 9.3%. The state's Democratic floor (~45%) and Republican floor (~40%) leave only ~15% of the vote for third-party candidates — not enough to win a plurality in any scenario. A third-party candidate would need to simultaneously appeal to Hispanic Democrats, northern conservatives, and Native American voters — three groups whose priorities rarely align.

## Relationship to State Electoral Reliability

The [[domains/usa/concepts/state-electoral-reliability/_concept]] explains why a **major-party** candidate cannot flip a state absent a landslide. This concept explains why a **third-party** candidate cannot win the state in a two-party-dominant system. The two are complementary: the state's partisan floor (D+5 to D+15 for Likely D states) also functions as a third-party ceiling because even disaffected major-party voters tend to return to their party's candidate on election day.

## Relationship to Cross-Domain Third-Party Ceiling

This concept is a domain-specific instance of the general [[domains/east-asia/concepts/third-party-ceiling-fptp/_concept]] framework. The structural drivers are the same (FPTP, organizational deficit, donor scarcity, strategic voting, media disparity) with US-specific amplifiers (Electoral College, state-level ballot access, debate commission threshold, campaign finance asymmetry).
