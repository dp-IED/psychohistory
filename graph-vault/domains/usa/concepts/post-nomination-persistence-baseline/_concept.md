# Post-Nomination Persistence Baseline

## Definition

A structural baseline in US presidential elections: once a candidate has secured their party's nomination (sufficient delegates to clinch), withdrawal from the race is extraordinarily rare. Since the modern primary system was established (~1972), **zero non-incumbent presumptive major-party nominees have withdrawn from the race**. The only withdrawals by presumptive nominees in this period were by **sitting incumbents** (Truman 1952, LBJ 1968, Biden 2024) — a categorically different scenario governed by the [[concepts/incumbent-withdrawal-cascade]] framework.

This baseline is distinct from the [[concepts/leadership-persistence-under-threat]] concept. The persistence-under-threat concept explains why external threats *harden* a leader's determination. The post-nomination baseline explains why even *without* external threats, withdrawal is structurally blocked by institutional mechanics. The two reinforce each other when both are active (as with Trump 2024), but they operate through different mechanisms.

## The Structural Mechanism

Post-nomination withdrawal is blocked not by psychological determination but by institutional lock-in:

1. **Delegate commitments**: State parties require delegates to vote for the candidate who won their primary/caucus (at least for the first ballot). Releasing delegates is procedurally complex and requires the candidate's formal consent at the convention, creating a Catch-22: a candidate who wants to withdraw must first be released by the same delegates they won.

2. **Ballot access**: By the time a candidate has secured the nomination, they have filed for ballot access in 50 states, each with different deadlines, requirements, and legal procedures. Withdrawing after ballot access is filed creates legal and logistical chaos — replacement candidates may miss deadlines in some states, face litigation, or have to be substituted by party committees.

3. **Campaign infrastructure**: Staff (paid and volunteer), office leases, advertising contracts, polling contracts, and vendor relationships are built around the nominee. Shutting these down costs money and creates legal exposure. Transferring them to a successor creates coordination problems and loyalty issues.

4. **Fundraising dependencies**: By the nomination phase, the campaign has accepted contributions designated for the general election. These cannot be transferred directly to another candidate. The nominee would need to refund donors or transfer to the party — both administratively costly and legally constrained by FEC rules.

5. **VP selection disruption**: The nominee has typically selected a running mate by or shortly after securing the nomination. Withdrawal creates a cascading problem: does the VP candidate stay, get replaced, or become the new nominee? This was the central problem in the Eagleton case (1972 — VP nominee withdrew, not the presidential nominee) and Biden's case (2024 — a smooth VP-succeeds transition was possible only because Harris was the sitting VP).

## Canonical Test: The 2024 Paired Case

The 2024 election provides the strongest test of this baseline in modern history — both a persistence case (Trump) and a withdrawal case (Biden) within the same cycle:

### Trump 2024: Post-Nomination Non-Incumbent Persistence

| Variable | Value | Signal |
|----------|-------|--------|
| Nomination status | Clinched March 12 | Locked in — delegate commitments, ballot access filed, convention scheduled |
| Incumbent? | No (former president, challenging incumbent) | Non-incumbent frame applies |
| Legal jeopardy | Yes — 34 felony convictions (May 30) | Creates existential motivation to persist |
| Internal withdrawal pressure | Limited to <5% of party | Weak — suppresses cascade before it starts |
| Outcome | **Did not withdraw** (won general election) | Consistent with baseline |

Key insight: Trump's persistence was **over-determined** — the post-nomination baseline alone (>99% persistence) would have predicted NO withdrawal even without the legal jeopardy compounding factor. The legal jeopardy made withdrawal additionally impossible but was not necessary for the baseline prediction.

### Biden 2024: Incumbent Withdrawal

| Variable | Value | Signal |
|----------|-------|--------|
| Nomination status | Presumptive nominee (won all primaries) | Nominally locked in, but incumbency creates different dynamics |
| Incumbent? | **Yes** — sitting president | Incumbent frame applies |
| Legal jeopardy | None — no federal charges, not a target | Withdrawal-cascade conditions 1/5 met |
| Internal withdrawal pressure | Intense (70%+ of party elites) | Cascade conditions 2/5 met |
| Trigger event | Debate disaster (June 27) | Cascade conditions 3/5 met |
| Successor available | Harris (VP, 72-hour absorption) | Cascade conditions 4/5 met |
| Electoral position | Weak (trailing, dragging down-ballot) | Cascade conditions 5/5 met |
| Outcome | **Withdrew** (July 21) | 5/5 conditions met → >70% withdrawal probability per cascade framework |

Key insight: Biden's withdrawal does NOT contradict the post-nomination baseline because Biden was an **incumbent**. The baseline applies to non-incumbent nominees — a sitting president who chooses not to run for re-election is governed by the [[concepts/incumbent-withdrawal-cascade]] framework, not the post-nomination baseline.

| Hillary Clinton | 2016 | Non-incumbent (former SoS) | No | Lost general election |
| **Joe Biden** | **2024** | **Incumbent (president)** | **YES** | **Incumbent — see withdrawal-cascade concept** |

**Zero non-incumbent withdrawals in 12 cases over 52 years.**

The last time a non-incumbent who had clearly secured the nomination withdrew was... never in the modern primary system. You have to go back to the pre-modern, convention-dominated era (before 1972) to find cases like Thomas Eagleton (1972 VP nominee, not presidential nominee — withdrew after pressure about his electroshock therapy history) or Sherman (1880) — neither of which involved a presumptive presidential nominee.

## Distinction from Related Concepts

### vs. [[concepts/leadership-persistence-under-threat]]
- **Persistence-under-threat**: Psychological/situational — explains why threats harden determination. Applies to any leader facing legal, physical, or institutional threats.
- **Post-nomination baseline**: Structural/institutional — explains why even without threats, withdrawal is mechanically blocked. Applies only to US presidential nominees who have secured the nomination.

**Relationship**: When both are active (candidate has secured nomination AND faces legal jeopardy), the two mechanisms compound. The persistence probability approaches 100%. When only the baseline is active (candidate secured nomination, no legal jeopardy), persistence remains >99% for non-incumbents.

### vs. [[concepts/incumbent-withdrawal-cascade]]
- **Withdrawal-cascade**: Explains the conditions under which leaders DO withdraw (no legal jeopardy + internal pressure + trigger + successor + weak position). Applies primarily to incumbents.
- **Post-nomination baseline**: Explains why withdrawal is structurally blocked. Applies to non-incumbent nominees.

**Relationship**: The two are mirror frameworks for different candidate types. The withdrawal-cascade framework explains the three incumbent withdrawals in the modern era (Truman, LBJ, Biden). The post-nomination baseline explains why zero non-incumbents have withdrawn. **The most common forecasting error is applying the wrong frame to the wrong candidate type** — using the incumbent-withdrawal-cascade for a non-incumbent (which would overestimate withdrawal probability) or using the post-nomination baseline for an incumbent (which would underestimate it).

## Forecasting Application

### When to Use This Concept

Apply this concept whenever a question asks whether a US presidential candidate will drop out of the race, and the candidate has already secured the nomination or is within 4 weeks of doing so.

### Calibration Table

| Candidate Type | Withdrawal Baseline | Notes |
|----------------|-------------------|-------|
| **Non-incumbent, nomination clinched** | <1% | Structural lock-in + zero historical cases since 1972 |
| **Non-incumbent, likely nominee (>80% delegate count)** | <5% | Technically not clinched but functionally locked in |
| **Incumbent, nomination clinched** | See [[concepts/incumbent-withdrawal-cascade]] | 5-condition framework applies; baseline removes to 15-35% even before trigger |
| **Non-incumbent, still in primary phase** | 5-25% | Depends on campaign financial health, polling trajectory, primary results |
| **Primary-phase challenger, no path to nomination** | 50-90% | Depends on whether the candidate stays for leverage/VP/future runs |
| **VP nominee (post-convention)** | <5% | Zero VP nominees have withdrawn since Eagleton 1972 (he was selected at convention, not a sitting VP) |

### Key Forecasting Steps (Cross-Reference to Procedure)

1. **Gate on nomination status**: Has the candidate secured the nomination? If yes and non-incumbent → <1% baseline. Stop here for most questions.
2. **Gate on incumbency**: Is the candidate a sitting president? If yes → switch to the [[concepts/incumbent-withdrawal-cascade]] framework entirely. The post-nomination baseline does not apply.
3. **Check for compounding factors**: Even with the <1% baseline, legal jeopardy (present), an assassination attempt (present), and party restructuring around the candidate (present) all drive the probability even lower — making the prediction essentially deterministic.
4. **Combine with the [[procedures/candidate-withdrawal-probability]] procedure** for the full 7-step assessment integrating both concepts.

### Risk of Misapplication

The most common forecasting error is applying the incumbent-withdrawal-cascade framework to a non-incumbent candidate. This error works as follows:

1. Forecaster sees a non-incumbent candidate facing challenges: poor debate performance, donor anxiety, trailing in polls.
2. Forecaster checks the 5-condition withdrawal-cascade framework: 2-3 conditions met (internal pressure absent, trigger event ambiguous, electoral position weak).
3. Forecaster estimates 15-30% withdrawal probability.
4. **Wrong** — the post-nomination baseline (<1% for non-incumbents) overrides the cascade framework. The candidate will not withdraw because the structural lock-in makes it mechanically nearly impossible, regardless of how challenging their electoral position becomes.

The Biden-Trump 2024 paired case is the canonical example: applying the cascade framework to Trump (non-incumbent) would have suggested a small but non-zero withdrawal probability (legal jeopardy + trigger events = persistence, not withdrawal, per the framework — but a literal reading of "5 conditions met = withdrawal" could mislead). Trump's structural baseline was <1% from the moment he clinched the nomination on March 12, 2024. Biden's baseline (as an incumbent governed by the cascade framework) was materially different.
