# Impeachment Inquiry Failure Mode

## Definition

The observation that the initiation of a House impeachment inquiry — an investigative process — is not a reliable leading indicator for the House actually voting on articles of impeachment. Across US history, multiple presidential impeachment inquiries have been launched, debated, or authorized without ever advancing to articles being voted on by the full House. The structural characteristics that make an inquiry likely to fail to produce articles are distinct from those that make articles likely.

## Core Distinction

| Stage | Description | Threshold | Historical frequency |
|-------|-------------|-----------|---------------------|
| **Impeachment inquiry** | Formal House investigation into whether grounds for impeachment exist | Simple majority vote (or Speaker's unilateral authority) | Multiple per century; exact count depends on definition |
| **Articles of impeachment** | Formal charges voted by the full House | Simple majority on each article | 3 presidents (Johnson, Clinton, Trump x2) in US history |
| **Senate conviction** | Removal from office | 2/3 supermajority (67 votes) | 0 presidents removed via conviction |

**Key insight**: The gap between "inquiry" and "articles" is vast and structurally determined. Most inquiries fail to produce articles. The forecasting error is treating an inquiry as a step toward impeachment when it may be an end in itself.

## Structural Factors That Predict Inquiry Failure

1. **Opposition party's narrow House majority** (<10 seats): A narrow majority means the Speaker can lose only 3-5 votes on any party-line measure. If even a small number of moderate members from swing districts oppose impeachment, articles cannot pass. The 118th House (222-213 at start, narrowing to 217-215 after expulsions/resignations) had no margin for defections on a controversial impeachment vote.

2. **Lack of a "smoking gun" connecting the president directly**: Inquiries based on indirect association (family members' business dealings, administration policy decisions, "weaponization" allegations) are structurally weaker than those based on direct presidential action (obstruction of justice, abuse of power, incitement). The stronger the chain of inference between evidence and presidential culpability, the higher the probability of articles. The Comer/Jordan inquiry relied entirely on indirect evidence — Hunter Biden's business dealings, IRS whistleblower claims, FD-1023 form allegations — none of which directly implicated Joe Biden. Contrast with Trump's first impeachment (solicitation of foreign interference, directly documented in the White House call summary).

3. **Split-committee investigation** (multiple chairs, no unified strategy): When the investigation is divided between committees with different chairs, different evidence focuses, and potentially competing agendas, the probability of producing a unified set of articles decreases. Fragmented leadership creates coordination costs, mixed public messaging, and competing theories of impeachment that are harder to translate into coherent articles.

4. **Electoral proximity**: As a presidential election approaches, the political calculus for impeachment changes. For the party in the minority in the House, impeaching a president of the opposing party in an election year:
   - **Advantage**: Base mobilization, campaign messaging
   - **Disadvantage**: Swing-district members risk backlash if the impeachment is seen as purely partisan; Senate trial during the campaign season; lack of governing time
   - The net effect shifts from "impeachment as governance" to "impeachment as messaging" — and messaging investigations rarely produce articles because their purpose is content creation, not legislative action.

5. **Senate composition precluding conviction**: When the president's party holds 34+ Senate seats (enough to block conviction), the impeachment can never result in removal. This reduces the incentive for House members to take the political risk of voting for articles — they are incurring the cost of a controversial vote for a symbolic outcome. The exception is when the House's goal is purely symbolic (e.g., Trump's second impeachment after Jan 6, where the purpose was to bar future office-holding).

6. **Speaker succession mid-inquiry**: If the Speaker who initiated the inquiry is removed or resigns during the investigation, the new Speaker faces different political incentives. The successor may be:
   - More cautious (needs to consolidate support, avoid risky votes)
   - More responsive to the base (incentivized to pursue impeachment more aggressively)
   - Less personally invested in the inquiry (no "ownership" of the announcement)
   
   The probability of articles typically decreases after a Speaker change because the new Speaker has not staked their reputation on the outcome and can allow the inquiry to quietly conclude without floor action.

## Canonical Case: Biden Impeachment Inquiry (2023-2024)

| Factor | Status | Impact on articles probability |
|--------|--------|-------------------------------|
| House majority | 222-213 (narrowest in history for 118th) | Strongly negative |
| Smoking gun? | No. No direct evidence linking Joe Biden to Hunter's business dealings | Strongly negative |
| Split investigation | Oversight (Comer) + Judiciary (Jordan) — different focuses, no unified strategy | Moderately negative |
| Election proximity | Articles would come 3-12 months before Nov 2024 | Moderately negative |
| Senate composition | 51-49 Democratic (conviction impossible) | Strongly negative |
| Speaker change | McCarthy removed Oct 2023; Johnson less invested in inquiry timeline | Moderately negative |

**Composite assessment**: The Biden inquiry exhibited ALL five structural factors predicting failure. The probability of articles reaching the floor was <15% from the outset, declining to <5% once Johnson took the speakership and ordered committees to produce a report rather than articles.

## Canonical Counterexample: Trump First Impeachment (2019)

| Factor | Status | Impact |
|--------|--------|--------|
| House majority | 235-199 Democratic | Moderately favorable (more margin for defections) |
| Smoking gun? | Yes — White House call summary, whistleblower complaint, witness testimony | Strongly favorable |
| Unified investigation | Intelligence Committee controlled the process, then Judiciary | Favorable |
| Election proximity | 13 months before Nov 2020 | Neutral (early enough to act, late enough to matter) |
| Senate composition | 53-47 Republican (conviction impossible) | Strongly negative (but House proceeded anyway — symbolic purpose) |
| Speaker ownership | Pelosi personally drove the process | Favorable |

**Lesson**: The Trump first impeachment succeeded in producing articles despite Senate conviction being impossible, because (a) the direct evidence was overwhelming and (b) the House leadership owned the outcome. The Biden inquiry had neither.

## Forecasting Application

When a question asks "Will [President] be impeached before [date]?":

1. **Determine the stage**: Has the House launched an inquiry? Introduced articles? Held a floor vote?
2. **If only an inquiry exists**: Apply the structural factors above. The default assumption should be that inquiries do NOT produce articles unless:
   - Direct evidence links the president to alleged misconduct
   - The majority party has a comfortable margin (10+ votes) on party-line measures
   - A single committee controls the investigation (no fragmentation)
   - The president's party does NOT control the Senate
   - The deadline is more than 6 months from the next election
3. **If articles have been introduced**: The probability rises significantly. The remaining question is whether the Speaker will bring them to a floor vote — which depends on the whip count and whether the majority can hold its members.
4. **If articles pass the House**: The question shifts to Senate conviction probability (requiring 2/3 supermajority).
