# Prosecutorial Election-Year Timing Constraints

## Definition

A set of informal DOJ policies, norms, and institutional constraints that limit federal prosecutors' actions during election seasons — independent of any defense-side delay strategy. These constraints create a structural bias against federal trials of political candidates occurring in the pre-election window, regardless of the merits or pace of the case.

This concept is the **prosecutor-side mirror** to the defense-side delay framework in [[concepts/judicial-timing-political-deadline]]. Both operate simultaneously: the defense actively delays, while the prosecutor is institutionally constrained from acting too close to an election. The combined effect is that federal criminal trials of presidential candidates occurring in the final 6-12 months before an election are structurally improbable.

## The DOJ "60-Day Rule" / October Surprise Policy

The DOJ has a longstanding informal policy — variously called the "60-day rule," "September rule," or "October surprise" policy — that discourages overt investigative or prosecutorial steps within approximately 60 days of a federal election. The policy is designed to avoid the appearance of influencing election outcomes through the timing of prosecutorial actions.

### What the Rule Restricts

- New indictments naming a candidate or their close associates
- Pre-trial hearings that generate significant media attention
- Trial starts that would overlap with the campaign's final weeks
- Sentencing hearings for high-profile political figures
- Public statements, press releases, or document dumps that could affect voter perceptions

### What the Rule Does Not Restrict

- Routine non-public investigative steps (subpoenas, witness interviews, document reviews)
- Ongoing proceedings already before a judge with fixed schedules
- State-level prosecutions (DOJ has no authority over state prosecutors — the NY hush-money case proceeded despite being in the pre-election window)
- Cases involving national security emergencies

### Legal Basis

The policy is not codified in statute or formal DOJ regulation. It derives from:

1. **The DOJ Manual's "Principles of Federal Prosecution"** (Section 9-27.260): The manual discourages actions that could "unduly influence" an election but does not specify a time window or define "unduly influence." This provides wide prosecutorial discretion.

2. **Attorney General memoranda**: Each AG may issue specific guidance. Merrick Garland issued memos reinforcing the policy but neither expanded nor contracted it from prior administrations.

3. **Historical practice**: The policy has been followed by both Democratic and Republican administrations since at least the 1980s, operating as a durable norm that would require explicit reversal to change.

### Enforcement and Waiver

- The policy is self-enforcing within DOJ — career attorneys and political appointees both internalize it.
- The Attorney General can waive the rule for specific cases, but this would create a public controversy that itself undermines the purpose of avoiding political influence.
- In practice, the rule is treated as effectively binding for high-profile cases involving presidential candidates.

## Historical Examples

### FBI October Surprise (2016): James Comey's Letters

The most controversial application of the proximity-to-election principle was FBI Director James Comey's October 28, 2016 letter to Congress announcing the reopening of the Hillary Clinton email investigation — 11 days before the election. Comey was criticized for violating the "60-day rule" by taking a public step that could affect the election. The backlash from this episode made subsequent DOJ leaders *more* cautious about election-year actions, reinforcing rather than weakening the constraint for the 2020 and 2024 cycles.

### Merrick Garland's 2024 Approach

Garland, appointed in the aftermath of the Trump investigations, was acutely aware of the Comey precedent. His approach:

- **Appointed Jack Smith in November 2022**: Before Trump formally announced his 2024 campaign, insulating the investigation from accusations of political timing.
- **Secured indictments by August 2023**: Well before the "60-day window" (which started roughly September 2024), ensuring the charging phase was complete before the election-year constraint took effect.
- **Avoided further public steps near the election**: No new indictments, no public statements about ongoing cases, no visible investigative steps after mid-2024.
- **Manages the "acceleration vs. restraint" tension**: Garland's DOJ moved aggressively in 2023 to get indictments filed, knowing that 2024 would bring the election-year constraint. But the defense's delay strategy (immunity appeal) consumed the available pre-constraint window.

The key insight: Garland was constrained BOTH by the 60-day rule AND by the defense's delay strategy. The 60-day rule meant that even if the immunity appeal had been resolved faster, there was a hard deadline (roughly September 2024) beyond which the DOJ would resist taking visible steps.

## Application to the Trump 2024 Federal Cases

| Factor | Impact on Timing |
|--------|-----------------|
| Indictments filed August 2023 | Compliant with rule (12+ months before election) |
| March 4, 2024 trial date | Well within pre-60-day window — would have been compliant |
| SCOTUS immunity appeal (Feb-Jul 2024) | Consumed the usable window |
| Remand proceedings (Jul-Nov 2024) | Entered the 60-day window — DOJ uncomfortable pushing for trial |
| Post-September 2024 | Effectively no trial possible per DOJ norms |
| November 5, 2024 election | Cases mooted by Trump's victory |

The table shows that even if SCOTUS had ruled by May 2024 instead of July 2024, the remand proceedings would have spilled into the 60-day window, creating a separate (non-defense-driven) obstacle to trial.

## Distinction from Defense Delay

| Dimension | Defense Delay (judicial-timing-political-deadline) | Prosecutorial Timing Constraint |
|-----------|---------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| Actor | Defendant's legal team | DOJ leadership & career attorneys |
| Mechanism | Appeals, motions, stays | Policy adherence, norm compliance |
| Motivation | Existential (victory moots case) | Institutional (avoid political appearance) |
| Legal force | Procedural rights (appeal, motion practice) | Internal policy (revocable by AG) |
| Binding on state cases? | No (state courts independent) | No (DOJ has no state authority) |
| Predictability | High — defendant clearly benefits from delay | High — policy is well-established |
| Trump 2024 effect | Pushed trial from Mar to Nov 2024 | Rendered trial impossible after Sep 2024 |

Both mechanisms independently prevent pre-election federal trials. The defense delay consumed the pre-September window; the 60-day rule would have blocked the September-November window even without defense delay.

## Forecasting Significance

When forecasting whether a federal criminal trial of a political candidate will reach a milestone before an election:

1. **Check the proximity to the election**: If the target date falls within 60 days of the election, the DOJ's informal policy makes trial structurally unlikely regardless of the court's schedule or the defense's legal maneuvers.

2. **Map the dual constraints**: Calculate both (a) the minimum time needed for procedural steps (the defense-delay timeline from [[concepts/judicial-timing-political-deadline]]) AND (b) the date by which the DOJ would effectively cease visible action (roughly 60 days before the election). The milestone must be attainable within the EARLIER of these two constraints.

3. **Distinguish state vs. federal**: State prosecutors are not bound by the DOJ's 60-day rule. The NY hush-money case (state-level) proceeded to trial and verdict well within the pre-election window. When forecasting state-level trials, apply only the judicial-timing-political-deadline framework, not this concept.

4. **Assess the AG's posture**: A politically independent AG like Garland is more likely to follow the 60-day rule strictly. An AG appointed by the defendant's party may be more willing to take election-year actions against a political opponent. The AG's independence (or lack thereof) is a variable in the constraint's stringency.

5. **Calibrate the "Garland Doctrine"**: After the Comey 2016 backlash, DOJ leaders have been hyper-cautious about election-year actions. Any forecast involving DOJ action within 60 days of an election should assume near-zero probability absent extraordinary circumstances (national security emergency, congressional subpoena).
