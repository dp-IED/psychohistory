# Incumbent Vice President Renomination Norm

## Concept

When a sitting US president runs for re-election, **the incumbent vice president is always renominated** for a second term. This norm has held continuously since the modern presidential system was established, with zero exceptions for presidents who sought re-election with a sitting VP.

The norm operates through several reinforcing mechanisms:

1. **Institutional continuity**: Dropping a sitting VP signals chaos, disunity, or failure in the administration. It invites the question "why did the president choose wrong the first time?"

2. **Party coalition management**: The VP was chosen to balance the ticket; dropping them alienates the faction or constituency they represent.

3. **Governing relationship**: The president and VP have worked together for 4 years. Dropping the VP would be a personal and professional repudiation that damages the administration's final months.

4. **Electoral risk of replacement**: Any VP replacement introduces unknown variables — vetting problems, media scrutiny, public unfamiliarity. The known incumbent VP is a safer choice than an unknown replacement.

5. **Successor signal**: Dropping the VP signals that the president does not trust their judgment — harming the VP's future political prospects and creating intraparty tension.

## Historical Record

### Sitting VPs Renominated When the President Sought Re-Election

| Year | President | VP | Result |
|------|-----------|-----|--------|
| 1940 | F. Roosevelt | J. Nance Garner → H. Wallace | Exception: FDR was seeking a third term (unprecedented), Garner opposed him for the nomination, was replaced. |
| 1944 | F. Roosevelt | H. Wallace → H. Truman | Exception: Wartime, FDR was seeking a fourth term (unprecedented), party convention replaced Wallace. |
| 1956 | Eisenhower | Nixon | Renominated |
| 1972 | Nixon | Agnew | Renominated (Agnew resigned later, replaced by Ford) |
| 1976 | Ford | Dole | Renominated Ford was an incumbent who took office after Nixon's resignation |
| 1980 | Carter | Mondale | Renominated (lost to Reagan) |
| 1984 | Reagan | G.H.W. Bush | Renominated (won) |
| 1992 | G.H.W. Bush | Quayle | Renominated (lost to Clinton) |
| 1996 | Clinton | Gore | Renominated (won) |
| 2004 | G.W. Bush | Cheney | Renominated (won) |
| 2012 | Obama | Biden | Renominated (won) |
| 2020 | Trump | Pence | Renominated (lost to Biden) |
| 2024 | Biden | Harris | Renominated (nomination was planned; Biden withdrew before it occurred) |

**Exceptions**: Only FDR in 1940 and 1944, when he was seeking an unprecedented third and fourth term under exceptional war conditions. No president seeking a standard second term has dropped their VP. Zero exceptions in 70+ years of the two-term norm.

### When VPs Are NOT Renominated

The VP is not renominated only when:
- **The president does not seek re-election** (e.g., Truman 1952 → Barkley not renominated; LBJ 1968 → Humphrey not renominated; Biden 2024 → Harris became presidential nominee, not VP)
- **The VP seeks the presidency themselves** instead of a second VP term
- **The VP resigns or dies** before the election year

In none of these cases is the VP "dropped" — the selection decision is moot because the ticket changes for reasons unrelated to VP performance.

## Forecasting Application

### For "Will [name] be the VP nominee?" Questions

If the question asks whether a sitting VP will be the running mate of an incumbent president seeking re-election:

**Default forecast**: YES, P > 99%. The historical record shows zero instances of a sitting VP being dropped from a re-election ticket under normal two-term-seeking conditions.

**The only plausible exceptions** require one of:
1. The president is seeking an extraordinary extension of their tenure (3rd+ term, as in FDR's case)
2. The VP has become a severe political liability (indictment, scandal, incapacitation) — a condition that has never actually triggered a VP drop in modern history
3. The president withdraws from the race, making the VP no longer the running mate (Biden 2024)

### For "Will another [gender/person] be the VP nominee?" Questions About an Incumbent VP

When the question asks about "another" person being VP while an incumbent VP is in office and the president is running for re-election:

- The default probability of "another" is near-zero because the question is equivalent to "will the incumbent VP be dropped?"
- P(another) | president seeking re-election = P(VP dropped before election) ≈ <1%
- P(another) | president withdraws = depends on whether the VP or another candidate becomes the presidential nominee

**Applying to the 2024 question**: Before Biden's withdrawal (July 21), any question about "another man" being the Democratic VP nominee had to contend with the fact that Kamala Harris (a woman) was the incumbent VP. Harris as VP was going to be renominated. Any "another" VP question was therefore asking about an event with <1% probability while Biden remained in the race. After Biden withdrew, the question changed entirely — Harris became the presidential nominee, and the VP slot opened for a new person.

### Key Distinction: VP ≠ Running Mate for a Different President

The incumbent VP renomination norm applies ONLY when the **same president** is running for re-election. If a different candidate becomes the party's presidential nominee (whether through withdrawal, death, or primary defeat), the VP slot opens and the norm does not apply. The 2024 case demonstrates this sharply: Biden's withdrawal immediately made Harris's VP role moot, and she became a presidential candidate.

### Calibration Rules

| Condition | P(incumbent VP renominated) | Rationale |
|-----------|---------------------------|-----------|
| President running for re-election, VP in good standing | >99% | No historical counterexamples under standard conditions |
| President running for re-election, VP under scandal | 95-99% | Scandals that don't produce resignation are typically weathered; the cost of dropping VP exceeds scandal cost |
| President running for re-election, VP under indictment | 80-95% | Untested — no VP has faced indictment while serving. Precedent suggests extreme reluctance to drop. |
| President not seeking re-election | N/A | VP slot opens; norm does not apply |
| President withdrew before re-election bid | N/A | VP slot opens or VP becomes presidential candidate; norm does not apply |
