# Program Restriction vs Elimination

## Core Insight

When a politician threatens to "eliminate" a federal program, the actual outcome is almost always **restriction** (tightening eligibility, reducing funding, slowing processing, or adding enforcement) rather than **elimination** (terminating the statutory authority or completely ending operations). The gap between elimination rhetoric and restriction reality is one of the most reliable patterns in US policy forecasting — and one of the most commonly missed by naive forecasters.

## Why Elimination Is Structurally Harder Than Restriction

### Legal Framework: The *Youngstown* Problem
- Programs created by statute (the vast majority of federal programs) cannot be eliminated by executive action alone. The President may direct agencies to stop enforcing a law, but this triggers immediate litigation under *Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer* (1952) — the "steel seizure case."
- The Supreme Court has consistently held that the President cannot nullify a statute. Even Trump's first-term travel ban (EO 13769) was sustained only after multiple revisions and on national security grounds, not a general power to set aside immigration law.
- Restriction, by contrast, operates within delegated authority: agencies can interpret ambiguous statutes, set enforcement priorities, and adjust regulations within the bounds Congress set.

### Administrative Law: Rulemaking Timelines
- Program elimination via regulation requires notice-and-comment rulemaking under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), which takes 6-18 months minimum. An attempt to rush this invites procedural challenge.
- Restriction can often be achieved through interim guidance, policy memoranda, or enforcement discretion — tools that take days or weeks, not months.
- Even "emergency" rulemaking (good cause exemption under 5 U.S.C. § 553(b)(B)) faces immediate litigation risk.

### Political Economy: The "Ally Fallacy"
- Every federal program has a constituency. Threatening elimination mobilzes the full constituency. Threatening restriction fragments it.
- For H-1B: elimination would unite Amazon, Google, Apple, Microsoft, Tesla, SpaceX, universities, hospitals, and industry associations into a single opposition bloc. Restriction (higher wage requirements, narrower definitions) divides them — large firms can absorb cost increases, small firms cannot.
- This is a general pattern: elimination creates a unified opposition; restriction shifts the burden unevenly, fracturing the opposition.

### Congressional Dynamics
- Elimination requires either (a) new legislation or (b) an executive action that Congress funds and fails to challenge. Both are high bar.
- Restriction requires neither — it operates within existing appropriations and statutory authority.
- Congress can restrict program funding in appropriations, but elimination by defunding requires multiple consecutive CRs/appropriations with the restriction — this takes years, not 100 days.

## Application to Forecasting

When evaluating a "will X eliminate/abolish/end program Y" question, assess:

1. **Statutory basis**: Is the program created by statute (high elimination bar) or by executive order (low bar)?
2. **Coalition alignment**: Would elimination unite the executive's own coalition in opposition?
3. **Constituency size**: How many voters, businesses, and institutions benefit? Larger = harder to eliminate.
4. **Time horizon**: First 100 days? Use [[concepts/first-100-days-action-horizon]].
5. **Administrative path**: Can restriction achieve the political goal at lower cost?
6. **Precedent**: Has this executive restricted the program before? (Restriction precedent predicts more restriction, not sudden elimination.)

## Historical Examples

| Program | Rhetoric | Action Taken | Outcome |
|---------|----------|-------------|---------|
| H-1B visa (Trump 2017-2021) | "They're taking our jobs" / "Buy American, Hire American" | EO 13788 tightened enforcement; 2020 rule raised wage standards (partially blocked) | Restriction, not elimination |
| DACA (Trump 2017) | "End DACA immediately" | Rescission announced Sep 2017; Supreme Court blocked full rescission (June 2020); Trump administration stopped accepting new applications | Attempted elimination blocked; de facto restriction achieved |
| Affordable Care Act (Trump 2017-2021) | "Repeal and replace" | Legislative repeal failed (2017); administrative actions weakened individual mandate, expanded short-term plans | Legislative elimination failed; administrative restriction partially succeeded |
| DOE Office of Civil Rights (Trump 2017) | Eliminate/defund | Budget proposed cuts; Congress rejected; enforcement priorities shifted | Restriction via enforcement shift, not elimination |
| Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (Trump 2017-2021) | "End the CFPB" | Appointment of Mulvaney as acting director; reduced enforcement; budget requests for zero funding rejected by Congress | Restriction via leadership, not elimination |

## Signals That Elevate Elimination Probability

- Explicit legislative proposal with cosponsors (not just campaign rhetoric)
- Prior successful elimination of analogous program (e.g., if Congress already defunded a similar program)
- Crisis framing that shifts public opinion sharply against the program (e.g., national security threat linked to program)
- Broad bipartisan support for elimination (rare)
- Program has zero organized constituency (very rare for federal programs)

## Diagnostic Questions for Forecasters

1. Is this program statutory or executive-created?
2. Has Congress ever considered eliminating it, and what was the outcome?
3. Does the executive's own coalition have a faction that would be harmed by elimination?
4. What is the cheapest restriction action that achieves the political objective?
5. How long until the next election creates accountability pressure?
