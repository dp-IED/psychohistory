# Governor Veto of Tech Bill

## Definition

A structural pattern in California (and potentially other tech-heavy states) where the governor vetoes or allows to die a first-of-its-kind technology regulation bill, often after it passes the legislature. The veto reflects competing structural pressures on the governor: progressive safety/consumer advocates demand action, while the tech industry (a major economic driver and source of campaign contributions) demands restraint.

## Key Variables

1. **Governor's National Ambition**: A California governor with national ambitions (Newsom, potentially future governors) faces a cross-pressure:
   - Signing a tough tech bill builds progressive/regulatory credentials with the national Democratic base.
   - But it also creates a target for opponents (anti-regulation, anti-California messaging) and alienates a key donor and constituency (the tech industry).
   - **Signal**: When a governor convenes national convenings on tech policy (e.g., Newsom's AI executive order and expert convening in Sep 2024), the governor is signaling national ambition. The stronger the national ambition, the HIGHER the probability of vetoing controversial industry-opposed bills.

2. **State Economic Exposure**: California's economy is uniquely dependent on the technology sector. Tech industry GDP contribution is ~15-20% of state output. A governor who harms the tech industry's competitiveness risks:
   - Reduced state tax revenue (capital gains, corporate taxes).

3. **Bill Novelty**: First-of-its-kind bills face higher veto probability because:
   - The governor cannot benchmark against other states' experience.
   - Unintended consequences are uncertain.
   - The governor can claim a "wait and see" approach.
   - **Each subsequent attempt at similar legislation has a lower veto probability** because the novelty discount shrinks.

4. **Override Threshold**: California requires a 2/3 supermajority in both chambers to override a veto. This is virtually impossible for a controversial bill that divides along party lines — Democratic governors can rely on the override threshold as an absolute veto. This makes the governor's signature effectively dispositive.

5. **Alternative Executive Action**: Governors can pursue tech regulation through executive orders, task forces, and expert panels — avoiding the binding legislative process. When an alternative executive pathway is available, the governor is more likely to veto legislation and substitute executive action, because:
   - Executive orders are more flexible and reversible.
   - They build the governor's national brand without creating binding law.
   - They satisfy the "doing something" demand without the political cost of signing.

## Canonical Case: SB 1047 — Newsom's Virtual Veto (2024)

SB 1047 (Wiener) was the first AI safety bill to pass a chamber in the US. Key facts:

| Parameter | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Bill scope | Broad liability for AI model "critical harms" |
| Legislative outcome | Passed Senate (32-1), failed to pass Assembly before deadline |
| Governor posture | Never received bill (Assembly didn't pass), but Newsom signaled veto posture through executive order alternative |
| Override possibility | 0% — bill lacked support for 2/3 override in either chamber |
| Industry opposition | Major tech companies (OpenAI, Google, Meta) lobbied heavily against |
| Governor alternative | Executive order convening AI experts and establishing state AI risk framework |
| National ambition signal | Newsom positioning for post-2024 national role; wanted balanced AI posture |

The veto dynamic operated through mechanism 5 (alternative executive action): Newsom could achieve his national-credibility goal through executive order without the political cost of signing a bill that divided his party and drew industry opposition.

## Forecasting Application

For any future state-level tech bill on the governor's desk:

1. **Check governor national ambition**: Is the governor positioning for higher office? If yes → 50-70% veto probability for broad, novel, industry-opposed bills.
2. **Check alternative pathways**: Can the governor achieve the same policy goal through executive action? If yes → 60-80% veto probability (the governor gets the benefit without the cost).
3. **Check industry opposition intensity**: Are major tech companies united against the bill? If yes → 50-70% veto probability. If divided (some support, some oppose) → 30-50%.
4. **Check bill novelty**: First-of-its-kind → 60-80% veto probability. Second/third attempt → 30-50%.
5. **Check override capacity**: If override is impossible (party split or narrow majority), treat the governor's position as dispositive.
