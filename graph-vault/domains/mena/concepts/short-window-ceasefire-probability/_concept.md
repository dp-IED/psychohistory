## Definition

The probability that a ceasefire is announced within a specific short calendar window (days to weeks) in an active conflict follows a characteristic distribution that differs dramatically from longer windows (months to years). Unlike military strikes — which are unilateral actions requiring only one party's decision — ceasefires require **mutual consent from both belligerents**, functioning mediation infrastructure, and alignment of political incentives on both sides. This makes short-window ceasefire forecasts structurally distinct from short-window military strike forecasts, and typically **lower probability for a given window length** because two parties must say "yes" rather than one.

## Core Principle: The Mutual-Consent Penalty

For any given window length W, the probability of a ceasefire is approximately:

P(ceasefire in W) = P(A ready) * P(B ready) * P(mediators bridge gap) * P(political will aligned)

Where each term is a distinct multiplicative factor. For military strikes: P(strike in W) ≈ P(attacker ready) * P(patron green light) — only one party's readiness matters.

**The mutual-consent penalty** means that even when one side is ready for a ceasefire, the probability remains low if the other side is not. This is the dominant structural difference from military strike forecasting.

## Calibration Scale: Ceasefire Probability by Window Length

For an **ongoing asymmetric conflict** (state vs non-state, e.g., Israel-Hamas) with no prior ceasefire history in the current war phase:

| Window Length | Approximate Ceasefire Probability | Notes |
|----|---|---|
| 1 week | < 0.01 | Unless superpower patron imposes one (escalation-bargaining pattern) |
| 3 weeks | ~0.01-0.03 | Requires pre-existing negotiation framework AND both sides ready |
| 1 month | ~0.02-0.05 | Possible if hostage/prisoner exchange mechanism exists |
| 3 months | ~0.05-0.15 | Depends on military exhaustion and diplomatic pressure accumulation |
| 6 months | ~0.15-0.30 | Longer window allows diplomatic infrastructure to mature |
| 1 year | ~0.30-0.50 | If structural drivers exist and war aims are achievable |

These are **significantly lower** than military strike base rates for comparable windows, reflecting the mutual-consent penalty.

## Factors That Increase Short-Window Ceasefire Probability

1. **Pre-existing negotiation framework**: Are mediators (Qatar, Egypt, US) actively shuttling proposals? A framework in place, even if stalled, compresses the timeline from 6+ months to weeks.

2. **Imminent political deadline**: A known upcoming transition (inauguration, election, summit) creates temporal compression. See [[political-deadline-ceasefire]].

3. **Superpower patron imposition**: When a superpower enters combat on one side (escalation-bargaining pattern), a ceasefire can be imposed within 72 hours. See [[escalation-bargaining-termination]].

4. **Leadership decapitation of hardliner**: Killing the most hardline leader of a non-state group opens a 2-4 month negotiation window. See [[leadership-decapitation-negotiation-window]].

5. **Military exhaustion**: One or both sides have reached their military objective or have been exhausted by 12+ months of combat.

6. **Hostage/prisoner exchange mechanism**: If a deal framework exists that ties prisoner releases to a ceasefire, negotiations can accelerate rapidly — the exchange creates a mutually beneficial outcome that can overcome political obstacles.

## Factors That Decrease Short-Window Ceasefire Probability

1. **War aims incompatibility**: When one side's stated war aim includes destroying the other side (e.g., Israel's aim to destroy Hamas), a ceasefire would mean accepting mission failure. This is the single strongest veto on short-window ceasefire probability. See [[war-aims-incompatibility]].

2. **No prior ceasefire in current conflict phase**: If no ceasefire has occurred since the conflict began, the first one requires the longest negotiation timeline. Each subsequent ceasefire is faster (January 2025 took 15 months; October 2025 took 7 months after collapse).

3. **Active offensive preparation**: If one side is planning a major military operation (e.g., Israel's Rafah operation planned for March 2024), ceasefire probability drops because military momentum is toward escalation, not de-escalation.

4. **Mediator leverage insufficient**: Does the lead mediator have real leverage over both parties? Qatar has influence with Hamas (hosts political leadership) but limited leverage on IDF operations. The US has leverage with Israel but limited direct access to Hamas.

5. **Domestic political constraints**: A leader facing a fragile coalition or rightward flank pressure cannot appear "weak" by agreeing to a ceasefire without achieving stated war aims. Netanyahu's coalition in Jan-Feb 2024 included Itamar Ben-Gvir and Bezalel Smotrich — both opposed any ceasefire.

6. **Asymmetric incentives**: In state-vs-non-state conflicts, the non-state actor (Hamas) has an attrition-based strategy: prolonging the war increases international pressure on the state and creates legitimacy costs. The state has a deadline-based strategy: achieve military objectives before domestic and international patience erodes. These asymmetric timelines mean the weaker party often prefers delay, which blocks quick ceasefires.

## The "Temporary Pause" Exception

The base rates above apply to **ceasefires as defined in standard resolution criteria** (publicly announced, mutually agreed halt in military engagement). However, **temporary humanitarian pauses** (4-7 day ceasefire for hostage/prisoner exchanges) follow different dynamics and can occur much sooner than enduring ceasefires:

| Factor | Temporary Pause | Enduring Ceasefire |
|--------|-----------------|---------------------|
| Negotiation timeline | Days to weeks | Months to years |
| War aims compatibility | Not required (pause ≠ end of war) | Required (ceasefire implies at least tacit acceptance) |
| Mediation complexity | Low (single issue: hostages for prisoners) | High (withdrawal, governance, reconstruction) |
| Typical window | 2-8 weeks into conflict | 6-24 months into conflict |

**Forecasting rule**: For any "ceasefire by date X?" question, check whether a humanitarian pause or hostage deal would satisfy the resolution criteria. If yes, the probability is significantly higher than for an enduring ceasefire. The November 2023 pause (announced Nov 22, 4-day duration) is the canonical example.

## Application for Jan-Feb 2024 Israel-Hamas Question

At the Jan 27, 2024 cutoff, for a ceasefire by Feb 29 (33-day window):

| Factor | Assessment | Impact on P |
|--------|-----------|-------------|
| Prior ceasefire in this conflict? | Yes — Nov 22-30, 2023 pause | ↑ (precedent exists) |
| War aims compatible with ceasefire? | No — Israel aimed to destroy Hamas | ↓↓ (strongest single blocker) |
| Pre-existing negotiation framework? | Yes — Qatar/Egypt/US mediating hostage deal | ↑ |
| Leadership decapitation? | No — Haniyeh and Sinwar still alive | ↔ (no change) |
| Political deadline? | None in window (US election far off) | ↔ |
| Superpower imposition? | No — US containing, not imposing | ↔ |
| Active offensive preparation? | Yes — Israel planning Rafah operation for March | ↓ |
| Mediator leverage on both sides? | Limited — US leverage on Israel, Qatar on Hamas, neither on both | ↓ |
| Domestic constraint on Israel? | Yes — far-right coalition partners oppose ceasefire | ↓ |
| Asymmetric incentive (Hamas delay)? | Yes — Hamas seeks attrition advantage | ↓ |
| Temporary pause could satisfy? | No — Nov pause expired, no new hostage deal close | ↔ |

**Calibrated P(ceasefire by Feb 29, 2024): ~0.02-0.05**

The single dominant factor is war aims incompatibility: Israel could not accept a ceasefire that left Hamas standing while its stated war aim was destroying Hamas. Even if a temporary hostage deal were theoretically possible, the Nov pause had collapsed with mutual recriminations, and Israel was preparing the Rafah offensive — the opposite direction from de-escalation.

A correct NO prediction reflects the structural impossibility of a ceasefire given incompatible war aims in a 33-day window without a major shift in either side's political calculus.

## When This Framework Fails

**CRITICAL: This framework applies ONLY to Pathway A (diplomatic) ceasefires.** For Pathway B (war-termination via superpower entry), the base rates below are irrelevant. The escalation-bargaining pattern can produce a ceasefire within 48 hours regardless of diplomatic pressure levels, war aims compatibility, or mutual consent.

Before applying any short-window base rate from this concept, first classify the ceasefire pathway using [[concepts/ceasefire-pathway-decomposition]]:

- If Pathway B (war-termination): **Do not use this concept.** Use [[concepts/escalation-bargaining-termination]] and [[procedures/state-on-state-ceasefire-decomposition]] instead. The base rates here are 10-100x too low for Pathway B.
- If Pathway A (diplomatic): This concept's base rates apply as documented below.
- If Pathway C (none likely): Both this concept and the escalation-bargaining concept predict NO.

**Specific cases where this framework fails:**
- **Escalation-bargaining pattern**: When a superpower enters combat, ceasefire can be imposed in hours, not weeks. This mechanism bypasses the mutual-consent penalty entirely.
- **Humanitarian catastrophe as forcing function**: Famine confirmation or genocide finding can create sudden diplomatic pressure that compresses ceasefire timelines from months to days.
- **Complete military defeat of one side**: If a party ceases to exist as an organized military force, the war ends by capitulation, not negotiation. The mutual-consent framework does not apply.
