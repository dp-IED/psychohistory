## Summary

Permanent international tribunal established by the Rome Statute (2002) to prosecute individuals for genocide, war crimes, and crimes against humanity. Issued arrest warrants for [[entities/benjamin-netanyahu]] and [[entities/yoav-gallant]] on November 21, 2024, for alleged war crimes in Gaza — a key diplomatic pressure vector in the Gaza ceasefire dynamics.

## Significance for Forecasting

ICC arrest warrants against leaders of a US-allied state are a relatively rare event that signals:
- Erosion of the post-WWII norm of sovereign immunity for state leaders
- Increased diplomatic cost of continued military operations, even if the warrants have limited enforcement mechanisms (Israel is not an ICC member; Netanyahu traveled to ICC member states less freely)
- A compounding factor in [[concepts/diplomatic-pressure-tipping-point]] — each new legal/judicial finding adds to the cumulative pressure on a belligerent
- The ICC's limited enforcement capacity means warrants primarily affect leaders' travel freedom and diplomatic standing, not immediate prosecution risk

### Case B: Rodrigo Duterte (2025)

In March 2025, the ICC executed its first arrest warrant against a former head of state from an Asian democracy. Rodrigo Duterte (Philippines, 2016-2022) was arrested on March 11, 2025 at Manila airport on ICC warrant for crimes against humanity committed during his war on drugs (2016-2022). The arrest was notable because:

- The Duterte administration had withdrawn the Philippines from the Rome Statute in 2019 (effective March 2019), but the ICC asserted jurisdiction over crimes committed while the Philippines was still a state party (2011-2019).
- The current Philippine government (Marcos Jr.) cooperated with the arrest, facilitating Duterte's transfer to The Hague.
- This established a new precedent: ICC withdrawal does not shield former leaders from prosecution for pre-withdrawal crimes when a successor government cooperates.

### ICC Pretrial Custody Dynamics

A key structural feature for forecasting: ICC pretrial detention is governed by Article 60 of the Rome Statute. For crimes against humanity charges:

- **Base rate of release before confirmation of charges**: <5% since the court's establishment (2002)
- **Time from arrest to initial appearance**: 3-7 days
- **Time from arrest to confirmation of charges hearing**: typically 60-90 days
- **Release criteria**: flight risk, obstruction risk, and seriousness of charges create a presumption against release for Tier 1 charges
- **Former head of state status**: Increases assessed flight risk (resources, international connections)

This makes any "released from ICC custody" question with a deadline <30 days post-arrest a structural P(YES) < 0.05 forecast (see [[domains/global/concepts/icc-pretrial-custody-duration/_concept]]).
