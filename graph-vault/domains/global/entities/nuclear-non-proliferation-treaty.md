## Summary

The Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT) is the cornerstone of the global nuclear non-proliferation regime. Opened for signature in 1968, entered into force in 1970, extended indefinitely in 1995. The NPT has 191 state parties — the most widely adhered-to arms control agreement in history. Only India, Israel, Pakistan, and South Sudan are non-parties (North Korea withdrew in 2003).

The NPT establishes three "pillars":
1. **Non-proliferation**: Non-nuclear-weapon states agree not to acquire nuclear weapons
2. **Disarmament**: Nuclear-weapon states (US, Russia, UK, France, China — the P5) commit to pursue disarmament
3. **Peaceful uses**: All parties have the right to develop nuclear energy for peaceful purposes

The treaty designates five Nuclear Weapon States (NWS): those that tested before 1967. India, Pakistan, Israel, and North Korea possess nuclear weapons outside the NPT framework.

## Significance for Forecasting

The NPT provides the foundational structural barrier against nuclear proliferation and use. Its forecasting relevance:
- **Treaty compliance dynamics** are leading indicators for nuclear breakout scenarios (Iran, North Korea)
- **NPT Review Conferences** (held every 5 years) serve as diplomatic pressure points where proliferation disputes are surfaced
- The treaty's **Article X withdrawal clause** is the legal mechanism through which a state can exit before acquiring nuclear weapons (North Korea invoked it in 2003)
- A state's **IAEA safeguards agreement** (required by the NPT) determines the inspection regime that detects diversion of nuclear materials
