## Summary

Hamas (Harakat al-Muqawama al-Islamiya — Islamic Resistance Movement) is a Palestinian Sunni Islamist militant and political organization founded in 1987 during the First Intifada. It has governed the Gaza Strip since 2007, when it took control after winning the 2006 Palestinian legislative election and defeating Fatah forces in the Battle of Gaza. Hamas is designated as a terrorist organization by the US, EU, UK, Israel, Japan, Canada, Egypt, and several other nations.

The group's military wing, the Izz ad-Din al-Qassam Brigades, launched the October 7, 2023 attack on Israel, killing approximately 1,200 people and taking over 250 hostages — the deadliest attack in Israeli history and the trigger for the 15-month Gaza War. Hamas political leadership has been based in Doha, Qatar, since 2012, while military leadership operated within Gaza.

## Significance for Forecasting

Hamas is the central non-state actor in the Gaza conflict and any question about Israel-Hamas ceasefires. Understanding Hamas's organizational structure (political bureau vs military wing, Doha-based vs Gaza-based leadership) is essential for forecasting ceasefire timelines: the political leadership in Doha has typically been more willing to negotiate than the military leadership in Gaza. Leadership decapitation (Haniyeh killed July 2024, Sinwar killed October 2024) shifted the organization's posture over time, with each successor facing different internal and external constraints.

## Key Dynamics for Ceasefire Forecasting

1. **Political vs military leadership split**: Political leaders (Haniyeh, later Haniyeh's successor) were based in Doha and more engaged in diplomatic channels. Military leaders (Sinwar, Deif) were in Gaza tunnel networks and more focused on military outcomes. The assassination of political leaders could create negotiation openings; the assassination of military leaders could harden resistance.

2. **Leadership decapitation tends to shift posture toward pragmatism**: After Haniyeh's assassination (July 2024), Sinwar took over both political and military leadership — consolidating power but also inheriting the negotiation portfolio. After Sinwar's death (October 2024), the remaining leadership showed more willingness to accept ceasefire terms. This pattern — successor regimes after decapitation becoming more pragmatic — is a key forecasting input.

3. **External patron dynamics**: Hamas's primary external patrons (Iran, Qatar, Turkey) had varying degrees of influence and different agendas. Qatar's role as mediator and host of political leadership made it the key diplomatic channel. Iran's support was essential for military rebuilding but less relevant for political decisions about ceasefire terms.

4. **Attrition strategy in early war phase (Nov 2023 - Jan 2024)**: During the first 3-4 months of the war, Hamas's strategy was to **prolong the conflict** — believing that international pressure on Israel would grow with each month of combat, eventually forcing an Israeli withdrawal without Hamas having to concede on hostage releases or political terms. This made Hamas an unwilling negotiation partner during this period. Any ceasefire before March 2024 would have required Hamas to abandon its attrition strategy while its leadership (Sinwar) was still alive and in command — something it had no incentive to do. The November 2023 humanitarian pause was accepted because it involved tangible hostage-for-prisoner gains, not because Hamas was ready for an enduring ceasefire.

5. **Hostage leverage as a double-edged sword**: The hostages (~250 taken on Oct 7) were Hamas's single most valuable bargaining chip. Hamas would not release them without major concessions from Israel (IDF withdrawal, end of war). But holding them also prevented Hamas from negotiating a ceasefire — Israel would not accept a ceasefire that did not include hostage release, and Hamas would not release hostages without a ceasefire. This **mutual hostage-ceasefire deadlock** paralyzed negotiations from November 2023 through mid-2024.

6. **Sinwar's command control in the tunnels**: Yahya Sinwar, the military leader in Gaza, maintained operational control throughout the early war period. Unlike the Doha-based political leadership (Haniyeh), Sinwar had no incentive to compromise — he was in the tunnel network, had not been targeted for assassination at that point, and believed time was on Hamas's side. The split between Doha's desire to negotiate and Gaza's commitment to fight was a structural brake on any ceasefire in the first year of the war.

## Ceasefire Negotiation Stalemate (Nov 2023 - Jan 2025)

The period between the November 2023 humanitarian pause and the January 2025 ceasefire (14 months) was characterized by ongoing negotiation failures. Understanding this stalemate is essential for forecasting ceasefire timing:

**Core sticking points (persistent throughout 2024):**
- **Hostage release mechanism**: Hamas demanded phased release tied to IDF withdrawal; Israel demanded all hostages released before any withdrawal.
- **IDF withdrawal scope**: Hamas demanded complete withdrawal; Israel refused to leave Gaza with Hamas still standing.
- **End-of-war guarantees**: Hamas demanded guarantees that the war would not resume; Israel refused to commit to ending the war without destroying Hamas.
- **Prisoner exchange ratio**: Hamas demanded large-scale Palestinian prisoner releases; Israel limited the number and categories (refused to release high-value prisoners).

**Why no ceasefire was possible in Jan-Feb 2024 (the question window):**
- Sinwar was alive and in command — no decapitation window had opened
- Israel's war aim was "destroy Hamas" — incompatible with any ceasefire that left Hamas standing
- The November pause had collapsed with mutual recriminations — no trust remained
- Israel was planning the Rafah offensive (escalation, not de-escalation)
- Hamas's attrition strategy gave it a timeline advantage — it believed waiting was winning
- Mediation triad (Qatar, Egypt, US) had structural leverage gaps — no single mediator could pressure both sides

**Timeline**
- **1987**: Founded during First Intifada
- **2006**: Won Palestinian legislative election
- **2007**: Took control of Gaza after Battle of Gaza
- **October 7, 2023**: Al-Aqsa Flood attack on Israel — ~1,200 killed, ~250 hostages taken
- **November 22-30, 2023**: First humanitarian ceasefire (4-day pause, extended to 7). 105 hostages exchanged for 240 prisoners. Fighting resumed Nov 30.
- **Nov 2023 - Jan 2024**: Negotiation stalemate — core gaps on hostage release, IDF withdrawal, end-of-war guarantees unresolved. Qatar/Egypt/US mediation active but insufficient.
- **Jan-Feb 2024**: Israel planning Rafah offensive. No ceasefire structurally possible due to war aims incompatibility, Sinwar's intact leadership, and mutual hostage-ceasefire deadlock.
- **June-July 2024**: Ceasefire talks intensify but fail. US-backed proposal (3-phase deal) announced May 31 — Hamas demands written guarantees; Israel demands changes. Talks collapse by August.
- **July 31, 2024**: Ismail Haniyeh (political bureau chairman) assassinated in Tehran
- **August 6, 2024**: Yahya Sinwar appointed new political bureau chairman
- **October 16, 2024**: Yahya Sinwar killed in Rafah, Gaza
- **January 15, 2025**: Agreed to ceasefire with Israel (took effect January 19)
- **March 18, 2025**: Ceasefire collapsed; Israel resumed airstrikes
- **October 3, 2025**: Agreed to parts of Trump's 20-point peace plan
- **October 8, 2025**: Ceasefire announced (took effect October 10)
