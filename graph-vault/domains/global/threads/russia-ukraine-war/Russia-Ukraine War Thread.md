# Russia-Ukraine War

## Overview

Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine began on February 24, 2022 — the largest conventional military conflict in Europe since 1945. What was initially anticipated by many observers as a rapid Russian victory has, by the end of 2022-Q3, transformed into a protracted war of attrition. Russian forces failed to capture Kyiv in the opening weeks, withdrew from northern Ukraine in April, and shifted to a concentrated offensive in the Donbas. By July, Russia had captured the entirety of Luhansk Oblast after weeks of heavy artillery bombardment. However, a Ukrainian counteroffensive in September 2022 recaptured thousands of square kilometers in Kharkiv Oblast, demonstrating that the war was far from decided.

As of September 30, 2022, the conflict encompasses occupied territories in four Ukrainian oblasts that Russia has formally (and illegally) annexed, a partial mobilization of Russian reservists, a European energy crisis driven by Russian gas-supply weaponization, and a fundamental restructuring of European security architecture (Finland and Sweden applying for NATO membership, EU candidate status for Ukraine).

## Timeline

### Phase 0: Pre-War Buildup and Failed Diplomacy (Jan-Feb 2022)

- **2022-01-10 to 2022-01-14**: US-Russia security talks in Geneva, NATO-Russia Council, and OSCE meetings all fail to resolve Russia's demand for NATO non-expansion guarantees. [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-01-17**: Russia begins joint military exercises in Belarus, massing troops on Ukraine's northern border. [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-01-19**: Biden authorizes $200M in security assistance to Ukraine. [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-01-24**: NATO places forces on standby; US evacuates embassy families from Kyiv. [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-01-28**: Biden warns invasion is likely. US/UK begin declassifying intelligence to preempt Russian pretexts. [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-02-04**: Putin attends Beijing Olympics, issues joint statement with Xi Jinping opposing NATO expansion. [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-02-11**: Biden warns allies that Russia has enough forces to invade "any day." US evacuates most embassy staff. [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-02-15**: Russia claims partial withdrawal; US/UK say no withdrawal occurred. [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-02-21**: Putin formally recognizes DNR/LNR and orders "peacekeeping" forces into Donbas. Initial US/EU/UK sanctions follow. [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-02-22**: Federation Council authorizes military force abroad. Ukraine declares state of emergency. Germany halts Nord Stream 2 certification. [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-02-23**: Cyberattacks hit Ukrainian government sites. UN Security Council emergency session. [[2022-Q1]]

### Phase 1: Full-Scale Invasion & Russian Reversals (Feb-Nov 2022)

- **2022-02-24**: Russia launches a full-scale invasion along three axes — northern (advancing on Kyiv via Belarus/Chernobyl), eastern (Donbas toward Kharkiv), and southern (Crimea toward Kherson and Mariupol). Initial strikes hit military infrastructure across Ukraine. Battle of [[hostomel-airport]]: Russian VDV paratroopers attempt to capture Antonov Airport as an airbridge; fiercely contested. [[snake-island]]: Ukrainian border guards reply "Russian warship, go f*** yourself" to surrender demand. [[chernobyl-exclusion-zone]] captured by Russian forces. Zelenskyy imposes martial law, refuses US evacuation offer: "I need ammunition, not a ride." [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-02-25 to 2022-02-27**: Russian forces reach Kyiv's northern suburbs (Obolon). Street fighting erupts in Kyiv. Putin puts nuclear forces on high alert (Feb 27). US/EU/UK agree to block select Russian banks from SWIFT and freeze Russian Central Bank assets (Feb 26). EU funds weapons deliveries to Ukraine for the first time. [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-02-28**: First Russia-Ukraine talks in Gomel, Belarus. No breakthrough; further rounds agreed. Russian forces lay siege to Kherson and Berdyansk; Mariupol cut off from water/power. UN reports 500,000+ refugees. [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-03-02**: Kherson falls — first major Ukrainian city captured. UN General Assembly votes 141-5 demanding withdrawal. [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-03-03**: Zaporizhzhia Nuclear Power Plant shelled and seized by Russian forces. Second round of talks agrees to humanitarian corridors (largely non-functional). [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-03-08**: US bans Russian oil and gas imports. EU announces REPowerEU plan to cut Russian gas dependency. Western corporate exodus begins (McDonald's, Coca-Cola, Starbucks suspend Russian operations). [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-03-09**: Russian airstrike destroys Mariupol maternity hospital — international outrage. [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-03-13**: Russia strikes Yavoriv military base near Polish border (35 killed). [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-03-16**: Russian airstrike destroys Mariupol Drama Theatre where hundreds of civilians sheltered. ICJ orders Russia to suspend operations. [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-03-24**: NATO Extraordinary Summit in Brussels announces new Eastern flank battle groups. NATO estimates 7,000-15,000 Russian soldiers killed. [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-03-28 to 2022-03-29**: Istanbul peace talks: Ukraine proposes neutral/non-aligned status with international security guarantees. Russia promises to "drastically reduce military activity" around Kyiv and Chernihiv. [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-03-31**: Putin signs decree requiring "unfriendly" countries to pay for Russian gas in rubles starting April 1, threatening supply cutoffs. [[2022-Q1]]
- **2022-04-01 to 2022-04-03**: Russia withdraws from the Kyiv region; mass graves and evidence of summary executions discovered in Bucha — accusations of genocide follow. [[2022-Q2]]
- **2022-04-14**: Ukrainian missiles sink the [[moskva]], Russia's Black Sea Fleet flagship — the largest warship lost in combat since World War II. [[2022-Q2]]
- **2022-04-18**: Russia shifts to a concentrated ground campaign in the Donbas — the war's second phase. [[2022-Q2]]
- **2022-05-20**: Surrender of the [[azovstal]] steel plant in [[mariupol]] after an 82-day siege; city is 90% destroyed. [[2022-Q2]]
- **2022-05-18**: Sweden and Finland formally apply to join NATO, ending decades of neutrality. [[2022-Q2]]
- **2022-06-23**: EU grants Ukraine official candidate status. [[2022-Q2]]
- **2022-06-24**: Ukrainian forces withdraw from [[severodonetsk]] after weeks of Russian artillery bombardment — the heaviest concentration of firepower in the war to that point. [[2022-Q2]]

### Phase 2: Russian Capture of Luhansk and HIMARS Campaign (Jul-Aug 2022)

- **2022-07-03**: Ukrainian forces withdraw from [[lysychansk]], the last Ukrainian-held city in [[luhansk-oblast]]. Russia completes its capture of the entire Luhansk region. The battles for Severodonetsk and Lysychansk cost Russia enormous quantities of ammunition and personnel through "scorched earth" tactics. [[2022-Q3]]
- **2022-07-10**: Ukraine's [[himars]] campaign begins in earnest. US-supplied M142 HIMARS systems strike the [[nova-kakhovka]] ammunition depot deep behind Russian lines in occupied [[kherson-oblast]], destroying a massive stockpile. This marks a turning point: Ukraine can now strike Russian command posts, ammunition depots, and supply nodes far from the front line. [[2022-Q3]]
- **2022-07-18 to 2022-07-31**: Repeated HIMARS strikes target the [[antonivskyi-bridge]] in Kherson city, rendering it unusable and isolating Russian forces on the west bank of the [[dnipro-river]]. Additional strikes hit railway bridges, severing Russian supply lines into the Kherson pocket. [[2022-Q3]]
- **2022-08-05 to 2022-08-07**: Shelling around the [[zaporizhzhia-nuclear-power-plant]] — Europe's largest, under Russian occupation since March — damages buildings and monitoring systems. Ukraine and Russia trade blame. The crisis raises international fears of a nuclear disaster. [[2022-Q3]]
- **2022-08-11**: [[rosatom]], Russia's state nuclear corporation, formally takes over management of the Zaporizhzhia NPP. [[2022-Q3]]
- **2022-08-25**: For the first time in the plant's history, the Zaporizhzhia NPP is fully disconnected from Ukraine's power grid after shelling damages a coal-ash pond near a power line. Emergency diesel generators maintain cooling functions. [[2022-Q3]]
- **2022-08-26**: IAEA Director-General [[rafael-grossi]] announces the agency will lead a mission to the plant. [[2022-Q3]]

### Phase 3: Kharkiv Counteroffensive and Russian Escalation (Sep 2022)

- **2022-09-01**: An IAEA team arrives at the Zaporizhzhia NPP for a four-day inspection. They document sustained shelling and damage but judge the immediate nuclear risk as not critical. Two inspectors remain as a permanent presence. [[2022-Q3]]
- **2022-09-06 to 2022-09-08**: Ukraine launches a surprise counteroffensive in [[kharkiv-oblast]], striking the weakest sector of the Russian front line. [[balakliya]] is liberated on September 8, collapsing the Russian front in the northeast. [[2022-Q3]]
- **2022-09-10**: Ukrainian forces liberate [[kupiansk]] and [[izyum]] — critical railway hubs for Russian supply lines. Commander-in-Chief [[valeriy-zaluzhnyi]] announces Ukrainian forces have reached the international border at the [[hoptivka]] checkpoint. Russian forces flee in disorder, abandoning vast quantities of equipment. The front line shifts over 100km in less than a week. [[2022-Q3]]
- **2022-09-12**: Russia's Defense Ministry acknowledges the loss of Balakliya and Izyum, framing it as a planned "regrouping" — independent analysts describe it as a rout. [[2022-Q3]]
- **2022-09-15 to 2022-09-20**: Fighting intensifies around [[lyman]] in Donetsk Oblast as Ukraine pushes eastward. Russian forces attempt to stabilize a new defensive line along the [[oskil-river]]. [[2022-Q3]]
- **2022-09-21**: President [[vladimir-putin]] announces a "partial mobilization" of 300,000 reservists — Russia's first mobilization since World War II. The announcement triggers protests across Russia (over 1,000 arrests on day one) and a mass exodus of military-age men to Kazakhstan, Georgia, Finland, and Mongolia. [[2022-Q3]]
- **2022-09-23 to 2022-09-27**: Russian-organized sham referendums on joining the Russian Federation are conducted at gunpoint in [[donetsk-oblast]], [[luhansk-oblast]], [[zaporizhzhia-oblast]], and [[kherson-oblast]]. The votes are universally condemned as illegal by the UN, G7, EU, and NATO. Official results report >90% in favor in each region. [[2022-Q3]]
- **2022-09-30**: Putin signs "accession treaties" formally annexing the four Ukrainian territories in a ceremony at St. George's Hall in the [[moscow-kremlin]]. The UN Security Council condemns the annexation (resolution vetoed by Russia). Ukraine's President [[volodymyr-zelenskyy]] responds by formally applying for accelerated NATO membership. [[2022-Q3]]

## Key Dynamics

### 1. The Strategic Shift: From Russian Offensive to Ukrainian Counteroffensive

Q3 2022 marks the most consequential strategic inflection point of the war to date. Through the first half of 2022, Russia held the operational initiative — capturing territory in Donbas, consolidating control of the southern coast, and laying siege to Mariupol. The Kharkiv counteroffensive (September 6-10) shattered that narrative. Ukraine demonstrated an ability to conduct operational-level maneuver warfare — assembling concealed forces, striking at a weak point, and rapidly exploiting a breach — that the Russian military had been unable to achieve since the invasion began. The collapse of Russian defenses in Kharkiv Oblast raised fundamental questions about the viability of Russia's entire occupation project.

### 2. HIMARS as a Tactical Revolution

The introduction of US-supplied HIMARS systems was the enabling condition for the Kharkiv breakthrough. By systematically targeting Russian ammunition depots, command posts, and supply nodes — and by rendering the Antonivskyi Bridge unusable — Ukraine degraded Russia's ability to sustain frontline operations. The HIMARS campaign demonstrated that precision-strike capabilities, even in limited numbers, could have an outsized operational effect against a force dependent on mass artillery and centralized logistics. This validated the theory that Western weapons supply was not merely palliative but structurally transformative for Ukraine's battlefield capabilities.

### 3. Mobilization and Annexation: Escalation from Weakness

Putin's twin responses to the Kharkiv defeat — partial mobilization and formal annexation of four oblasts — represent a distinctive pattern: escalation from a position of weakness rather than strength. The mobilization of 300,000 reservists acknowledges that Russia's professional military is insufficient to hold captured territory against Ukrainian counterattacks. The annexation — conducted through sham referendums universally condemned as illegal — appears designed to create a narrative framework for potential escalation (framing attacks on "Russian territory" as attacks on Russia itself) rather than reflecting genuine territorial control. The mass exodus of military-age men from Russia and the domestic protest response indicate significant social costs to this escalation.

### 4. The Nuclear Plant Crisis and European Energy War

The Zaporizhzhia NPP crisis introduced a new dimension of risk: the prospect of a radiological disaster in an active war zone. While the IAEA judged imminent risk as manageable, the plant's disconnection from the grid and Russian operational control created enduring uncertainty. Simultaneously, Russia's weaponization of natural gas supplies — the Nord Stream 1 indefinite shutdown followed by the September 26 pipeline sabotage — demonstrated that energy infrastructure had become a primary battlefield for economic warfare. These twin crises — nuclear safety and energy security — directly link the war to European domestic politics in ways that sustain Western support but also create political vulnerabilities as winter approaches.

### 5. The Diplomatic Landscape Frozen

The collapse of the Istanbul peace talks in April 2022 effectively closed the window for negotiated settlement. Ukraine's success on the battlefield reduces any domestic political incentive for territorial concessions. Russia's annexation (even if unrecognized) creates a legal and political barrier to negotiation — Putin cannot be seen to surrender territory he has formally absorbed into Russia. Neither side has a viable off-ramp. The war is structurally locked into a trajectory of protracted conflict, with future outcomes determined primarily by battlefield attrition and the sustainability of Western arms supplies.

## Forecasting Significance

This thread enables prediction of:

- **Duration and trajectory of the war**: The structural barriers to negotiation — mutual maximalism, existential framing, no credible mediator, no mutually hurting stalemate — suggest the war will continue well beyond 2022 with no near-term ceasefire or settlement. The question is not whether the war ends but what form of attrition or frozen conflict emerges.

- **Western military aid sustainability**: Ukrainian battlefield success is directly correlated with Western weapons deliveries. Future Ukrainian breakthroughs or collapses are a function of NATO political will, not endogenous Ukrainian military capacity. Leading indicators: US Congressional aid votes, European defense production capacity, and domestic political support for continued Ukraine funding.

- **Russian regime stability**: The partial mobilization — the first since WWII — and the evident military failures create domestic political risk for Putin. Mass emigration, protest activity, and elite discontent are measurable signals. Whether the regime can absorb these shocks without fracturing is a central forecasting question.

- **NATO expansion and European security architecture**: Finland and Sweden's NATO accession process — triggered by the invasion — permanently alters European security geography. The war's outcome will shape whether this represents a temporary spike or a permanent restructuring of the continent's defense posture.

- **Global spillover effects**: The war's impact on energy prices, food security (especially via Black Sea grain exports), inflation, and alliance cohesion (G7, EU, NATO) makes it a structural driver of macroeconomic and geopolitical forecasting across multiple domains.
