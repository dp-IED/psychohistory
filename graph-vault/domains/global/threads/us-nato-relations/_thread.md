# US-NATO Relations in the Trump Era

## Overview

The US-NATO relationship has undergone a structural transformation during Trump's two presidencies (2017-2021, 2025-present). From Trump's first-term demands for burden-sharing (2% GDP spending targets) through his transactional peace-broker second term, the alliance has experienced both heightened tension (threats of withdrawal, Article 4 invocations by frontline states) and demonstrated value (expedited NATO Article 5 invocation procedures, enhanced rapid-reaction deployments). The central forecasting question is whether the alliance can survive a sustained US presidency that treats it as a transactional arrangement rather than a foundational security commitment.

## Timeline / Key Events

### Phase 1: First-Term Pressure (2017-2021)
- **2017**: Trump publicly criticizes NATO allies for insufficient defense spending, calls the alliance "obsolete"
- **July 2018**: Trump threatens to withdraw from NATO at a summit, reportedly considering it multiple times
- **2019**: NATO defense spending among European allies begins increasing; 9 members reach 2% target by 2020
- **2020**: US reduces troop presence in Germany from 34,500 to 25,000

### Phase 2: Biden Restoration (2021-2025)
- **2021**: Biden administration reaffirms Article 5 commitment, rebuilds alliance relationships
- **2022**: Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine galvanizes NATO. Finland and Sweden apply for membership
- **2023**: Finland joins NATO (April); Sweden joins (March 2024)
- **2024**: NATO summit in Washington marks 75th anniversary. Alliance at 32 members

### Phase 3: Trump Second Term (2025-)
- **January 2025**: Trump inaugurated. European allies brace for policy shift
- **April 2, 2025**: Liberation Day tariffs imposed — included tariffs on EU goods, straining economic relationship alongside security relationship
- **June 2025**: Twelve-Day War (Iran-Israel). NATO not directly involved, but the US's unilateral military action without NATO consultation raises alliance coordination questions
- **August 8, 2025**: Armenia-Azerbaijan peace deal brokered at White House — demonstrates Trump's bilateral broker approach
- **August 15, 2025**: Alaska Summit (Trump-Putin meeting) — first direct US-Russia leader talks since 2022 invasion. European allies express concern about being excluded [[2025-Q3]]
- **September 9, 2025**: Russian drones breach Polish airspace during Ukraine attack. Poland invokes NATO Article 4 [[2025-Q3]]
- **September 19, 2025**: Three Russian MiG-31 jets violate Estonian airspace. Estonia also invokes Article 4 [[2025-Q3]]
- **September-October 2025**: Trump brokers Gaza ceasefire (October 8-10) — reinforces pattern of US acting as unilateral mediator rather than through alliance frameworks
- **2026**: Ongoing US-NATO tension over defense spending commitments, with some European allies accelerating defense spending increases while others lag

### Key Dynamics

1. **Transactional Brokerage vs Alliance Framework**: Trump's second-term foreign policy follows a consistent pattern of bilateral deal-making (Armenia-Azerbaijan, US-Russia Alaska summit, Gaza ceasefire) rather than multilateral alliance processes. This structural preference means even when US and NATO interests align (e.g., countering Russian aggression), the method differs — and the method matters for alliance cohesion.

2. **Article 4 as Warning Signal**: The dual Article 4 invocations in September 2025 (Poland and Estonia within 10 days) are unprecedented in frequency. While Article 4 is a consultation mechanism (not collective defense), its repeated invocation signals that frontline NATO states perceive existential threat even without formal Article 5 activation. This creates political pressure on the US to demonstrate commitment.

3. **The Alaska Summit Precedent**: Trump meeting Putin bilaterally before engaging NATO allies on Ukraine terms establishes a hierarchy the alliance cannot ignore. The summit's existence — regardless of its outcome — signals that the US will set the terms of Russia engagement unilaterally. This is a structural departure from the Biden-era "nothing about Ukraine without Ukraine" principle.

4. **Defense Spending as Leverage**: Trump's consistent demand for 2%+ GDP defense spending from allies creates a measurable compliance variable. Allies that reach or exceed 3% (Poland at ~4%, Baltic states at ~3%) gain political insulation from US withdrawal threats. Allies below 2% (Canada, Spain, Belgium, Italy) remain vulnerable to US pressure campaigns.

5. **NATO Withdrawal Mechanics**: US withdrawal from NATO requires either (a) 2/3 Senate approval of a withdrawal treaty or (b) an act of Congress terminating the North Atlantic Treaty. The Congressional bar is extremely high — the 1949 North Atlantic Treaty was ratified 82-13, and NATO enjoys broad bipartisan support. However, a president could functionally hollow out the alliance by refusing to fund US contributions, delaying deployments, or ignoring Article 5 requests — a "withdrawal by neglect" that would not require congressional approval.
