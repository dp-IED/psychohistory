## Definition

A structured framework for assessing the probability of a nuclear weapon detonation — whether through wartime use, accidental launch, terrorist acquisition, or nuclear testing. The framework establishes a baseline probability derived from historical frequency, then adjusts up or down based on the presence of specific leading indicators.

The core insight: nuclear weapon detonation is an **extremely low base-rate event** in any given time window, and forecasting it requires identifying specific mechanisms that would disrupt the powerful structural barriers against use. Most questions about nuclear detonation will correctly resolve NO, but the reasoning must demonstrate that the relevant mechanisms were checked.

## Historical Base Rate

### Wartime Use

| Era | Events | Frequency |
|-----|--------|-----------|
| 1945 | Hiroshima (Aug 6), Nagasaki (Aug 9) | 2 detonations, last use in warfare |
| 1945-present | No wartime use since Nagasaki | ~0/year for 78+ years |

**Structural barriers to wartime use:**
- **Nuclear taboo**: Since 1945, no state has used a nuclear weapon in armed conflict. This norm is reinforced by repeated US and Soviet/Russian non-use across multiple wars where they were losing (Korea 1950-53, Vietnam 1954-75, Afghanistan 1979-89, Ukraine 2022-ongoing).
- **Deterrence**: Any nuclear use against a nuclear-armed state risks immediate retaliation. Against a non-nuclear state, it risks complete international isolation, loss of alliance partners, and crippling sanctions.
- **Alliance constraints**: Nuclear-armed states leading alliances (US in NATO, Russia in CSTO, China with North Korea) face constraints from non-nuclear allies who would be endangered by fallout or escalation.
- **Command and control**: Military organizations are designed to prevent unauthorized nuclear use, not enable it. Any use requires deliberate authorization at the highest political level.

### Nuclear Testing

| Period | Testing rate | Notes |
|--------|-------------|-------|
| 1945-1992 | ~2,000 total tests (US 1,054, USSR 715, UK 45, France 210, China 45) | Peak in 1950s-1960s; ~50+ tests/year at peak |
| 1992-1996 | Declining sharply | Last US test: 1992. Last USSR/Russia test: 1990 (until 2025). Last UK test: 1991. Last France test: 1996. Last China test: 1996. |
| 1996-present (CTBT regime) | Very low | Only North Korea has tested (2006, 2009, 2013, Jan 2016, Sep 2016, 2017) — 6 tests total. Zero tests by any CTBT-signatory state. |

**Key date**: The Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT) opened for signature September 24, 1996. Though not yet in force (requires ratification by 8 specific Annex 2 states, of which the US, China, Iran, Israel, Egypt, and others have not ratified), it has established a **de facto moratorium** on nuclear testing. All P5 nuclear states (US, Russia, UK, France, China) observe the moratorium. Only North Korea has tested since 1996.

**Detection capability**: The CTBT's International Monitoring System (IMS) is capable of detecting any nuclear explosion over 1 kiloton anywhere in the world through seismic, hydroacoustic, infrasound, and radionuclide sensors. As of 2023, the IMS had 300+ operational stations out of a planned 337. **A clandestine nuclear test by a P5 state is effectively impossible** without detection and attribution.

### Accidental Launch / Unauthorized Detonation

| Category | Historical incidents | Frequency |
|----------|---------------------|-----------|
| Accidental launch (false alarm nearly triggered) | 2+ known near-misses (1983 Stanislav Petrov, 1995 Norwegian rocket incident, 1979 NORAD false alarm) | ~1 per decade serious enough to reach highest alert levels |
| Accidental detonation | 0 | No accidental nuclear detonation has ever occurred outside of deliberate testing or use |
| Weapons accident (broken arrow) | ~32 known "Broken Arrow" incidents (1950-2021) | Multiple, but NONE resulted in nuclear detonation (conventional explosives in warheads have detonated in accidents like Palomares 1966 and Thule 1968, but the nuclear cores did not produce fission yield) |

**Key structural fact**: No accidental nuclear detonation has ever occurred in the history of nuclear weapons. The safety mechanisms (one-point safety, insensitive high explosives, permissive action links) are designed to make accidental nuclear yield effectively impossible. Conventional explosives in warheads can detonate in accidents, but the nuclear chain reaction cannot initiate accidentally.

### Terrorist Nuclear Detonation

| Factor | Assessment |
|--------|-----------|
| Feasibility of constructing a gun-type device | Moderate — gun-type design (Little Boy) is mechanically simple but requires HEU |
| Feasibility of acquiring fissile material | Very low — HEU and plutonium are among the most heavily guarded materials on Earth |
| Known attempts | Al-Qaeda reportedly sought nuclear materials; no evidence they acquired weapons-grade fissile material |
| Historical frequency | 0 terrorist nuclear detonations in history |

## Forecasting Application

### Step 1: Identify the Detonation Type

The question asks about a "nuclear weapon detonat[ion]" — this covers all types. But the probability varies enormously by type:

| Type | Baseline probability (any 6-month window, 2023) | Structural assessment |
|------|-------------------------------------------------|-----------------------|
| Wartime use by nuclear-armed state | <0.1% | Taboo + deterrence + alliance constraints |
| Nuclear test by P5 state | <0.1% | CTBT moratorium + IMS detection + diplomatic cost |
| Nuclear test by North Korea | 5-15% in any year (~0.3-1% per month) | NK has tested in clusters; 2022-23 was a quiet period (no tests since 2017) |
| Nuclear test by a new proliferator (Iran) | <1% per year | IAEA monitoring; enrichment not yet at weaponization level in 2023 |
| Accidental detonation | <0.01% | Never happened; multiple safety layers |
| Terrorist detonation | <0.01% | No fissile material acquisition known |

### Step 2: Check Leading Indicators

For the affirmative case (YES), one or more of these leading indicators must be active:

1. **Explicit nuclear threat escalation ladder**: A state has moved nuclear forces to higher alert levels, dispersed weapons from storage, or explicitly threatened nuclear use with operational preparations (not just rhetorical threats). Example: Russia's nuclear alert during Ukraine invasion (Feb 27, 2022) was rhetorical/deterrent signaling, not actual use preparation.

2. **Preparations for nuclear testing**: Satellite imagery showing tunnel excavation, test stand construction, or instrumentation installation at a known test site. This is the most detectable leading indicator.

3. **Withdrawal from the CTBT**: A state formally announces intent to resume testing. This is a mandatory precursor for any P5 nuclear test — the political cost would require advance signaling.

4. **Breakdown of nuclear command and control**: Indicators of a military coup in a nuclear-armed state, loss of positive control over weapons, or unauthorized movement of nuclear materials. This is the most dangerous scenario.

5. **Credible terrorist acquisition of fissile material**: Specific intelligence indicating that a non-state actor has acquired weapons-grade uranium or plutonium. No known case in history.

### Step 3: Calibrate for the Question's Window

For a question like "Will a nuclear weapon detonate by [date]?":

1. **Start from base rate**: In any given 6-month window, the probability of any nuclear detonation (all types combined) is approximately 0.5-2%, driven almost entirely by the North Korean testing possibility. Remove North Korea (if they are in a quiet period, as 2018-2025 outside their 2017 test cluster), and the baseline drops to <0.1%.

2. **Check for active leading indicators**: Are any of the five leading indicators present? In most periods, none will be active. Document this explicitly.

3. **Check the specific window's nuclear-related events**: Some nuclear posture changes (New START suspension, nuclear weapons sharing agreements) are saber-rattling, not preparation for use. Distinguish between:
   - **Nuclear posture signaling** (threats to use, treaty withdrawals, deployment announcements) — adjusts baseline by 0.1-0.3x multiplier at most
   - **Use preparation** (alert level changes, weapon dispersal, test site preparation, fissile material movement) — adjusts baseline by 3-10x multiplier

4. **Apply the historical precedent check**: Has this type of event (nuclear detonation) occurred at this frequency before? If the answer is "never" (accidental detonation, terrorist detonation) or "not since 1945" (wartime use) or "not since 1996 by a P5 state" (testing), the baseline is near zero.

### Step 4: Document the Negative Case

An explicit "nothing happened" documentation is critical. The most common correct forecast for a nuclear detonation question is NO. The reasoning must state:

- "No wartime nuclear use has occurred since 1945 despite multiple losing wars by nuclear powers"
- "No P5 nuclear test has occurred since 1996"
- "No active test preparation indicators were present during this window"
- "No breakdown of nuclear command and control occurred during this window"
- "No credible terrorist fissile material acquisition was reported"

This explicit documentation prevents the "I don't know" error and forces verification that the base rate assessment is correct for the specific window.
