# Assassination as Political Pivot

## Definition

A single political assassination that alters the trajectory of a nation by removing a sitting leader and replacing them with someone who pursues fundamentally different policies. Distinct from assassinations that merely change personnel within the same political framework, a pivot assassination produces a break in policy direction — often the opposite of what the assassin intended.

## Canonical Examples

### McKinley-Roosevelt (1901)
The assassination of [[William McKinley]] elevated [[Theodore Roosevelt]], a progressive reformer. McKinley was a cautious conservative; Roosevelt was an activist who transformed the presidency, broke with the Republican machine, and pursued trust-busting, conservation, and an assertive foreign policy. This is the vault's primary example.

### Umberto I (1900)
The assassination of [[Umberto I of Italy]] by anarchist [[Gaetano Bresci]], who sought to avenge the [[Bava Beccaris massacre]], elevated the more conservative [[Victor Emmanuel III]]. The assassin's goal was revolutionary upheaval; the result was a more cautious monarch who nevertheless maintained the existing order.

## Pattern Archetype

1. A leader with a defined policy profile is assassinated
2. The successor — often the vice president, heir, or designated replacement — has a different profile (sometimes suppressed or concealed)
3. The resulting policy shift can be in the opposite direction from what the assassin intended
4. The regime survives (unlike in a coup), but its policy direction changes
5. The assassination creates a window of political opportunity for reform or reaction

## Forecasting Indicators

- A leader's successor has significantly different policy preferences that were suppressed during the leader's lifetime
- The leader is a "unifying" figure whose removal would fracture their coalition
- The assassination occurs during a period of elite dissatisfaction within the leader's own party

## Distinction from Other Assassination Patterns

- **Regime-change assassination** (e.g., Franz Ferdinand, 1914): triggers war or revolution, not just policy shift
- **Symbolic assassination** (e.g., Carnot, 1894): removes a generic representative of authority without changing policy direction
- **Decapitation assassination** (e.g., Caesar, 44 BCE): removes a leader whose personal authority held a system together
