# Leader Legal Disqualification — Candidate Substitution

## Definition

A pattern where a leading political candidate is legally disqualified from standing for office through judicial or administrative means, triggering the substitution of a party successor. The disqualification may be legitimate (prosecution for actual crimes) or politically motivated (authoritarian tool to remove opposition), and the substitution may be smooth (pre-designated successor) or chaotic (factional infighting).

## Key Dimensions

| Dimension | Range | Examples |
|-----------|-------|----------|
| **Disqualification basis** | Criminal conviction vs administrative ban vs constitutional ineligibility | Le Pen (criminal); Machado (administrative ban); Trump Jan 6 Section 3 cases (constitutional) |
| **Disqualification legitimacy** | Legitimate prosecution vs politically motivated | Le Pen (legitimate, well-documented EU funds case); Machado (politically motivated, Maduro regime targeting opposition frontrunner) |
| **Timing relative to election** | Years out vs months out vs weeks out | Le Pen (2 years before 2027 election); Machado (6 months before 2024 election) |
| **Substitution mechanism** | Designated successor vs primary/caucus vs factional competition | Bardella (designated — RN president, Le Pen's chosen successor); González (negotiated — unified opposition selection after Machado's ban) |
| **Electoral impact** | Fatal (party loses entirely) vs manageable (substitute competitive) | Le Pen→Bardella (manageable — Bardella leads polls); Machado→González (manageable — González won 67% vote share) vs less successful substitutions |

## Canonical Cases

### Case 1: Marine Le Pen → Jordan Bardella (France 2027)

| Parameter | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Disqualified | Marine Le Pen (RN), three-time presidential candidate |
| Date | March 31, 2025 — convicted of EU funds embezzlement |
| Penalty | 5 years ineligibility (immediate effect) + 4 years prison (suspended) |
| Appeals | Ruling on hold pending appeal (scheduled July 7, 2026) |
| Substitute | Jordan Bardella, RN president since November 2022 |
| Timing | ~2 years before April 2027 election |
| Impact | Bardella leads opinion polls at 30-35% first round; RN positioned for strongest-ever result |
| Uncertainty | If appeal overturns, Le Pen could re-enter — creating a candidate-surplus problem for RN |

**Structural insight**: The long runway (2 years before the election) makes this a low-disruption substitution. The party had time to position Bardella as Le Pen's successor, and the substitution mechanism was pre-established (Bardella was already RN president). This contrasts with short-notice substitutions where the party has no time to vet or position the replacement.

### Case 2: María Corina Machado → Edmundo González (Venezuela 2024)

| Parameter | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Disqualified | María Corina Machado, opposition frontrunner |
| Date | 2023-2024 — 15-year public office ban (existing ban from 2015 upheld June 2023) |
| Substitution | Edmundo González (former diplomat, compromise candidate) |
| Timing | ~6 months before July 2024 election |
| Impact | González won 67% in parallel vote tabulation; regime falsified results and retained power |
| Uncertainty | Substitution timing was tight; González was a placeholder with low name recognition but benefited from Machado's endorsement |

**Structural insight**: Short-notice substitution can still succeed if the disqualified candidate endorses and campaigns for the substitute. Machado's active campaigning for González was critical — she lent her organizational infrastructure and voter trust to a relatively unknown figure. Without endorsement, the substitution would likely have failed.

## Forecasting Application

When a leading candidate faces legal disqualification:

1. **Assess disqualification durability**: Is the ruling final or under appeal? What is the appeal timeline relative to the election? In authoritarian contexts, consider the regime's incentive to maintain the ban.

2. **Identify substitution mechanism**: Is there a pre-designated successor (party structure, constitutional line of succession) or will one emerge through competition? Pre-designated successors have higher substitution success rates.

3. **Evaluate substitute viability**: Does the substitute have name recognition, organizational backing, and the disqualified candidate's endorsement? Does the substitute face their own legal risks?

4. **Estimate electoral impact**: Will disqualification + substitution reduce the party's baseline vote share? The impact depends on runway length, substitute preparation, and whether the disqualification is seen as legitimate or as political persecution (persecution can increase sympathy/vote share).

5. **Calibrate for regime-context**: In authoritarian settings, disqualification is often the first move in a broader election-rigging strategy. The substitution may succeed at the ballot box but be nullified by post-election fraud.

## When NOT to Use

This concept applies to **leading candidates** (top 2-3 in credible polls) who are disqualified through a legal/administrative process. Do not use for:
- Candidates who voluntarily withdraw (retirement, scandal not involving legal disqualification)
- Party primary defeats (internal party competition, not disqualification)
- Incumbents who are term-limited (constitutional, not disqualification)
- Low-polling fringe candidates whose disqualification doesn't materially affect the race
