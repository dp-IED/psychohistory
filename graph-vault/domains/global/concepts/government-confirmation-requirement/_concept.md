# Government Confirmation Requirement

## Definition

A recurring structural dynamic in prediction-market forecasting where the resolution criteria for a question require an official government statement, admission, or confirmation — as distinct from the question asking whether an event merely EXISTS. Questions structured as "Will the government confirm that X is true?" have a systematically higher probability bar than "Is X true?" or "Did X happen?" This distinction is frequently missed by forecasters who treat the underlying event probability and the confirmation probability as identical.

## Core Principle

The probability that an event occurs AND the probability that a government confirms it are NOT the same distribution. Government confirmation requires:

1. **The event must actually occur** (necessary condition)
2. **The government must have knowledge of it** (investigative condition)
3. **The government must choose to publicly confirm it** (political condition)

The political condition is the binding constraint in most cases. Governments systematically refuse to confirm events that would:
- Admit vulnerability (e.g., "we lost track of radioactive material")
- Reveal capability gaps (e.g., "we cannot identify who is operating drones over our military bases")
- Create legal liability (e.g., "we failed to prevent X")
- Invite escalation pressure (e.g., "foreign drones are surveilling our nuclear facilities")
- Trigger panic (e.g., "unexplained drones are searching for nuclear material")
- Acknowledge a prior cover-up or error

## Types of Government Confirmation Questions

### Type A: Admission of Vulnerability or Failure
Examples: "Will the US government confirm that drones are searching for nuclear weapons?" "Will the government admit that sensitive material was lost?"

Probability profile: VERY LOW (<5%). Governments almost never admit vulnerability or failure unless forced by external circumstances (leaks, litigation, congressional investigation). The institutional incentive structure strongly favors denial, minimization, or silence.

### Type B: Attribution of Responsibility (Adversarial)
Examples: "Will the US government confirm that North Korea was behind the X attack?"

Probability profile: MODERATE (30-60%). Governments are more willing to confirm attributions that assign blame to adversaries, as this serves foreign policy and domestic political interests. However, they require sufficient evidence and intelligence consensus to make a public attribution credible.

### Type C: Technical Fact Confirmation
Examples: "Will the DoD confirm that a drone incursion occurred at Base X?"

Probability profile: MODERATE-HIGH (60-90%). For events that are operationally visible, not politically sensitive, and independently verifiable, governments will sometimes confirm them as part of routine information-sharing.

### Type D: Admission of Error or Wrongdoing
Examples: "Will the government confirm that it misled the public about X?"

Probability profile: VERY LOW (<5%). Requires external compulsion (court order, unanimous oversight committee pressure, incontrovertible leaked evidence). Voluntary admission of error is institutionally rare.

## The Nuclear Search Canonical Case (Q44)

The question "Mystery Drones searching for nuclear weapons?" was a clear Type A question:
- Resolution required the US federal government to CONFIRM the drones were searching for nuclear weapons
- This would require admitting: (1) radioactive material was lost, (2) standard tracking mechanisms failed, (3) the government needed drones to find it, (4) it couldn't publicly explain what was happening
- Multiple agencies explicitly denied the theory:
  - **NNSA**: Its emergency response unit did not use drones for radiological detection
  - **NJ DEP**: Drones were not used to locate the missing object
  - **FBI/DHS/DoD joint statement**: Nothing anomalous found in 5,000+ reports
  - **White House (Jan 2025)**: Drones were FAA-authorized research/hobbyist drones
- The correct NO forecast followed from recognizing this was a Type A government confirmation question, not a question about whether the theory existed or was plausible

## Pre-Forecast Audit for Government Confirmation Questions

When a question's resolution criteria include "confirm," "admit," "announce," "acknowledge," "officially state," or similar government-action language:

1. **Categorize the confirmation type** (Type A/B/C/D above)
2. **Identify the specific agency** whose confirmation would resolve the question — which cabinet department, agency, or official has the authority to make such a statement?
3. **Assess the institutional incentive**: Would confirming the event serve or harm the agency's interests? Would it create legal liability, political vulnerability, or operational risk?
4. **Check for pre-existing denials**: Has the agency already denied the claim? A prior denial raises the confirmation bar to near-impossible (the agency would have to admit it was wrong)
5. **Check for alternative resolution pathways**: Could the question resolve YES through a different mechanism (leaked documents, congressional testimony, whistleblower) that doesn't require the agency's voluntary confirmation?
6. **Apply default probability**: For Type A questions, default to p_yes < 0.10 unless there is specific evidence of external compulsion (lawsuits, subpoenas, unanimous bipartisan pressure, leak of irrefutable evidence)
