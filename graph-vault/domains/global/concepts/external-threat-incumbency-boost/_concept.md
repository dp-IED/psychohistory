# External Threat Incumbency Boost

## Definition

A pattern in which an external threat, pressure campaign, or hostile action by a foreign power or adversary toward a country **increases** the domestic electoral standing of the incumbent government or ruling party. The external threat functions as an electoral asset for the incumbent rather than a liability — it shifts issue salience toward national security, triggers rally-around-the-flag dynamics, and delegitimizes opposition critiques as unpatriotic or appeasing the adversary.

Key characteristics:
- External actor takes visible, escalatory action (military exercise, sanctions, hostile rhetoric, covert influence operation)
- Domestic election is within weeks to months of the escalatory action
- Incumbent's approval or polling improves after the escalatory action
- Opposition is perceived as weaker on national security or more conciliatory toward the adversary
- The effect is stronger when the external threat is acute/vivid rather than chronic/background

## Distinction from Rally-Around-the-Flag

The "rally-around-the-flag" effect (Mueller 1970) describes a temporary approval boost for a leader during an international crisis, regardless of electoral timing. External-Threat Incumbency Boost is distinct in two ways:

| Feature | Rally-Around-the-Flag | External-Threat Incumbency Boost |
|---------|----------------------|----------------------------------|
| Trigger | Any international crisis or conflict | Specifically an escalatory action by a pre-identified adversary |
| Effect | Temporary (weeks to months) | Can persist through an election cycle if the threat remains salient |
| Mechanism | National unity response | Partisan advantage: the threat specifically helps one side of the domestic political divide |
| Timeframe | Crisis duration | The electoral window around the threat's salience peak |
| Opposition effect | Opposition also shows support initially | Opposition is structurally disadvantaged because their policy stance is easier to frame as "weak" |

The key distinction: rally-around-the-flag is a **national unity** effect; external-threat incumbency boost is a **partisan polarization** effect. The former benefits all incumbents temporarily; the latter specifically helps the party perceived as more hawkish or nationalist.

## Canonical Examples

### Taiwan: PRC Pressure Boosts DPP

The most clean example for vault purposes. China's military and diplomatic pressure campaigns against Taiwan consistently boost the Democratic Progressive Party (DPP), which positions itself as the defender of Taiwanese sovereignty.

- **2020 election**: Tsai Ing-wen won a landslide (57.1%) — her highest margin ever — partly driven by the Hong Kong protests (July 2019 onward) and PLA saber-rattling ahead of the election. The KMT's perceived China-friendly stance became a liability.

- **2024 election**: Lai Ching-te won with 40.05% — less dramatic than 2020 but the DPP still won despite (and partly because of) PRC pressure signaling opposition to Lai. The "China threat effect" is documented in [[domains/east-asia/threads/taiwan-cross-strait-relations/_thread]] as a key dynamic.

**Conditional application**: The China threat effect does not guarantee a DPP win — it improves the DPP's mobilization relative to a no-pressure baseline. In 2024, the effect was weaker than in 2020 (Hong Kong protests were 18 months prior, not 6), but still operant. The DPP's win in 2024 was primarily driven by opposition fragmentation, with the threat effect as a secondary amplifier.

### Georgia: Russian Invasion Boosts Saakashvili

- **August 2008**: Russia invades Georgia over South Ossetia. The war lasts 5 days. Saakashvili's government is defeated militarily but the invasion triggers a massive nationalist response.

- **May 2008 (pre-invasion)**: Saakashvili's United National Movement wins re-election with 59% in the parliamentary elections — already strong.

- The invasion occurred 3 months after the election, so there was no electoral boost. However, polling showed Saakashvili's approval at 80%+ immediately after the war, and the nationalist framing persisted for years. **Modified example**: Russian pressure has historically helped Georgian nationalist parties over pro-Russian opposition.

### Israel: Rocket Attacks Boost Right-Wing Incumbents

- **2014 Gaza War**: Operation Protective Edge (July-August 2014) boosted Netanyahu's Likud ahead of the March 2015 election. Polling showed Likud gaining 3-5 seats during and immediately after the operation.

- **2019**: Escalations with Hamas in March-April 2019, two months before the April 2019 election, favored Netanyahu's security-focused campaign.

- **2023-present**: The October 7 attack triggered a short-term rally for Netanyahu (approval rose in October-November 2023) but this faded into long-term decline as the war became protracted — demonstrating the **limitation**: protracted/chronic threats can reverse the effect if the incumbent is seen as having failed in prevention.

### Pakistan: India Tensions Boost Military-Aligned Government

- **February 2019**: Pakistan-India border clashes after the Pulwama attack. PM Imran Khan's approval rose from ~40% to ~60%. The tension framed the opposition PML-N as soft on India.

- The effect was temporary — Khan's approval declined once the crisis faded and economic issues re-emerged. But the electoral benefit was real during the narrow window around the crisis.

### Counterexample: US 2020 (COVID-19)

A useful negative case. Trump attempted to frame COVID-19 as an "external Chinese threat" to boost his re-election. The framing failed because:
1. The threat was a pandemic, not a targeted adversary action
2. The US government's response was perceived as inadequate
3. The economic damage from the pandemic hurt the incumbent more than the threat frame helped
4. The threat was global and indiscriminate, not a targeted adversary action that the incumbent could claim to stand against

This counterexample establishes the boundary: the effect requires a **targeted adversary action** that the incumbent can credibly claim to be the defender against.

## Mechanisms

### 1. Issue Salience Shift
External threats increase the salience of national security/foreign policy issues. When these issues rise in voter importance, the incumbent party (which controls the military, diplomacy, and intelligence apparatus) gains an advantage over the opposition, which by definition does not control state instruments.

**Causal chain**: External escalation → media coverage shifts to security → voters rank security as top issue → security-advantaged party gains.

### 2. Incumbent-as-Defender Framing
The incumbent can credibly claim: "We are the ones protecting you from this threat. The opposition would be weaker / more conciliatory / divided." Even if the threat originated after the incumbent took office, framing can blame the adversary's inherent hostility rather than the government's policy.

### 3. Opposition Delegitimization
The opposition's critiques of the incumbent can be reframed as "aiding the enemy" or "undermining national unity." Policy disagreements on foreign relations become electorally toxic because the opposition cannot propose conciliatory policies without being accused of appeasement.

### 4. Nationalist Mobilization Base
Parties with a nationalist/patriotic base see higher turnout when their core identity issues (sovereignty, territorial integrity) are under threat. This is particularly strong in countries where national identity is contested or where the adversary is a well-defined historical rival.

## Conditions That Strengthen the Effect

| Condition | Mechanism | Examples |
|-----------|-----------|----------|
| Threat is VISIBLE (military exercises, aggressive speeches, overt sanctions) | Media covers it; voters see it daily | PLA exercises around Taiwan |
| Threat is RECENT (within 3 months of election) | Salience has not decayed | 2019 India-Pakistan clashes |
| Incumbent is CLEARLY more hawkish than opposition | Voters have a clear choice on the issue | DPP vs KMT on China |
| Opposition is PERCEIVED as sympathetic to the adversary | Makes opposition defensives | KMT's China ties |
| Threat appears EXTERNAL (not caused by incumbent's own actions) | Incumbent can frame as defender | Russia invading Georgia |
| Electoral system produces CLEAR INCUMBENT PARTY | Parliamentary or presidential winner | Taiwan's presidency |

## Conditions That Weaken or Reverse the Effect

| Condition | Mechanism | Examples |
|-----------|-----------|----------|
| Threat is CHRONIC (ongoing for years with no end) | Issue fatigue; voters stop responding | Israeli-Palestinian conflict post-2023 |
| Incumbent is seen as having CAUSED the threat through provocation | Blowback effect | US 2020 (Trump-China trade war) |
| Incumbent's response is seen as inadequate | Voters punish weakness | Afghanistan withdrawal under Biden |
| Opposition is equally or more hawkish | Voters can switch without changing stance | Both US parties on terrorism |
| Threat leads to ECONOMIC pain that outweighs security benefit | Economic voting dominates | Pakistan 2019 (eventual reversal) |
| The threat was an unprovoked attack on a neutral country | Rally effect helps ALL incumbents equally | Post-9/11 (Bush, but Congress also approved) |

## Forecasting Application

When a forecast question involves an election and there is a known adversary that has recently escalated pressure:

1. **Identify the adversary and the escalatory action**: Has a foreign power taken a visible hostile action (military exercise, sanctions, trade war, cyberattack, hostile diplomacy) in the past 6 months?

2. **Assess the partisan alignment**: Is the incumbent party clearly more hawkish on this adversary than the opposition? Does the opposition have policy positions that could be framed as "weak" or "conciliatory"?

3. **Check timing**: Is the escalatory action within 3 months of the election? Nearer = stronger effect. (Caveat: if the action is yesterday and the election is in 3 years, the effect will decay before election day.)

4. **Check for blowback**: Did the incumbent take actions that plausibly provoked the adversary? If yes, the effect may be neutral or reversed.

5. **Check for economic pain**: Does the external threat cause economic harm (sanctions, trade disruption, energy prices) that voters will blame on the incumbent? If the economic channel dominates, the threat may hurt rather than help.

6. **Calibrate magnitude**: 
   - Strong effect (3-8 percentage point boost): Visible escalation within 3 months, clear hawk/dove distinction, no blowback, no major economic pain → factor into polling adjustment
   - Moderate effect (1-3 pp boost): Less visible or more distant escalation → treat as modest advantage
   - Neutral/reversed effect: Chronic threat, blowback, economic pain, or opposition equally hawkish → do NOT apply the boost

7. **Distinguish from rally-around-the-flag**: If the threat is an unprovoked attack on the country itself (not a targeted adversary action against a particular party), the rally effect may temporarily boost ALL incumbents but the partisan advantage is weaker. Use this concept specifically for the **partisan advantage** channel.
