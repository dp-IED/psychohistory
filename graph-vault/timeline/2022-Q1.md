# 2022-Q1: Invasion, Energy Shock, and the End of the Post-Cold War Order

## Conjuncture

The first quarter of 2022 is the quarter in which multiple pre-existing tensions — frozen conflicts, energy dependencies, inflation dynamics, pandemic transitions — simultaneously activated into open crises. The dominant event is Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine on February 24, the largest conventional military campaign in Europe since 1945. But the invasion did not emerge from a vacuum: it landed atop a pre-war architecture of failed diplomacy, a European energy market already pricing in supply anxiety, a Federal Reserve preparing to exit emergency accommodation, and a world emerging from the acute phase of the COVID-19 pandemic on deeply uneven footing. The quarter's conjuncture is defined by how these distinct domains — war, energy, money, and the pandemic — fed each other in real time.

The quarter divides into three phases. **January** was dominated by the pre-war diplomatic failure: three rounds of US-Russia and NATO-Russia talks produced no movement on Russia's demand for NATO non-expansion guarantees, while the Kremlin continued massing forces along Ukraine's borders and in Belarus for ostensibly "exercises." Simultaneously, Kazakhstan experienced a sudden political crisis (fuel price protests escalating into a national security emergency, resolved by a rare CSTO military intervention), the Omicron wave crested across the US and Europe, and the global economic backdrop was defined by inflation accelerating beyond the "transitory" framing the Fed had recently abandoned. **February** opens with the Beijing Winter Olympics — a stage for the Putin-Xi declaration of "no limits" partnership that prefigures the coming realignment — and culminates in Russia's recognition of the DNR/LNR separatist entities on the 21st, followed by the invasion on the 24th. The invasion itself was a multi-axis shock: northern forces advancing toward Kyiv from Belarus, eastern forces pressing toward Kharkiv, and southern forces pushing out of Crimea toward Kherson and Mariupol. The speed of international response — SWIFT exclusions, Russian Central Bank asset freezes, the German decision to halt Nord Stream 2 — was historically unprecedented but insufficient to reverse the invasion. **March** became a month of adaptation to war. The initial Russian offensive failed to take Kyiv (the Battle of Hostomel Airport and the extended logistical tail through the Chernobyl Exclusion Zone became symptoms of an overextended operation), but the southern axis captured Kherson and laid siege to Mariupol. The Mariupol Drama Theatre and maternity hospital airstrikes crystallized the war's human cost. Markets experienced their own version of the shock: oil surged past $100 on invasion day and briefly touched $130, European gas prices hit all-time intraday highs above EUR 300/MWh, nickel prices triggered an exchange halt, and wheat futures hit records as the Black Sea grain corridor closed. The Federal Reserve delivered its first rate hike (25bp on March 16), beginning the tightening cycle under conditions of war-driven commodity inflation — a preview of the stagflationary tension that would define the year.

Beneath the war lay a deeper structural break: the post-Cold War European security order — built on the premise that borders would not be redrawn by force, that energy interdependence made conflict less likely, and that NATO enlargement would proceed without triggering a Russian military response — was simultaneously revealed as both fragile and already dismantled. Germany's Zeitenwende announcement (February 27), the REPowerEU plan (March 8), and Putin's rubles-for-gas decree (March 31) were the opening moves of conflicts that would dominate the next several quarters: a European energy crisis, a rearmament cycle, and the weaponization of trade as a military instrument. Meanwhile, the COVID-19 pandemic entered a new phase: Omicron was receding across vaccinated populations in the West, restrictions were lifted across Europe and much of the US, but the Hong Kong surge and Shanghai lockdown (announced March 27) demonstrated that the pandemic was far from global resolution, and the war itself disrupted testing and vaccination campaigns in vulnerable regions. The quarter thus stacks two global dislocations — war and pandemic — atop an already-unfolding monetary tightening cycle, producing a conjuncture of simultaneous, mutually reinforcing crises.

## Threads (this quarter)

### [[russia-ukraine-war]]
This thread is **born** this quarter. The pre-war buildup (January) and failed diplomacy set the stage; the February 24 invasion initiates the conflict. Initial Russian assumptions of rapid victory are contradicted within days as Kyiv holds, Hostomel is fiercely contested, and the northern axis bogs down. Kherson falls (March 2) but the siege of Mariupol becomes the defining atrocity theater. The Istanbul peace talks (March 29) produce the first serious negotiation framework (Ukrainian neutrality proposal, Russian promise to reduce activity around Kyiv) — a window that will close in April with the discovery of Bucha's mass graves. The quarter establishes the war's fundamental dynamics: Ukraine's willingness to fight, the West's willingness to supply weapons and impose sanctions, and Russia's willingness to escalate (nuclear alert, civilian infrastructure targeting) without winning quickly.

### [[european-energy-crisis-2022]]
Picks up from pre-Q1 (prices already elevated, storage below 5-year average). The invasion transforms a pre-existing price crisis into an active geopolitical energy war. Germany halts Nord Stream 2 certification on February 21 — a pipeline the government had defended as commercial — marking a revolution in German energy policy. REPowerEU (March 8) sets a target of cutting Russian gas dependency by two-thirds within the year. Putin's March 28 decree requiring ruble payments from April 1 inaugurates the gas-as-weapon phase. Prices: TTF hits an all-time intraday high of EUR 345/MWh on March 8; Brent crude peaks near $130 the same day. Crucially, physical Russian gas flows remain largely uninterrupted this quarter — the weaponization is still in its signaling phase, not yet the throttling that will arrive in Q2-Q3.

### [[us-monetary-policy-cycle-2022-2026]]
The Fed holds rates at 0-0.25% at its January 26 meeting but telegraphs a March hike. The March 16 hike (25bp to 0.25-0.50%) is the first since 2018, taken against the backdrop of war-driven commodity inflation (oil, wheat, metals) compounding supply-chain-driven price pressures already in the system. The January FOMC statement describes the labor market as "very strong" and inflation "well above 2%." February CPI (released March 10) hits 7.9% YoY. PCE inflation (released March 31) reaches 6.4% YoY — the highest since 1982. The war complicates the Fed's communications: it faces a stagflationary shock (higher energy prices = higher inflation + lower growth) that gives the Committee no clean direction. The dot plot at March signals 7 rate hikes in 2022 — an aggressive path that markets absorb with volatility.

### [[finland-sweden-nato-accession]]
Not yet a formal thread — both countries are still neutral at this point. But the seeds are planted in March. Finnish and Swedish public opinion shifts dramatically in favor of NATO membership within weeks of the invasion. Finland's government publishes a security policy review on March 13 that leaves the door open for application. The formal applications come in May, but the strategic decision is made in Q1 as a direct response to Russia's invasion demonstrating that neutrality offers no security guarantee.

### [[us-china-tech-decoupling]]
Continues its gradual escalation. The quarter's key developments are in semiconductor policy: Intel announces a $20 billion mega-site in Ohio (January 21), the EU proposes its Chips Act with €43 billion in funding (February 8), and the NVIDIA-Arm $40 billion deal collapses on regulatory hurdles (February 8). None of these directly relate to the war, but the war accelerates the narrative of strategic self-sufficiency in advanced manufacturing. Separately, the Beijing Olympics (February 4-20) showcase Chinese technological ambition while Putin's attendance and the "no limits" partnership declaration deepen the US perception of a Russia-China axis.

### [[us-crypto-regulation]]
The quarter's signature event is President Biden's Executive Order on Ensuring Responsible Development of Digital Assets (March 9) — the first comprehensive US government framework for cryptocurrency regulation. The EO directs the Treasury, State, Justice, and other agencies to study and produce reports on digital asset risks and opportunities, covering consumer protection, financial stability, illicit finance, and US competitiveness. This is a foundational document for the regulatory debate that will unfold over the next several years. Bitcoin opens the quarter at ~$46k, dips to ~$34k in the January risk-off move, and recovers to ~$45k by quarter-end — relatively stable by crypto standards.

### COVID-19 Pandemic Transition
The pandemic enters a new phase in Q1 2022. The Omicron wave peaks in the US around January 15 (7-day average ~802,000 cases) and recedes through February. The WHO maintains the PHEIC but shifts language, with Tedros stating on March 30 that the acute phase "could be ended in 2022." Country-level transitions diverge sharply: Denmark removes all restrictions (February 1), the UK's "Living with COVID" plan ends legal requirements (February 24), and the CDC shifts to its new Community Levels metric, telling 70% of Americans they can unmask indoors (February 25). But this is not a uniform global reopening. Hong Kong experiences its worst wave of the pandemic — the Omicron BA.2 subvariant overwhelms the zero-COVID system, with cases peaking at ~58,000/day in early March and crematoria overwhelmed. China escalates its Dynamic Zero-COVID strategy in response: Shanghai's two-stage lockdown is announced March 27, locking down Pudong first (March 28-31) and Puxi from April 1 — the largest lockdown of the pandemic by population affected. The war in Ukraine also disrupts pandemic response: WHO warns of testing and vaccination disruption creating variant emergence risk.

## Chronicle (evidence)

**2022-01-04:** Kazakhstan President Tokayev accepts cabinet resignation after fuel price protests escalate into nationwide unrest. Protests continue through January 7.

**2022-01-05:** CSTO (Collective Security Treaty Organization) deploys "peacekeeping" forces to Kazakhstan at Tokayev's request — the alliance's first-ever military intervention. At least 225 killed in the unrest.

**2022-01-05:** US 7-day average COVID-19 cases peak at approximately 802,000 — Omicron wave's US zenith.

**2022-01-10 to 2022-01-14:** US-Russia security talks in Geneva, NATO-Russia Council, and OSCE meetings all fail to resolve Russia's demand for NATO non-expansion guarantees. Russia's formal proposals (submitted December 2021) are rejected.

**2022-01-15:** Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha'apai volcano erupts (VEI 5-6), generating a Pacific-wide tsunami that reaches as far as Peru and Japan. The eruption severs the primary undersea communications cable connecting Tonga to the global internet, isolating the island nation for weeks.

**2022-01-17:** Russia begins joint military exercises in Belarus, deploying forces near Ukraine's northern border. The exercises are initially scheduled to end February 20.

**2022-01-18:** Microsoft announces $68.7 billion all-cash acquisition of Activision Blizzard — the largest acquisition in video game history.

**2022-01-19:** Biden authorizes $200 million in emergency security assistance to Ukraine.

**2022-01-19:** WHO's 9th IHR Emergency Committee reaffirms COVID-19 as a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC). Tedros warns "it is not the time to give up."

**2022-01-21:** Intel announces plans for a $20 billion semiconductor mega-site in Licking County, Ohio — the largest private-sector investment in Ohio history, part of Intel's push to restore US chip manufacturing.

**2022-01-24:** NATO places forces on standby; US State Department orders evacuation of embassy family members from Kyiv.

**2022-01-24:** Meta announces AI Research SuperCluster (RSC) — among the fastest AI supercomputers at the time.

**2022-01-26:** FOMC holds federal funds rate at 0.00-0.25% but signals a rate hike is "warranted soon." Statement describes labor market as "very strong" and inflation "well above 2%." Balance sheet reduction telegraphed for later in 2022.

**2022-01-27:** Apple reports record quarterly revenue of $123.9 billion, driven by iPhone 13 sales.

**2022-01-28:** Biden publicly warns that a Russian invasion of Ukraine is likely; US and UK intelligence begin declassifying information about Russian plans to create pretexts for military action.

**2022-01-28:** Canada Freedom Convoy truckers arrive in Ottawa, beginning a three-week occupation of downtown.

**2022-02-01:** Denmark removes all domestic COVID-19 restrictions — the first major EU country to do so.

**2022-02-02:** Meta (Facebook) reports its first-ever daily active user (DAU) decline — a drop of approximately 1 million users — triggering a 26% single-day stock decline.

**2022-02-04:** Beijing Winter Olympics open. Russian President Putin attends, meets with President Xi Jinping. The joint statement declares a "no limits" partnership between Russia and China and opposes NATO expansion.

**2022-02-04 to 2022-02-20:** Beijing Winter Olympics — the most geopolitically charged Olympics since the Cold War, held under strict zero-COVID bubble.

**2022-02-07:** Canada invokes the Emergencies Act for the first time since its passage in 1988, authorizing extraordinary measures to end the Freedom Convoy blockades. The Ambassador Bridge at Windsor (busiest US-Canada land border crossing) had been blocked since February 7.

**2022-02-08:** European Commission proposes the European Chips Act — €43 billion in public investment to double the EU's share of global semiconductor production to 20% by 2030.

**2022-02-08:** NVIDIA announces termination of its $40 billion acquisition of Arm Holdings, citing "significant regulatory challenges" — one of the largest failed tech mergers in history.

**2022-02-11:** Biden warns allies during a video call that Russia has amassed sufficient forces to invade Ukraine "any day." The US State Department orders most remaining embassy staff to evacuate Kyiv.

**2022-02-15:** Russia announces partial withdrawal of forces from the Ukrainian border; US and UK officials state no withdrawal has occurred and deployments have increased. Ukraine conducts a national "resilience day" with military exercises.

**2022-02-21:** Putin formally recognizes the Donetsk People's Republic (DNR) and Luhansk People's Republic (LNR) as independent states and orders Russian "peacekeeping forces" into eastern Ukraine. Initial US, EU, and UK sanctions follow — targeting Russian sovereign debt and individuals.

**2022-02-22:** Germany halts certification of the Nord Stream 2 pipeline — a project the Scholz government had consistently defended as a private commercial venture, marking a historic reversal of German Ostpolitik.

**2022-02-22:** The Federation Council authorizes Putin to use military force abroad. Ukraine declares a national state of emergency.

**2022-02-23:** Widespread cyberattacks — including distributed denial-of-service attacks — hit Ukrainian government and banking websites. An emergency session of the UN Security Council fails to produce a resolution.

**2022-02-24:** Russia launches a full-scale invasion of Ukraine along three axes: northern (toward Kyiv via Belarus and the Chernobyl Exclusion Zone), eastern (Donbas toward Kharkiv), and southern (from Crimea toward Kherson and Mariupol). The Battle of Hostomel Airport begins as Russian VDV paratroopers attempt to establish an airbridge. Snake Island defenders reply "Russian warship, go f*** yourself" to Russian surrender demand. President Zelenskyy imposes martial law and refuses a US evacuation offer, telling officials "I need ammunition, not a ride." International crude oil (Brent) crosses $100/barrel for the first time since 2014.

**2022-02-25 to 2022-02-27:** Russian forces reach the northern outskirts of Kyiv, with street fighting reported in Obolon. Putin orders Russian nuclear forces to "special combat readiness" — the highest alert status — on February 27. The EU, US, UK, and allies agree to remove select Russian banks from the SWIFT messaging system and freeze Russian Central Bank assets held in their jurisdictions — the most severe financial sanctions ever imposed on a G20 economy.

**2022-02-25:** Biden nominates Judge Ketanji Brown Jackson to the US Supreme Court — the first Black woman nominated to the Court.

**2022-02-25:** CDC announces a new "COVID-19 Community Levels" metric; 70% of Americans are classified as able to unmask indoors.

**2022-02-27:** German Chancellor Scholz announces a Zeitenwende ("turning point") in German defense policy: a €100 billion special defense fund and a commitment to meet NATO's 2% of GDP spending target — the most significant German strategic reorientation since 1945.

**2022-02-28:** First round of Russia-Ukraine peace talks held in Gomel, Belarus. No breakthrough, but agreement to continue dialogue. Russian forces enter Kherson and lay siege to Berdyansk and Mariupol.

**2022-03-01:** Biden delivers the State of the Union address, focusing on the Ukraine response, infrastructure implementation, and the pandemic transition. He announces the US will close its airspace to Russian aircraft.

**2022-03-02:** Kherson falls to Russian forces — the first major Ukrainian city to be captured. UN General Assembly votes 141-5 to demand Russian withdrawal (Resolution ES-11/1).

**2022-03-02 to 2022-03-03:** Hong Kong reports its highest daily COVID-19 case counts of the pandemic — approximately 55,000-58,000 confirmed cases per day, reflecting the collapse of the zero-COVID containment system against the Omicron BA.2 subvariant. Low elderly vaccination rates contribute to a death toll exceeding 3,000 for the wave.

**2022-03-03:** Zaporizhzhia Nuclear Power Plant — the largest nuclear plant in Europe — is shelled and captured by Russian forces during a firefight. A fire in a training building is contained; the IAEA is notified but remains unable to secure the facility.

**2022-03-04:** Nord Stream 2 AG, the Swiss-registered parent company of the Nord Stream 2 pipeline, files for bankruptcy and lays off all 140 employees.

**2022-03-07:** Third round of Russia-Ukraine talks in Belovezhskaya Pushcha, Belarus produces limited agreement on humanitarian corridors, largely non-functional.

**2022-03-08:** US President Biden issues an executive order banning imports of Russian oil, liquefied natural gas, and coal. The European Commission announces the REPowerEU plan to reduce EU dependence on Russian gas by two-thirds within the year. Brent crude oil peaks at approximately $128 (closing) with intraday highs near $139. European gas benchmark TTF hits an all-time intraday high of EUR 345/MWh. Nickel prices on the London Metal Exchange surge above $100,000/ton intraday, triggering a trading halt and a subsequent cancellation of billions in trades. Wheat futures (CBOT) hit a record $13.64 per bushel. The FAO Food Price Index reaches its highest level in the index's history at 159.3 points.

**2022-03-09:** Russian airstrike destroys the Mariupol Maternity Hospital No. 3, killing at least 3 and wounding 17. Images of injured pregnant women being carried from the rubble become defining images of the war. The event is widely condemned as a war crime.

**2022-03-09:** President Biden signs Executive Order 14067 on "Ensuring Responsible Development of Digital Assets" — ordering a whole-of-government review of cryptocurrency risks and benefits, covering consumer protection, financial stability, illicit finance, US competitiveness, and a potential US central bank digital currency (CBDC).

**2022-03-10:** US February CPI released at 7.9% YoY — the highest since January 1982, driven by energy, food, and shelter costs.

**2022-03-12:** California, Oregon, and Washington jointly announce the end of indoor mask mandates.

**2022-03-13:** Russian cruise missile strike on the Yavoriv International Peacekeeping and Security Center near the Polish border kills 35 people — the closest strike to NATO territory since the invasion began.

**2022-03-16:** FOMC raises the federal funds rate by 25 basis points to 0.25-0.50% — the first rate increase since December 2018. The Summary of Economic Projects (SEP) signals seven total rate hikes in 2022, with a terminal rate projected at 1.9%.

**2022-03-16:** Russian airstrike destroys the Mariupol Drama Theatre, where hundreds of civilians were sheltering. The Russian government claims the theater was a Ukrainian military base — a claim rejected by survivors and investigators.

**2022-03-16:** The International Court of Justice (ICJ) orders Russia to suspend military operations in Ukraine, ruling in Ukraine's favor on a preliminary basis.

**2022-03-18:** Federal Chancellor Scholz and President Macron jointly call on Putin to agree to an immediate ceasefire. Putin demands recognition of Crimea and the newly-recognized DNR/LNR.

**2022-03-21 to 2022-03-24:** Senate Judiciary Committee holds confirmation hearings for Supreme Court nominee Ketanji Brown Jackson. Hearings are contentious but Jackson is widely expected to be confirmed.

**2022-03-21:** China Eastern Airlines Flight MU5735, a Boeing 737-800, crashes in Guangxi province, killing all 132 aboard. Aviation authorities ground all similar aircraft for inspection.

**2022-03-22:** Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang announces the Hopper H100 GPU at GTC 2022, featuring a dedicated Transformer engine and a new architecture optimized for large language model training and inference.

**2022-03-24:** European Parliament and Council reach a provisional political agreement on the Digital Markets Act (DMA) — landmark legislation regulating "gatekeeper" platform companies (Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft) with obligations on interoperability, data access, and anticompetitive practices.

**2022-03-24:** NATO holds an Extraordinary Summit in Brussels, announcing four new battalion-strength battle groups in Bulgaria, Hungary, Romania, and Slovakia — reinforcing the Eastern flank to 40,000 troops under direct NATO command. NATO estimates 7,000-15,000 Russian soldiers killed.

**2022-03-25:** US federal mask mandate for public transit is extended to April 18; Hawaii drops its indoor mask mandate, leaving no US state with a universal indoor mask requirement.

**2022-03-27:** Shanghai announces a two-stage "grid-based" lockdown: Pudong (financial district) locked down from March 28 to April 1; Puxi (historic city center) from April 1 to April 5. The announcement triggers panic buying and represents an escalation of China's Dynamic Zero-COVID strategy.

**2022-03-28:** Putin signs a decree requiring "unfriendly" countries — including all EU member states — to pay for Russian natural gas in rubles starting April 1. The mechanism is designed to shore up the ruble (which had lost ~40% after sanctions) and forces European buyers to decide whether payments in rubles violate sanctions regimes.

**2022-03-29:** Russia-Ukraine peace talks in Istanbul produce the most significant framework yet: Ukraine proposes a neutral/non-aligned status with international security guarantees (modeled on Article 5 but without NATO membership), excluding Crimea and the occupied Donbas from the guarantee temporarily. Russia promises to "drastically reduce military activity" around Kyiv and Chernihiv.

**2022-03-30:** WHO Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus releases the updated Strategic Preparedness, Readiness and Response Plan, stating that the acute phase of the pandemic "could be ended in 2022" if equity gaps and surveillance are addressed.

**2022-03-31:** Putin's rubles-for-gas payment mechanism officially takes effect. European buyers continue to assess sanctions compliance risks. EU legal guidance issued shortly after the quarter advises against participation in the ruble payment scheme.

**2022-03-31:** Biden administration authorizes the largest release from the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve in history — 1 million barrels per day for six months (180 million barrels total) — to combat surging gasoline prices.

**2022-03-31:** Italy ends its COVID-19 state of emergency after more than two years. February US PCE inflation is reported at 6.4% YoY — the highest since January 1982.

**2022-03-31:** On the same day, as Russian forces withdraw from the Kyiv region in accordance with the Istanbul framework, evidence begins emerging of mass graves and summary executions in Bucha, a Kyiv suburb — atrocities that will dominate the global response in the following week.

## Cross-domain interactions

**War + Energy + Monetary Policy: The inflationary bottleneck.** The Russian invasion simultaneously tightened global oil, gas, and food supply at a moment when the Fed was already behind the curve on inflation. Oil crossed $100 on invasion day and peaked above $130 in March. European gas (TTF) hit EUR 345/MWh. Wheat hit $13.64/bushel. These commodity shocks transmitted directly into consumer price indices — February CPI hit 7.9%, PCE hit 6.4% — forcing the Fed to hike into what was already becoming a stagflationary environment. The war complicated the Fed's forward guidance by creating simultaneous upward pressure on prices and downward pressure on growth, reducing the clarity of the output-inflation tradeoff.

**War + Energy + European Policy: The destruction of Ostpolitik.** The invasion shattered the post-Cold War German doctrine that economic interdependence with Russia prevented conflict. The same administration (Scholz's SPD-Green-FDP coalition) that had defended Nord Stream 2 as a commercial project halted its certification within 48 hours of the DNR/LNR recognition. By March 8, the EU had announced REPowerEU — aiming to cut Russian gas dependency by two-thirds within a year. Putin's March 28 rubles decree inverted the dependency relationship: Europe needed Russian gas, but Russia needed to force a payment mechanism that would circumvent sanctions. The interaction between military aggression and energy leverage created a new domain of hybrid warfare — pipeline politics as a theater of conflict — that would dominate European headlines through the rest of 2022.

**War + Finance: The weaponization of the dollar system.** The decision to block select Russian banks from SWIFT and freeze approximately $300 billion in Russian Central Bank reserves represented an unprecedented financial intervention against a G20 economy. This interaction between military escalation and financial sanctions created a new risk perception in global markets — that the dollar-based financial system could be weaponized against sovereign states. The long-term consequence (accelerated de-dollarization efforts by China, Russia, and others) would only become visible in later quarters, but the precedent was set in February 2022. The March 9 Biden Executive Order on Digital Assets was partly a response to questions about whether decentralized crypto systems could bypass sanctions.

**COVID + War + Energy: A compounding crisis of state capacity.** The quarter is defined by two global dislocations running simultaneously: the pandemic transition (Omicron receding in the West but China locking down Shanghai, Hong Kong overwhelmed) and the war. These crises competed for fiscal capacity, political attention, and policy bandwidth. The $15.6 billion COVID supplemental stalled in Congress as war spending priorities shifted. European governments that had just lifted pandemic restrictions suddenly faced the need for hundreds of billions in energy price relief. China's decision to escalate zero-COVID — locking down Shanghai in March — occurred against the backdrop of the Ukraine war dominating global headlines, reducing external pressure on Beijing's domestic COVID strategy even as its economic costs mounted. The interaction between these two global emergencies meant neither received the full policy focus it would have demanded alone.
