# 2022-Q2: The War of Attrition and Monetary Regime Change

## Conjuncture

Q2 2022 is the quarter where the world absorbed the shock of Russia's invasion and discovered it would be long. The war in Ukraine shifted decisively from a failed blitzkrieg on Kyiv to a grinding artillery war in the Donbas, while the economic consequences that were latent in Q1 — energy coercion, food price spikes, inflation acceleration — now materialized as binding constraints on policy everywhere. The quarter's dominant contradiction: the same force (Russian aggression) that united the West in military support for Ukraine simultaneously tore at its economic cohesion through energy prices and inflation, creating political stress lines that deepened through the year.

The Federal Reserve crossed a Rubicon in Q2. After one year of treating inflation as "transitory," the May CPI print at 8.6% forced a policy emergency: the June 75bp hike (the largest since 1994) repudiated the Fed's own forward guidance from five weeks earlier, damaging communications credibility. This was not a gradual normalization but a regime change — from accommodation to aggressive tightening — and it rippled into every asset class. The S&P 500 entered bear market territory by mid-June. Crypto, which had boomed on liquidity abundance, suffered its first systemic stress test: Terra/LUNA's algorithmic stablecoin collapse ($40B+ destroyed in May) triggered cascading failures through Celsius and Three Arrows Capital, with the full extent of crypto credit contagion yet to be determined.

The European energy crisis escalated from slow-burn to acute. Russia's ruble-payment decree (April 1) and the cutoff of gas to Poland and Bulgaria (April 27) made explicit what had been implicit: energy was a weapon. This created a compound crisis for European governments — they had to simultaneously fund Ukraine's defense, fill gas storage before winter, and cushion households from price shocks that were driving inflation to double digits in several member states. The political logic pushed France's legislative election to a hung parliament (June 19), Italy's government toward crisis, and Britain toward a prime ministerial confidence vote (June 6) that Johnson survived but that left him politically exposed, with 41% of his own MPs voting against him.

On the battlefields of military and constitutional law, the quarter delivered two seismic decisions. The leaked Dobbs draft (May 2) and the final opinion (June 24) overturned Roe v. Wade after 49 years, triggering abortion bans in 13 states and reshaping the political landscape for the November midterms. The NATO Madrid Summit (June 29) invited Finland and Sweden to join the alliance — the most significant expansion in NATO's history, triggered directly by Russia's aggression. Both decisions strengthened opposing coalitions. Q2 is thus the quarter where the war's structural consequences — monetary, energy, electoral, and alliance-based — became legible, even as the war itself settled into a positional slog.

## Threads (this quarter)

### [[threads/russia-ukraine-war]] — The Turn to Donbas Attrition

The war entered its second phase. After withdrawing from the Kyiv region (April 1-3) amid the discovery of mass graves in Bucha, Russia concentrated all offensive power in the Donbas — a scorched-earth artillery campaign that prioritized territorial gains over force preservation. The sinking of the Moskva (April 14) was the quarter's most dramatic naval engagement, but the operational logic was set by the ground war: Russia captured Mariupol after an 82-day siege (May 20) and pushed Ukrainian forces out of Severodonetsk (June 24), the last major city in Luhansk Oblast. Ukraine's strategy shifted from positional defense to accepting gradual territorial loss to attrit Russian forces while Western heavy weapons (howitzers, HIMARS) were being trained and integrated. The diplomatic track was dead: Istanbul talks had collapsed; neither side could negotiate from the positions established by June 30. The EU's granting of candidate status to Ukraine (June 23) was a long-term structural move with no near-term effect on the war.

### [[threads/us-monetary-policy-cycle-2022-2026]] — The 75bp Break

The most consequential monetary policy turn since 1994 unfolded across two FOMC meetings. The May 4 meeting delivered a 50bp hike (to 0.75-1.00%) — telegraphed in advance, the largest single hike in 22 years. Chair Powell explicitly stated during the post-meeting press conference that 75bp was "not actively considered." Five weeks later, the May CPI print (June 10) showed inflation accelerating to 8.6% YoY, destroying the "peak inflation" narrative. At the June 15 meeting, the Fed hiked 75bp (to 1.50-1.75%), directly contradicting its own forward guidance. The gap between guidance and outcome was a self-inflicted wound to Fed credibility. The policy rate was still below neutral — meaning more hikes to come — but the pace had shifted from normalization to emergency mode. Mortgage rates crossed 5.5% for the first time since 2008, housing activity began freezing, and recession probability estimates jumped across forecasting models.

### [[threads/european-energy-crisis-2022]] — Weaponization and the Ruble Gambit

Russia's ruble-payment decree (effective April 1) forced European gas buyers to choose: pay Gazprom in rubles (circumventing sanctions and shoring up the ruble) or lose supply. The EU advised against compliance; Poland and Bulgaria refused and were cut off (April 27). This was the first operational test of the weaponization framework — Russia demonstrated it would use supply cuts against countries that resisted its payment terms. Germany, Italy, and other large buyers initially complied by establishing ruble-paying accounts through Gazprombank, creating a sanctions loophole that exposed intra-EU coordination failures. TTF gas prices, after peaking in March at ~€130/MWh, remained elevated through the quarter around €80-100/MWh — below crisis peaks but triple pre-crisis norms. Gazprom additionally reduced Nord Stream 1 flows in June, citing turbine maintenance delays — a new pretext in the escalating supply-throttling pattern. The quarter's energy dynamics fed directly into inflation: European headline CPI hit 8.1% in May, with energy as the primary driver.

### [[threads/dobbs-abortion-politics]] — The Leak, the Decision, and Trigger Laws

The Dobbs process defined Q2's domestic US political narrative. The Politico leak of the draft opinion (May 2) was an institutional earthquake — a first in the Supreme Court's modern history — and triggered protests, security lockdowns, and an internal investigation. The leak compressed what would have been a shock into seven weeks of anticipatory mobilization, fundamentally altering the political calendar: Democrats gained a turnout issue, Republicans lost the ability to campaign against Roe incrementally. When the final opinion was released (June 24), 13 states with trigger laws enacted near-total abortion bans within hours or days. The decision's release coincided with the Court's ruling in West Virginia v. EPA (June 30), which limited EPA's authority to regulate carbon emissions — making the term a trifecta of conservative judicial victories (Dobbs, Bruen on guns, WV v. EPA) that shifted constitutional law decisively rightward.

### [[threads/finland-sweden-nato-accession]] — The Application

The most significant NATO expansion in decades was set in motion during Q2. Finland (May 15) and Sweden (May 18) announced and submitted NATO membership applications, abandoning post-WWII and post-Napoleonic neutralities respectively within three months of Russia's invasion. This was the most dramatic illustration of the strategic self-defeat of Russia's aggression: the invasion's stated objective was to prevent NATO expansion, and it achieved the opposite. Turkey's surprise objections (May 18), demanding concessions on Kurdish groups, introduced a holdout dynamic that would define the accession process for the next year. The trilateral memorandum at the Madrid Summit (June 29) provisionally resolved the Turkish blockade, but ratification by all 30 NATO members remained pending.

### [[threads/us-crypto-regulation]] — The Terra Crash and the Grayscale Lawsuit

Q2 was the crypto industry's first systemic stress test. The algorithmic stablecoin TerraUSD (UST) depegged from $1 on May 7-8, triggering a death spiral that destroyed its sister token LUNA (from ~$80 to near-zero) and obliterated $40B+ in market value. The collapse cascaded: Celsius Network froze withdrawals (June 13), Three Arrows Capital defaulted on loans (June 27), and several crypto lenders suspended operations. Unlike earlier crypto corrections, this was a credit event — the first major test of DeFi's leverage architecture, and it failed. The SEC rejected Grayscale's spot Bitcoin ETF application (June) on market manipulation grounds, and Grayscale immediately filed suit in DC Circuit Court challenging the denial. The enforcement-denial followed by legal challenge established a pattern that could force eventual reconsideration.

### Additional Threads Active This Quarter

**[[threads/us-china-tech-decoupling]]**: Biden's trip to Tokyo (May 22-23) launched the Indo-Pacific Economic Framework (IPEF) with 13 founding members — a trade-and-technology mechanism designed as an alternative to China-led regional frameworks. Discussions on semiconductor export controls intensified within the administration, with new restrictions under active policy development. Shanghai's extended COVID lockdown (April-May) exacerbated semiconductor supply chain fears.

**[[threads/taiwan-strait-crisis-2022]]**: The thread was nascent — no crisis had occurred yet. But the May 23 IPEF launch, Biden's repeated statements about military intervention to defend Taiwan (May 23, walked back by the White House), and rising PLA activity signaled a deteriorating deterrence environment with elevated escalation risk.

## Cross-domain interactions

**1. War-driven inflation forcing monetary regime change**: The Ukraine war's impact on global energy and food prices was the primary factor pushing US CPI above 8.6% in May. This directly forced the Fed to abandon its pre-communicated 50bp plan and hike 75bp in June — an emergency pace that would not have been justified by the US domestic demand picture alone. The interaction between war (a geopolitical event) and monetary policy (a macroeconomic regime) created a compound tightening cycle in which inflationary pressure was largely exogenous to the US economy, giving the Fed no lever to address root causes while it raised rates with blunt force.

**2. Energy weaponization feeding European political instability**: Russia's gas-supply cuts to Poland, Bulgaria, and Finland (April-May), combined with the ruble-payment decree, transmitted war pressure directly into European domestic politics. Energy-driven inflation hit 9% in the UK, 8.1% in the Euro area — eroding real wages, fueling strikes (UK rail strikes in June), and shifting electoral outcomes. France's hung parliament (June 19) and Italy's impending government crisis were both exacerbated by the cost-of-living squeeze created by energy prices. The war was being fought on an economic front that Western allies had no institutional framework to manage, and the political costs were distributed unevenly across states that had varying dependence on Russian gas.

**3. Dobbs as a midterm-political shockwave in an inflationary environment**: The Dobbs leak and final decision created a new salience issue (abortion rights) just as the economy (inflation) was the dominant voter concern. The interaction between these two domains — economic pain pushing voters toward the opposition party, cultural backlash mobilizing the Democratic base — created a volatile midterm electoral environment with unpredictable turnout effects. The Uvalde shooting (May 24) added gun violence as a third vector, producing a compressed legislative response (Bipartisan Safer Communities Act, signed June 25) that was made possible by the cross-party political incentives created by the coming midterm election calendar.

**4. Crypto credit stress as a leading indicator of tightening liquidity**: The Terra/LUNA collapse and Celsius/Three Arrows cascade in Q2 demonstrated a pattern: the most leveraged parts of the financial system break first when liquidity contracts. The Fed's pivot from zero rates to 75bp hikes was the structural cause, and crypto — the most levered sector after a decade of cheap money — was the first domino. The regulatory response was bifurcated: the SEC rejected Grayscale's Bitcoin ETF (June), pushing the industry toward legal confrontation, while Treasury and Fed officials publicly framed crypto stress as contained to the sector. Whether the credit contagion would spread to traditional finance remained an open question at quarter-end.
